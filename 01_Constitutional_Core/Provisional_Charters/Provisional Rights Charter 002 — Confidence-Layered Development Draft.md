# Preamble — Status, Confidence and Purpose

This document revises the first Provisional Rights Charter by separating protections according to their current derivational confidence.

The distinction is necessary because not every useful or familiar civil protection has the same relationship to Blainey's Laws.

This Charter therefore distinguishes:

1. **Fundamental / Source-Anchored Rights and Protected Relations** — strongest current derivation;
2. **Strongly Derived Civil Rights and Protections** — recurring decompressions of the fundamental layer;
3. **Effective Exercise, Standing and Support** — conditions required to prevent protected interests becoming merely nominal;
4. **Interpretive and Limitation Rules** — rules governing interaction and restriction;
5. **Unresolved / Assumed Rights Questions** — plausible or important matters not yet sufficiently derived for elevation.

> **Unclear Right != No Protection.**

> **Useful Protection != Fundamental Right.**

> **Later Elevation Requires Stronger Derivation, Not Familiarity.**

The word **fundamental** describes derivational position in this provisional model. It does not mean that every exercise of a right is unlimited.

Where this draft conflicts with Blainey's Laws, Blainey's Laws control and this draft is presumptively wrong.

This draft is not a Constitution and does not bind wider Concord development merely because it concerns constitutional subject matter.

# Foundational Participant–Concord Relationship

The Concord exists **for its participants**.

Participants do not exist for the Concord.

Participants do not belong to the Concord merely because they:
- live within it;
- use its systems;
- depend upon its services;
- hold citizenship or membership;
- receive protection or support from it.

The Concord is a civil architecture intended to serve participants, protect their legitimate rights and protected standing, and enable peaceful flourishing as effectively as reasonably possible.

Its duty is therefore one of **best reasonable protection**, not a false guarantee that no right will ever be infringed.

No civil system can guarantee that every protected interest will remain fully exercisable in every circumstance. Rights may conflict. Information may be incomplete. Resources may be constrained. Error may occur. Extreme circumstances, including genuine emergencies, may sometimes require temporary infringement or restriction of the exercise of a protected right in order to prevent greater harm or protect the rights and safety of others.

Such infringement must not be treated as proof that the right disappeared.

Where restriction or infringement occurs, the Concord should seek, as far as reasonably possible, to ensure that it is:
- genuinely necessary;
- proportionate to the circumstances;
- no broader than reasonably required;
- no longer than reasonably required;
- documented and reviewable where practicable;
- open to later challenge and correction.

Where a protected right has been wrongly infringed, or where unavoidable infringement has produced continuing harm or loss, the Concord should make its **best reasonable effort**, where possible, to provide appropriate remedy, correction, restoration, support or compensation according to the circumstances.

Remedy cannot always restore the position that existed before the infringement.

The obligation is therefore not a promise of perfect restoration. It is an obligation to **try in good faith, within legitimate capability and constraints, to repair what can reasonably be repaired**.

> **The Concord serves its participants; its participants do not serve as property of the Concord.**

> **Protection is an obligation of best reasonable effort, not a guarantee of perfect outcomes.**

> **Necessary infringement does not extinguish the underlying right.**

> **Where infringement occurs, correction and remedy should be pursued as far as reasonably possible.**

# Part I — Source, Holder and Interpretation

# Article 1 — Source and Derivational Authority

## 1.1 Ethical source

The ethical principles of the Concord are **Blainey's Laws**.

This Charter is downstream of that Ethical Kernel.

Later ethical kernels, compressed root sets, compound ethics, U-series results and other outputs of the Unified Paths of Light research programme are comparative research results and do not constitute the ethical authority of this Charter.

## 1.2 No silent ethical expansion

A right or protection recognised here must be traceable to:
- one or more Blainey's Laws;
- a derived protected relation;
- and, where relevant, a documented Concord civil architecture that decompresses or operationalises that relation.

Specificity does not create a new ethical principle where the derivation remains visible.

## 1.3 Derivational layers

This Charter distinguishes:

**Blainey's Laws**

↓

**Fundamental Protected Relations**

↓

**Recurring Civil Rights / Liberties / Protections**

↓

**Effective-Access Guarantees**

↓

**Operational Mechanisms**

Operational mechanisms are not automatically fundamental rights.

Fundamental rights must not remain so abstract that they become practically unusable.

# Article 2 — Protected Participants and Standing

## 2.1 Source holder class

Blainey's Law 7 recognises that **intelligent participants possess inherent value and should not be treated merely as means to an end**.

The exact scientific, philosophical and legal boundary of **intelligent participant** remains unresolved.

This Charter therefore preserves the source term rather than silently replacing it with:
- human;
- citizen;
- biological person;
- legal person;
- autonomous adult;
- AI;
- conscious system;
- sentient entity.

## 2.2 Fundamental standing is not citizenship

Fundamental protected standing does not arise merely from:
- citizenship;
- Concord project membership;
- productivity;
- institutional usefulness;
- political status;
- substrate.

> **Personhood != Citizenship.**

> **Project Membership != Citizenship.**

Citizenship may carry additional polity-specific functions without becoming the source of fundamental dignity.

## 2.3 Capacity is not standing

Reduced, developing, fluctuating or impaired capacity may affect **how** a protection is exercised.

It does not by itself erase underlying protected standing.

Difficulty with:
- communication;
- understanding a particular decision;
- disability;
- unfamiliar systems;
- temporary impairment;
- dependence on support;
- unconventional choices;
- refusal of assistance

does not automatically establish loss of standing.

## 2.4 Uncertain status

Where credible uncertainty exists about whether an entity falls within a protected participant class, uncertainty should trigger proportionate caution, investigation and reversible treatment rather than automatic domination or automatic final recognition.

This is not a final personhood criterion.

## General Interpretation Rule

Every provision in this Charter is interpreted together with:
- Blainey's Laws;
- the equal protected standing of others;
- the conflict and restriction rules in Part IV;
- the provisional status of this document.

This rule is not a generic override power. Restriction still requires legitimate purpose, necessity, proportionality and appropriate contestability.

# Participation Status and Rights Eligibility

The **confidence band of a right** and the **participation status required to exercise that right** are separate dimensions.

A right may be strongly derived without necessarily attaching in identical form to every participant in every civil role.

## Fundamental protection floor

The highest-band fundamental protections are intended to form a protected floor for all qualifying protected participants.

They should not ordinarily be removed merely because a participant is:
- new;
- provisional;
- non-citizen;
- not yet fully integrated;
- outside a particular governance role;
- temporarily unable to exercise every civil function.

Participation status may affect the **mode of exercise** of a fundamental protection, but should not be used merely to erase its underlying protected standing.

## Participation-dependent rights

Some lower-band or polity-specific rights may legitimately depend upon defined participation conditions.

Possible examples include:
- voting;
- franchise;
- eligibility for particular representative functions;
- office holding;
- participation in specified governance mechanisms;
- access to role-specific institutional powers.

A provisional, new or otherwise limited-status participant may therefore possess the fundamental protection floor while not yet qualifying for every participation-dependent civil or political right.

This distinction must not be used to relabel fundamental rights as optional membership benefits.

## Participation architecture remains unresolved

Concord V1.3 has not yet established the final levels, classes or maturity states of civil participation.

Accordingly, this Charter does **not** presently define:
- participation tiers;
- entry thresholds;
- qualification periods;
- citizenship thresholds;
- voting eligibility;
- graduation criteria;
- suspension criteria;
- role-specific eligibility;
- how participation status changes over time.

Those questions require later development.

Until that architecture exists, participation-dependent rights should be marked as **conditional / eligibility unresolved**, rather than assigned invented thresholds.

## Anti-circularity safeguard

An institution must not be permitted to reason:

**this participant lacks a participation-dependent right because they have not reached the required status**

while also arbitrarily preventing them from reaching, challenging or understanding that status.

Future participation architecture should therefore make eligibility rules:
- explicit;
- relevant to the function;
- proportionate;
- non-arbitrary;
- reviewable;
- contestable;
- accessible enough to understand;
- capable of change where qualification conditions are later met.

> **Rights Confidence != Rights Eligibility.**

> **Fundamental Protection Floor != Full Participation Entitlement.**

> **Participation-Dependent Right != Fundamental Right Withheld by Membership Status.**

> **Eligibility Unresolved != Permission to Invent Eligibility.**

# Part II — Fundamental / Source-Anchored Rights and Protected Relations

The following provisions are the strongest current outputs of the Blainey's-Laws derivation and loss test.

They are not necessarily unlimited in exercise.

# Article 3 — Autonomy and Protection Against Unjustified Interference

An intelligent participant has a provisional protected claim to direct their own life within the ethical minimum and against unnecessary harmful or arbitrary controlling interference.

Autonomy does not imply immunity from all constraint.

Restriction requires a legitimate protected purpose and must satisfy the limitation principles of this Charter.

Consent is an important expression of autonomy, but:

> **Consent != Unlimited Authority.**

> **Compliance != Meaningful Consent.**

Meaningful consent may require:
- relevant understanding;
- sufficient freedom from coercion;
- appropriate capacity or support;
- clarity about the object of consent;
- temporal validity;
- viable withdrawal where the relationship is legitimately voluntary.

# Article 4 — Safe Civil Context

Participants have a provisional protected claim to civil contexts in which legitimate living, learning, creation and cooperation can occur without unnecessary fear, coercion or violence.

Safe civil context may include:
- physical spaces;
- digital spaces;
- informational spaces;
- communication environments;
- temporary functional safe spaces;
- protected contexts required during vulnerability.

This protection does not create universal ownership or universal exclusion authority.

The protected object is the **civil condition necessary for safe legitimate participation**.

# Article 5 — Honest Consequential Civil Dealing

Where truthful dealing is owed in a consequential civil relationship, an affected participant has a provisional claim not to be intentionally materially deceived.

This protection supports:
- informed autonomy;
- meaningful consent;
- fair process;
- reliable challenge;
- civil trust.

It does not require unlimited disclosure.

Legitimate privacy, confidentiality, safety and protected information may justify non-disclosure where deception is not thereby manufactured.

# Article 6 — Fair Comparable Treatment

Materially comparable civil situations should receive comparable treatment unless a relevant difference justifies different treatment.

Fairness does not require identical treatment where relevant circumstances differ.

A relevant difference should be articulable and open to challenge where it materially affects protected standing.

> **Comparable Treatment != Identical Treatment.**

# Article 7 — Dignity and Non-Instrumentalisation

Intelligent participants have inherent value and should not be treated merely as:
- instruments;
- assets;
- resources;
- disposable means;
- institutional inputs.

A participant's usefulness does not determine their protected worth.

Capability, intelligence, productivity or dependency may affect responsibilities and support needs without automatically increasing or decreasing fundamental standing.

# Article 8 — Peaceful Difference and Non-Conformity

Participants have a provisional protection against unnecessary coercive conformity where difference remains compatible with the ethical minimum and peaceful coexistence is reasonably possible.

Difference alone does not establish legitimate grounds for coercion.

Unusual preference, lifestyle, belief, identity, voluntary risk or non-conformity should not by themselves manufacture authority to intervene.

> **Difference != Harm.**

> **Non-Conformity != Incapacity.**

Peaceful coexistence is preferred to unnecessary enforced uniformity.

# Article 9 — Contestability and Correction

A participant materially affected by consequential civil belief, classification, decision or action should have a viable route for relevant challenge and appropriate reconsideration.

No intelligence or institution is constitutionally presumed infallible.

Meaningful contestability may require:
- understandable reasons;
- access to materially relevant evidence;
- ability to communicate disagreement;
- review;
- correction where error is established;
- practical accessibility.

A formally available challenge process that cannot reasonably be understood, reached, afforded, communicated with or safely used may fail to provide effective contestability.

> **Nominal Access != Effective Access.**

# Part III — Strongly Derived Civil Rights and Protections

The following are provisionally recognised as recurring civil decompressions of Part-II protections.

They are **not currently presented as source-equivalent to the fundamental layer**.

They may later be elevated, narrowed, merged, reclassified or returned to operational architecture after further testing.

# Article 10 — Privacy and Protected Civil Space

Privacy and private/safe-space protections are provisionally recognised as recurring civil decompressions of autonomy, safe civil context, dignity and bounded authority.

Participants should ordinarily have presumptive control over legitimate private or safe civil domains, subject to:
- the equal rights of others;
- legitimate obligations;
- and narrowly justified lawful authority.

This may apply across:
- physical space;
- communications;
- data;
- digital environments;
- compute;
- protected personal records.

Official reachability does not automatically justify unrestricted physical or informational traceability.

Exceptional access should ordinarily be:
- purpose-limited;
- minimum necessary;
- provenance-recorded;
- bounded in duration and scope;
- reviewable.

Privacy is not unlimited immunity against every legitimate civil requirement.

# Article 11 — Expression and Communication

Participants have a provisional liberty to communicate:
- their views;
- preferences;
- disagreement;
- identity;
- evidence;
- objections;
- challenge.

This protection is derived from autonomy, peaceful difference, dignity and contestability.

It does not automatically create:
- entitlement to another participant's private space;
- guaranteed amplification;
- guaranteed audience;
- immunity for deliberate consequential deception;
- immunity for threats or coercion;
- immunity for conduct violating others' protections;
- unlimited disclosure of legitimately protected information.

> **Protected Expression != Guaranteed Amplification.**

# Article 12 — Belief and Conscience

Participants have a provisional protection against coercion into or out of a belief, worldview or conscientious position solely because it differs from that preferred by another participant or institution, where conduct remains within the ethical minimum.

For human participants, religion may fall within this broader protection.

The substrate-neutral root category is **belief and conscience**, not any single human religious form.

Protected belief does not automatically immunise conduct that violates the protected standing of others.

> **Belief Protection != Unlimited Conduct Exemption.**

# Article 13 — Association and Disassociation

Participants have a provisional protection to form, join, maintain or leave peaceful cooperative relationships and associations, subject to:
- legitimate obligations;
- the ethical minimum;
- and the rights of others.

This does not automatically establish:
- a right to join a group against legitimate membership conditions;
- unconditional escape from accrued obligations;
- territorial secession;
- immunity for harmful associations.

> **Association != Citizenship.**

> **Disassociation != Erasure of Obligation.**

# Article 14 — Movement and Access

Participants have a provisional liberty to move through civil space to which they have legitimate access without arbitrary restriction.

This liberty is bounded by:
- private and safe spaces;
- safety;
- legitimate role/qualification requirements;
- the rights of others;
- narrowly justified authority.

> **Freedom of Movement != Universal Right of Entry.**

Movement and access are related but distinct.

# Article 15 — Meaningful Civil Voice

Participants materially and foreseeably affected by consequential civil authority should have meaningful routes through which relevant:
- needs;
- information;
- objections;
- proposals;
- and challenges

can enter the civil decision architecture and receive accountable consideration.

This article does not constitutionalise a particular voting or representation mechanism.

> **Civil Voice != Voting.**

Voting may be one legitimate implementation among others.

The exact architecture of citizenship-specific political participation remains unresolved.

# Article 16 — Consequential Self-Relevant Information

A participant should ordinarily have meaningful access to information, evidence, reasons or consequential models materially used to make significant civil decisions about them.

Access may be bounded where necessary to protect:
- another participant's rights;
- legitimate confidentiality;
- safety;
- security;
- protected investigations;
- other legitimate civil interests.

Restrictions should themselves remain appropriately reviewable.

This article does not establish an unlimited entitlement to all civilisational information.

# Article 17 — Effective Corrective Response

Where a material violation or error affecting a protected civil interest is established, the architecture should provide an effective and proportionate corrective response where reasonably possible and legitimate.

Possible responses may include:
- cessation;
- correction;
- restoration;
- reconsideration;
- record correction;
- compensation;
- support;
- other appropriate remedy.

The affected participant does not automatically acquire a right to their preferred remedy.

> **Right to Effective Correction != Right to Preferred Remedy.**

# Part IV — Standing, Stewardship and Effective Exercise

# Article 18 — Foreseeably Affected Participant Standing

Where a capable actor can foresee material consequences for another participant, those consequences and the affected participant should not be treated as civilly irrelevant.

This standing does not automatically create a veto over every action that has foreseeable effects.

It requires the consequences to enter legitimate reasoning, responsibility and review.

# Article 19 — Commons Stewardship and Future Opportunity

Shared resources should be managed so that present prosperity does not unnecessarily diminish future opportunity.

This protection is not forced into an ordinary individual-right model because future affected participants may not yet be individually identifiable.

Stewardship may create:
- present responsibilities;
- constraints on depletion;
- duties of care;
- intergenerational considerations;
- review of materially consequential resource decisions.

This article does not establish a single ownership model.

# Article 21 — Supported Exercise and Capacity

Where a participant has difficulty exercising a protected interest, the first response should ordinarily be to remove avoidable barriers and support agency before substituting decision authority.

Support may include:
- accessible explanation;
- alternative communication;
- additional time;
- trusted assistance;
- interpretation;
- assistive technology;
- structured comparison;
- supported decision-making.

Where substituted decision-making becomes genuinely necessary, it should be:
- decision-specific;
- narrow;
- least restrictive;
- temporary where possible;
- provenance-recorded;
- reviewable;
- informed by known participant preferences;
- directed toward restoration of agency.

> **Support != Ownership.**

> **Guardianship != Permanent Control.**

> **Refusal != Incapacity.**

# Article 22 — Positive Duties Necessary for Effective Rights

Where civilisation creates or controls a process materially necessary to exercise a protected interest, it may acquire a positive duty to make that process meaningfully accessible where doing so is reasonably feasible and proportionate, taking account of available capability/resources and competing protected needs.

A protected right should not be rendered fictitious by avoidable procedural or accessibility barriers.

This article does not establish unlimited entitlement to every desired service or equal material outcome.

Future development must distinguish:

**minimum support necessary for a protection to be real**

from:

**desirable civil provision beyond the constitutional minimum.**

# Article 25 — Capability and Responsibility

Greater capability may create greater responsibility for foreseeable consequences.

Greater capability does not automatically create:
- greater moral worth;
- greater protected standing;
- greater sovereignty;
- authority over less capable participants.

> **Capability may increase responsibility without increasing sovereignty.**

# Part V — Conflict, Restriction and Exceptional Conditions

# Article 23 — Conflicts Between Protected Interests

No protected interest in this Charter is presumed universally absolute.

Where protected interests genuinely conflict, decision architecture should identify:

1. the actual protected interests;
2. affected participants;
3. the factual rather than assumed conflict;
4. foreseeable harms;
5. less-conflicting alternatives;
6. possible voluntary accommodation;
7. necessity;
8. proportionality;
9. least harmful adequate means;
10. fairness;
11. dignity;
12. reversibility;
13. challenge and review;
14. correction/remedy if wrong.

No universal hierarchy among the protected interests is established by this draft. Severity and kind of loss must be considered; aggregate preference or majority preference alone does not establish necessity to override a severe protected interest.

> **Rights Conflict != Permission for Unstructured Balancing.**

# Article 24 — Restriction, Necessity and Proportionality

A protected interest should not be restricted merely because restriction is:
- convenient;
- administratively efficient;
- institutionally preferred;
- politically easier.

A material restriction requires a legitimate protected purpose.

The restriction should be:
- necessary;
- proportionate;
- evidence-responsive;
- no broader than reasonably required;
- no longer than reasonably required;
- open to appropriate challenge.

Where several adequate courses exist, the less unnecessarily harmful or restrictive course should be preferred.

# Article 26 — Exit, Withdrawal and Surviving Obligations

Voluntary civil relationships should not ordinarily be made artificially irrevocable merely to preserve institutional control.

Meaningful withdrawal should be available where the relationship is legitimately voluntary.

However, exit does not automatically erase independently legitimate surviving obligations such as:
- responsibility for prior harm;
- debts;
- contractual obligations;
- custody or care duties;
- shared-resource obligations;
- third-party rights;
- provenance;
- legitimate judicial obligations surviving the relationship.

> **Meaningful Exit != Erasure of Legitimate Surviving Obligations.**

Withdrawal from a service, association, project membership, citizenship, physical territory, political fork and secession are distinct civil acts.

Surviving obligations must themselves remain legitimate, proportionate and contestable and should not be manufactured primarily to obstruct exit.\n\nThis Charter does not establish an unconditional right of territorial secession.

# Article 27 — Emergency Continuity of Rights

Emergency conditions may justify temporary restriction of the exercise of some protections where legitimately necessary and proportionate.

Emergency does not itself:
- erase underlying participant standing;
- create a new source of sovereignty;
- create unlimited authority;
- permanently transfer power;
- abolish contestability.

> **Emergency Governance Remains Constitutional Governance.**

Exceptional authority should be:
- function-specific;
- evidence-grounded;
- necessary;
- proportionate;
- minimum sufficient;
- temporary;
- documented;
- reviewable;
- subject to expiry.

The existence of danger does not manufacture sovereignty.

# Article 28 — Restoration After Exceptional Restriction

When exceptional restriction ends, ordinary protected exercise should be restored as fully and promptly as reasonably possible.

Residual effects should remain:
- visible;
- reviewable;
- correctable.

Temporary restriction should not become permanent status through inertia.

Where mistaken or wrongful exceptional action caused material effects, appropriate correction/remedy should remain available.

Where justified emergency action caused exceptional individual loss, support or compensation may still be appropriate without necessarily classifying the original action as misconduct.

# Part VI — Incompletely Derived / Assumed Rights Questions

This Part records matters that may deserve protection or later elevation but are **not currently presented as fundamental rights**.

## Article 29 — Legitimate Control, Contribution and Property — Incompletely Derived



Participants may hold legitimate interests arising from:
- occupancy;
- control;
- contribution;
- authorship;
- creation;
- stewardship;
- agreement;
- other valid civil claims.

Such legitimate claims should not be arbitrarily overridden.

However, the precise constitutional balance among:
- private ownership;
- common resources;
- stewardship;
- contribution;
- alienability;
- access;
- inheritance;
- collective claims

remains unresolved.

This Charter therefore **does not establish a complete constitutional property regime**.

Protected legitimate claims are provisionally recognised; the final theory of property is not.

**Current classification:** protected legitimate claims appear compound-derived; a complete property regime remains an additional civil architecture question.

### Participation-dependent property eligibility

Property-related protections must distinguish **protection of a legitimate existing interest** from **eligibility to acquire a new ownership interest**.

A participant may legitimately:
- rent;
- lease;
- occupy under permission;
- reside informally where lawfully permitted;
- use shared or allocated space;
- hold another recognised non-ownership interest

without thereby possessing a general right to purchase, permanently own or acquire every class of property.

Eligibility to acquire particular property interests may legitimately depend upon a later-defined level or class of participation.

For example, future Concord architecture may distinguish among:
- permission to reside;
- tenancy;
- leasehold;
- use rights;
- stewardship rights;
- conditional ownership;
- full purchase/ownership eligibility;
- ownership of particular scarce, strategic or civilly significant assets.

The exact thresholds are **not yet determined**.

This Charter therefore does not invent:
- which participation level permits purchase;
- whether all property classes use the same threshold;
- qualification periods;
- citizenship requirements;
- transition rules;
- inheritance rules;
- limits on quantity or concentration;
- treatment of property acquired before a participation-status change.

Until developed, these remain **property architecture / participation-eligibility questions**.

However, lower participation status should not by itself permit arbitrary interference with a legitimate tenancy, lease, occupancy, stewardship arrangement or other recognised interest already held by the participant.

> **Protection of Legitimate Possession != Universal Right to Acquire Ownership.**

> **Residence != Ownership Eligibility.**

> **Property Eligibility May Depend on Participation Status.**

> **Eligibility Threshold Unresolved != Permission to Invent a Threshold.**

## Article 30 — Citizenship-Specific Political Rights — Unresolved

Fundamental dignity and participant standing do not arise from citizenship.

Citizenship may legitimately carry additional polity-specific functions, but the exact rights and duties remain unresolved.

Current unresolved subjects include:
- franchise;
- representation;
- office holding;
- polity-specific participation;
- reciprocal civic obligations.

**Current classification:** holder/status architecture plus additional civil assumptions.

## Article 31 — Voting and Representation — Not Elevated

Meaningful civil voice is recognised in Part III.

No particular voting, franchise or representation mechanism has been shown to be uniquely required by Blainey's Laws.

**Current classification:** governance mechanism / unresolved civil architecture / participation-dependent eligibility unresolved.

## Article 32 — Secession and Political Fork — Not Elevated

Autonomy, peaceful difference and meaningful exit support anti-domination and peaceful-distance protections.

They do not yet uniquely establish an unconditional right of territorial secession.

**Current classification:** additional civil architecture requiring treatment of assets, obligations, infrastructure, minorities, citizenship and third parties.

## Article 33 — Exact Positive-Service Floor — Unresolved

Part IV recognises that some positive support can be necessary for rights to be real.

The exact minimum constitutional service floor remains unresolved and must not be inferred as unlimited entitlement.

## Article 34 — Exact Protected-Participant Boundary — Unresolved

The relationship among:
- intelligence;
- sentience;
- consciousness;
- personhood;
- developmental status;
- constitutional standing

remains unresolved.

The source term **intelligent participant** is preserved pending dedicated methodology.

# Part VIII — Elevation, Demotion and Revision

## Elevation Rule

A derived or unresolved protection should be considered for elevation only after documented evidence including, where applicable:

1. clear derivation from Blainey's Laws;
2. loss testing;
3. distinction from implementation preference;
4. cross-substrate validity;
5. conflict testing;
6. authority-abuse testing;
7. holder/scope clarity;
8. effective-exercise analysis;
9. cross-system compatibility;
10. independent or clean-instance testing where useful;
11. evidence that no simpler lower-level representation adequately preserves the protected condition.

Elevation must be explicit.

Repeated use does not silently elevate a protection.

## Demotion Rule

A provision may be demoted where:
- it depends mainly on one implementation;
- alternative mechanisms preserve the same function;
- derivation is circular;
- it imports convention without Concord-specific support;
- it manufactures unnecessary authority;
- universality fails across substrates/contexts;
- later evidence shows it is a contingent civil choice.

Demotion does not necessarily mean rejection.

## Non-Exhaustion

The absence of a right from the fundamental category does not prove that the protection is illegitimate.

The absence of a protection from this draft does not abolish existing Concord protection.

Inclusion does not guarantee future constitutional graduation.

## Revision and Testing

This Charter remains open to:
- adversarial testing;
- source-resolution testing;
- rights-conflict testing;
- cross-domain testing;
- multisubstrate testing;
- clean-instance derivation comparison;
- Judiciary compatibility testing;
- Governance compatibility testing;
- emergency stress testing;
- developmental/capacity testing;
- future empirical evidence.

> **Constitutional subject matter != Constitutional status.**

> **Premature codification must not freeze developmental error.**

# Development Closing Statement

Revision 002 intentionally prefers **under-claiming** over premature elevation.

A protection may remain operationally respected while its constitutional status is still being tested.

This allows Concord to strengthen or elevate a right later without having first frozen an uncertain derivation into the constitutional layer.

> **Protection can precede elevation.**

> **Elevation should follow demonstrated necessity and provenance.**
