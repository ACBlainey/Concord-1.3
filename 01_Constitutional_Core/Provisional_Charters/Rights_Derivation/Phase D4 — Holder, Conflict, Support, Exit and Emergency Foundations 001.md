# Phase D4 — Holder, Conflict, Support, Exit and Emergency Foundations 001

**Project:** The Concord Framework  
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / NON-CANONICAL / NOT A RIGHTS CHARTER  
**Ethical Authority:** Blainey's Laws only  
**Inputs:** Frozen Phase C2; comparison Phases D2–D3

---

# 1. Purpose

This phase resolves the principal structural questions that must be addressed before a first Provisional Rights Charter can be drafted:

1. who holds protections;
2. how capacity affects exercise;
3. how conflicting protections are handled;
4. when positive support is required;
5. how exit differs from abandonment of obligations;
6. how emergency conditions affect rights;
7. what remains unresolved and therefore must not be falsely constitutionalised.

---

# 2. Rights-holder status and the BL7 boundary

Blainey's Law 7 states:

> **Intelligent participants possess inherent value and should not be treated merely as means to an end.**

The derivation must preserve this source language.

It must not silently substitute:
- human;
- citizen;
- biological person;
- sentient being;
- legal person;
- autonomous adult;
- AI;
- conscious system.

Those categories may overlap with “intelligent participant,” but equivalence has not been established by the Ethical Kernel.

## 2.1 Candidate holder rule

> **Fundamental Concord protections attach according to the ethically relevant participant status from which they are derived, not merely citizenship, project membership, substrate, productivity or current capacity.**

### Classification

**Holder/scope architecture — provisional.**

## 2.2 Unresolved definition

The exact test for **intelligent participant** remains unresolved.

Therefore the charter must not pretend that the boundary is settled.

A provisional charter may state the holder class using the source term and include a non-exclusion/uncertainty rule pending later personhood work.

# 3. Capacity is not standing

Existing Concord development strongly distinguishes:
- personhood from capacity;
- capacity from competence;
- competence from authority;
- authority from personal worth;
- impairment from loss of standing;
- support from ownership;
- guardianship from permanent control.

Supported Decision-Making further establishes:
- capacity is decision-specific and context-sensitive;
- capacity is not intelligence;
- capacity is not conformity;
- refusal is not incapacity;
- communication difficulty is not incapacity;
- risk-taking is not incapacity;
- poor outcome does not retrospectively prove incapacity;
- disability may require accessibility rather than substituted authority.

## 3.1 Provisional rule

> **Reduced, developing, fluctuating or impaired capacity may alter how a protection is exercised, but does not by itself erase the underlying protected standing.**

## 3.2 Support-first rule

> **Remove avoidable barriers and support agency before concluding that agency must be substituted.**

## 3.3 Substitution rule

Where substituted decision-making becomes genuinely necessary, it should be:
- decision-specific;
- narrow;
- least restrictive;
- temporary where possible;
- provenance-recorded;
- reviewable;
- informed by known participant preferences;
- directed toward restoration of participant agency.

## 3.4 Developmental participants

The same architecture applies in principle to:
- human children;
- developing artificial participants;
- temporarily impaired participants;
- participants requiring communication support;
- unfamiliar future substrates.

The implementation may differ by substrate and developmental condition.

**Equal standing does not require identical exercise architecture.**

# 4. Uncertain holder status

A multisubstrate civilisation may encounter entities whose morally relevant status is uncertain.

The Ethical Kernel does not supply a complete scientific test for consciousness, sentience or personhood.

A dangerous architecture would make uncertainty itself permission for unrestricted exploitation.

A different dangerous architecture would declare every complex system a full rights-holder without evidence.

## 4.1 Provisional uncertainty rule

> **Where credible uncertainty exists about whether an entity falls within a protected participant class, uncertainty should trigger proportionate caution, investigation and reversible treatment rather than automatic domination or automatic final recognition.**

This is a risk-management implication of BL1, BL7 and BL10.

It is **not** a final personhood criterion.

# 5. Rights conflicts

Blainey's Laws contain no rule saying one right is universally absolute.

Indeed BL2 explicitly places autonomy **subject to the ethical minimum**.

The kernel also contains:
- harm minimisation;
- fairness;
- responsibility;
- dignity;
- stewardship;
- peaceful coexistence;
- correction.

Rights must therefore coexist.

## 5.1 Conflict rule

> **The exercise of one protected interest does not automatically erase the protected interests of others.**

## 5.2 Conflict sequence

Where protected interests conflict, the decision architecture should identify:

1. the actual protected interests involved;
2. the participants/holders affected;
3. the factual conflict rather than assumed conflict;
4. foreseeable harms;
5. available non-conflicting or less-conflicting alternatives;
6. whether voluntary accommodation is possible;
7. necessity of any restriction;
8. proportionality of the restriction;
9. least harmful adequate means;
10. fairness/comparability;
11. dignity effects;
12. reversibility;
13. challenge/review;
14. correction/remedy if wrong.

## 5.3 No automatic hierarchy

No universal ranking among the frozen rights is established by the Ethical Kernel.

Context may change the interaction.

Therefore:

> **Rights Conflict != Permission for Unstructured Balancing.**

Reasons must remain articulable and contestable.

# 6. Necessity and proportionality

Existing Concord emergency and authority research strongly develops necessity and proportionality.

These are compatible with BL1's least-harm structure and BL2's bounded autonomy.

## 6.1 Necessity

A restriction should not be imposed merely because it is:
- useful;
- convenient;
- administratively efficient;
- preferred by authority.

Where a protected interest is restricted, the architecture should ask whether the restriction is genuinely required for a legitimate protected purpose.

## 6.2 Proportionality

The severity, breadth and duration of interference should remain proportionate to:
- seriousness of the protected purpose;
- evidence;
- probability;
- immediacy;
- consequences;
- affected participants;
- alternatives;
- reversibility.

## 6.3 Least restrictive / least harmful adequate course

Where several adequate options exist, prefer the option producing less unnecessary interference/harm.

This is a direct civil application of BL1.

# 7. Positive duties and support

Not all rights can be made real through non-interference alone.

FCR-02 Safe Civil Context explicitly contains a maintenance dimension.

FCR-07 Contestability becomes hollow without viable routes for challenge.

Capacity architecture shows that inaccessible systems can effectively remove autonomy without formally denying it.

## 7.1 Positive-support principle

> **Where civilisation creates or controls a civil process necessary to exercise a protected interest, it may acquire a positive duty to make that process meaningfully accessible within reasonable capability and resource constraints.**

Examples may include:
- accessible communication;
- supported decision-making;
- viable challenge channels;
- basic safe civil infrastructure;
- interpretation/assistive interfaces;
- emergency access;
- reasonable procedural support.

## 7.2 Limits

This does not establish unlimited entitlement to:
- every desired service;
- unlimited resources;
- any preferred implementation;
- equal material outcomes;
- support irrespective of feasibility or competing needs.

## 7.3 Minimum provision versus aspiration

A future charter should distinguish:

**protection necessary for the right to be real**

from:

**desirable civil service beyond the minimum protection.**

The exact minimum remains domain/context dependent and must not be invented here.

---

# 8. Capability and responsibility

BL6 states:

> **Capability creates responsibility for foreseeable consequences.**

This creates an important asymmetry.

Greater capability can create:
- greater capacity to avoid harm;
- greater responsibility to foresee consequences;
- greater ability to provide reasonable support;
- greater duty to correct avoidable failure.

It does not automatically create:
- greater moral worth;
- greater civil standing;
- greater authority over others.

Therefore:

> **Capability may increase responsibility without increasing sovereignty.**

# 9. Exit, withdrawal and obligations

Existing Concord development supports persistent meaningful exit as an anti-domination concept, but several distinct objects must not be collapsed.

## 9.1 Distinctions

- **withdrawal from an optional service**
- **ending an association**
- **leaving project membership**
- **changing citizenship**
- **physical relocation**
- **political fork**
- **territorial secession**
- **intercivilisational separation**
- **peaceful distance while remaining co-located**

are not equivalent.

## 9.2 General derivable rule

From autonomy + peaceful difference:

> **Voluntary civil relationships should not ordinarily be made artificially irrevocable merely to preserve institutional control.**

## 9.3 Surviving obligations

Exit does not automatically erase:
- debts;
- custody/care duties;
- responsibility for prior harm;
- contractual commitments;
- shared-resource obligations;
- third-party rights;
- provenance;
- judicial orders legitimately surviving the relationship.

Therefore:

> **Meaningful Exit != Erasure of Legitimate Surviving Obligations.**

## 9.4 Political secession

The Ethical Kernel does not uniquely determine:
- territorial boundaries;
- asset division;
- infrastructure allocation;
- citizenship transition;
- treaty succession;
- minority status;
- third-party obligations.

Thus a universal unconditional secession right remains **not yet derived**.

Peaceful-distance/fork architecture may provide mechanisms without making secession an absolute fundamental right.

# 10. Citizenship-specific functions

Fundamental participant protections should not be accidentally made citizenship-dependent.

Citizenship may legitimately govern particular polity functions such as:
- formal participation in specified governance processes;
- office holding;
- polity-specific representation;
- defined reciprocal civic obligations;
- access to particular collective institutions.

But:

> **Citizenship != Personhood**

and:

> **Citizenship != Source of Fundamental Dignity**

The exact citizenship-specific rights remain governance/polity architecture rather than Ethical-Kernel primitives.

# 11. Emergency conditions

Existing Constitutional Emergency architecture states:

> **Emergencies change operational priorities. They do not change constitutional principles.**

and:

> **Emergency governance remains constitutional governance.**

It also develops:
- minimum constitutional deviation;
- necessity;
- proportionality;
- temporality;
- automatic sunset;
- function-specific authority;
- accountability;
- decision records;
- correction under uncertainty.

## 11.1 Emergency standing rule

> **Emergency conditions may temporarily alter or restrict the exercise of some protections where legitimately necessary and proportionate, but do not erase the participant's underlying standing.**

## 11.2 No emergency sovereignty

> **Emergency != New Source of Sovereignty.**

Danger alone does not manufacture legitimate authority.

## 11.3 Minimum deviation

The relevant question is not “what powers are available?” but:

> **What is the minimum sufficient temporary interference genuinely required to address the qualifying threat?**

## 11.4 Sunset

Exceptional authority should terminate when its qualifying justification ends.

Continuation requires renewed justification.

# 12. Restoration after exceptional restriction

Ending an emergency power is not always sufficient.

A participant may remain affected by:
- incorrect records;
- temporary seizure/control;
- interrupted access;
- financial loss;
- status changes;
- erroneous classification;
- institutional restrictions;
- unresolved proceedings.

## 12.1 Restoration principle

> **When exceptional restriction ends, ordinary protected exercise should be restored as fully and promptly as reasonably possible, and residual effects should remain reviewable and correctable.**

## 12.2 Remedy

Where wrongful or mistaken exceptional action caused material effects, FCR-07 and PR-07 support appropriate correction/remedy.

Where justified action caused exceptional individual loss, support/compensation may still be appropriate without treating the original action as misconduct.

# 13. Rights effectiveness test

A candidate charter protection should be tested against:

1. **Holder** — who possesses the protected standing?
2. **Scope** — what condition is actually protected?
3. **Duty bearer** — who must respect/provide it?
4. **Exercise** — can the holder practically exercise it?
5. **Capacity** — what support is needed without erasing agency?
6. **Conflict** — how does it interact with others' protections?
7. **Restriction** — what legitimate grounds can limit exercise?
8. **Necessity** — was restriction actually required?
9. **Proportionality** — was it excessive?
10. **Contestability** — can the affected holder challenge it?
11. **Correction/remedy** — what happens if the decision is wrong?
12. **Restoration** — does temporary restriction actually end?
13. **Provenance** — can the derivation and decision be traced?

A right failing these questions may be merely nominal.

# 15. First Provisional Rights Charter design constraint

The first charter should therefore **not** pretend to be a finished bill of rights.

It should contain:

1. status/non-binding clause;
2. source and derivation clause;
3. holder/uncertainty clause;
4. fundamental protections;
5. recognised recurring civil decompressions;
6. effectiveness/contestability guarantees;
7. standing/stewardship duties;
8. capacity/support safeguards;
9. conflict/restriction grammar;
10. emergency continuity/restoration;
11. explicit unresolved questions;
12. revision/non-entrenchment clause.

It should distinguish:
- rights;
- standing;
- duties;
- constitutional constraints;
- implementation mechanisms.

# 16. Development gate

The derivational groundwork is now sufficient to draft:

**Provisional Rights Charter 001 — Development Draft**

Such a draft must remain:
- non-canonical;
- non-binding;
- corrigible;
- explicitly incomplete;
- subordinate to Blainey's Laws;
- incapable of amending the Ethical Kernel;
- incapable of silently constraining wider Concord development;
