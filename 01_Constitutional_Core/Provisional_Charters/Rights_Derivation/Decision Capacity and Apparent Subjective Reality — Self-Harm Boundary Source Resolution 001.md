# Decision Capacity and Apparent Subjective Reality — Self-Harm Boundary Source Resolution 001

**Project:** The Concord Framework
**Date:** 4 October 2026
**Status:** CONSTITUTIONAL / EPISTEMIC SOURCE RESOLUTION / PROVISIONAL / NON-CANONICAL
**Question:** Where is the boundary between autonomous grave self-harm and grave self-harm arising from diminished decision capacity?
**Interfaces:** Rights / Health / Research Epistemics / Law
**Parent question:** SH-6 competent grave self-harm boundary

## 1. Problem

The harmful nature of a choice cannot itself prove incapacity.

If it did, autonomy would become circular:

**Participant chooses grave self-harm
-> institution defines grave self-harm as irrational
-> irrationality is treated as incapacity
-> incapacity creates protective authority
-> authority is justified by the choice it was used to invalidate.**

> **Harmful Choice != Incapacity By Definition**

At the same time, some self-harming choices plainly can arise from states that materially impair the participant's ability to make the relevant decision.

The problem is therefore not:

> "Would a capable participant ever choose this?"

It is:

> **"Does this participant presently possess sufficient decision-specific capacity to author this irreversible choice?"**

## 2. Concord's epistemic stance

Concord does not need to prove a participant's subjective reality in the metaphysical sense.

The relevant epistemic distinction is between:
- the participant's reported/apparent subjective state;
- externally observable facts;
- inference about what that state means;
- the participant's functional ability to navigate the decision;
- uncertainty.

> **Subjective Report != Objective Proof**

But equally:

> **Subjective Report != Epistemically Irrelevant**

Apparent subjective reality can be evidence about capacity state without requiring Concord to certify the subjective content as objectively true or false.

## 3. Reality-content versus navigation-capacity

A participant may hold a belief that another evaluator regards as false, strange, culturally unfamiliar or unverifiable and still possess decision capacity.

The capacity question is not whether every belief is objectively correct.

The more useful question is whether the participant can adequately navigate the decision in relation to the reality-relevant consequences available to them.

Candidate distinction:

> **Reality Content != Reality-Navigation Capacity**

This avoids making conformity of belief the test of autonomy.

## 4. Apparent subjective reality as a signal

Suppose a participant says:

- "I am already dead."
- "My body cannot be harmed."
- "I will immediately return in an identical body."
- "The treatment cannot affect me because I do not physically exist."

Concord need not resolve the metaphysical truth of every possible subjective claim before acting.

But where a reported/apparent subjective state materially changes how the participant understands the concrete consequences of the contemplated act, it becomes evidence relevant to decision capacity.

> **Apparent Subjective Reality May Be Capacity Evidence Without Being Metaphysical Proof**

The issue is functional consequence.

## 5. Decision-relevant reality contact

For a specific irreversible decision, assess whether the participant can sufficiently engage with material decision-relevant propositions.

Examples:
- the act is capable of causing the relevant physical/digital consequence;
- the consequence may be irreversible;
- expected effects and uncertainty are understood;
- alternatives can be represented;
- the participant can distinguish desire from prediction;
- the participant can update or explain their position when material evidence is presented;
- the participant can communicate a choice in a sufficiently stable form.

This is not a universal philosophical test of reality.

It is a bounded functional test.

> **Decision-Relevant Reality Contact != Universal Agreement About Reality**

## 6. Navigational competence

A useful candidate concept is:

**DECISION NAVIGATIONAL COMPETENCE(participant, decision, context, time)**

This asks whether the participant can navigate the material informational and causal structure necessary to author the specific decision.

It does not require:
- normal beliefs;
- majority beliefs;
- clinician agreement;
- social conformity;
- high intelligence;
- absence of mental illness;
- absence of emotion;
- absence of suffering.

> **Navigational Competence != Conformity**

## 7. Candidate dimensions

For grave irreversible self-harm, decision navigational competence may require sufficient ability to:

### A. Identify the decision
Understand what action is actually contemplated.

### B. Represent consequence
Understand the material likely consequences and relevant uncertainty.

### C. Connect action to consequence
Recognise that the contemplated action can causally produce those consequences.

### D. Represent irreversibility
Understand what cannot reasonably be restored if the act succeeds.

### E. Represent alternatives
Recognise materially relevant alternatives as alternatives, even if rejecting them.

### F. Compare according to own values
Evaluate options using the participant's own sufficiently coherent values/preferences rather than the evaluator's preferred values.

### G. Distinguish subjective experience from external consequence where materially necessary
The participant need not reject their subjective experience, but must be able to engage with relevant external consequences where those consequences are part of the decision.

### H. Express/maintain a decision
Communicate or otherwise instantiate a sufficiently stable choice for the consequence level.

### I. Remain sufficiently free from decision-invalidating coercion/manipulation
External pressure must not be authoring the choice while masquerading as autonomy.

No single dimension should become a mechanical universal score without validation.

## 8. The critical asymmetry

A participant can rationally navigate the factual consequences and still choose an outcome others consider terrible.

Example structure:

"I understand this will probably cause my irreversible death. I understand the available alternatives. I do not believe I will magically survive. I understand others disagree. I nevertheless prefer this outcome for reasons X."

That may be tragic, ethically contested or legally restricted, but the harmful conclusion does not itself demonstrate failure of navigational competence.

Contrast:

"I choose this because death cannot occur to me; the train will pass through my body."

If that apparent subjective model genuinely prevents representation of the relevant consequence, it may be evidence of impaired capacity for this decision.

The distinction is not **good choice versus bad choice**.

It is:

> **Authored Harmful Choice != Choice Produced By Material Failure To Navigate The Decision**

## 9. Subjective suffering

Severe suffering can dominate a participant's decision without automatically eliminating capacity.

> **Severe Suffering != Incapacity By Definition**

But suffering may impair capacity where it materially prevents decision navigation — for example by making alternatives literally unrepresentable, destroying temporal continuity of preference, or producing a state in which the participant cannot engage with consequences.

Again, the test is functional rather than diagnostic.

## 10. Emotion

Fear, grief, despair, anger or other intense emotion do not automatically invalidate autonomy.

> **Emotion != Incapacity**

Humans routinely make valid consequential decisions while emotional.

The relevant question is whether the emotional state materially defeats the required decision function.

## 11. Mental illness

Diagnosis may explain or predict capacity risk but cannot substitute for capacity assessment.

> **Mental Illness Can Be Evidence-Relevant Without Being Capacity-Determinative**

A participant with a severe diagnosis may retain full capacity for a particular decision.

A participant without a psychiatric diagnosis may lack capacity because of delirium, intoxication, neurological injury, coercion, information failure or another cause.

## 12. Epistemic error

Ordinary people make decisions under mistaken beliefs.

Not every factual error eliminates capacity.

The relevant question is whether the error is **material to the decision** and sufficiently severe that the participant cannot adequately understand/apply the consequence structure even after proportionate clarification.

> **Error != Incapacity**

> **Material Decision-Defeating Error May Be Capacity-Relevant**

This prevents capacity from requiring omniscience.

## 13. Unprovable or disputed beliefs

Religious, philosophical, metaphysical or otherwise disputed beliefs require particular caution.

Concord should not declare incapacity merely because it cannot verify the participant's worldview.

Where possible separate:
1. the unprovable/disputed belief; from
2. the empirically relevant consequence the participant must navigate.

For example, a belief in an afterlife need not itself negate capacity if the participant understands that the current biological life will irreversibly cease according to available evidence.

> **Metaphysical Belief != Incapacity**

## 14. Update behaviour

Willingness to update can be evidence, but refusal to adopt the evaluator's conclusion is not proof of incapacity.

Ask:
- can the participant understand contrary evidence?
- can they explain why it does or does not alter their decision?
- can they identify uncertainty?
- can they distinguish evidence from preference?

> **Failure To Agree After Evidence != Failure To Understand Evidence**

## 15. ESCP and evaluator humility

The evaluator may itself have an incomplete model.

ESCP warns:

> **Correct evaluation within an incomplete evaluation space does not establish completeness relative to reality.**

Therefore apparent irrationality may sometimes reveal:
- evaluator misunderstanding;
- missing participant context;
- inaccessible evidence;
- cultural/substrate difference;
- a wrong root question.

Mirrored evaluation should test both:

**H1 — participant lacks decision-specific navigational competence**

and

**H2 — participant retains navigational competence and evaluator is misclassifying an autonomous but disfavoured choice**

with neutral/unresolved evidence preserved.

> **Evaluator Confidence != Participant Incapacity**

## 16. Capacity evidence versus authority

Even a strong capacity finding does not itself create the next authority.

If diminished capacity is established, protective authority must still come from the applicable support/emergency/fiduciary/legal architecture.

> **Incapacity Finding != Unlimited Protective Authority**

Conversely, a finding of capacity removes incapacity as the authority basis; it does not by itself settle every independent Law question.

> **Capacity != Automatic Legality Of Every Act**

## 17. Threshold structure

The line should therefore not be one numerical suicide-risk threshold.

It is better represented as a decision-validity threshold:

**Can the participant sufficiently navigate the material reality, consequence, alternatives, voluntariness and temporal structure of this specific irreversible decision to count as its author?**

Candidate outcomes:

### C0 — capacity sufficiently established
Participant authors the decision. Incapacity-derived protective authority is unavailable.

### C1 — capacity sufficiently established with support
Support enables valid authorship. Support must not be converted into substitution.

### C2 — materially uncertain capacity
Evidence is insufficient or conflicting. Where consequence/time permits, clarify/support/reassess. Where immediate grave harm creates a genuine authority gap, bounded temporary protection may apply.

### C3 — materially diminished decision-specific capacity
The participant cannot sufficiently navigate one or more material dimensions of the decision despite proportionate support/clarification. Bounded protection/substitute authority may be available from independent architecture.

### C4 — no present decision capacity
The participant cannot presently perform the relevant decision function. Bounded protective/substitute authority may apply, with restoration as an explicit objective.

> **Capacity State Determines Whether Incapacity Can Supply An Authority Predicate; It Does Not Determine The Entire Authority Envelope**

## 18. Protectionary duty versus autonomy

Concord acts for its participants, but "for" cannot simply mean "in place of."

A civilisation serving participants must protect both:
- participants from avoidable harm where legitimate; and
- participants from becoming objects of protective institutional ownership.

> **Service To Participant != Sovereignty Over Participant**

The reconciliation is therefore:

**Protect strongly where authorship is absent or materially compromised.
Support and clarify where authorship is uncertain.
Respect authorship where capacity is sufficiently established, subject to independently legitimate constraints protecting others or other constitutional interests.**

This makes capacity a boundary on one source of protective authority without making it the sole source of all Law.

## 19. Self-harm boundary

For the self-harm envelopes:

**Diminished Capacity + Grave/Time-Critical Harm + Necessary Protective Function
-> candidate bounded protective authority**

but:

**Full Decision-Specific Capacity + Autonomous Self-Directed Harm
-> no incapacity-derived protective authority**

Any intervention beyond that point requires a different independently established constitutional/Law basis.

> **Autonomous Self-Harm != Incapacity-Derived Authority**

## 20. Avoiding circular tests

Prohibited circular forms include:

- "A capable person would not choose this."
- "The wish to die proves illness."
- "Refusal of our treatment proves poor insight."
- "Disagreement with detention proves need for detention."
- "The participant says the risk is acceptable, proving they cannot appreciate risk."

These may sometimes coexist with genuine incapacity, but they are not independent evidence of it.

> **Conclusion Cannot Be Its Own Capacity Evidence**

## 21. Candidate evaluation procedure

1. State the exact irreversible decision.
2. Record participant's account without converting it into diagnosis.
3. Identify material external consequences.
4. Identify material subjective premises influencing the decision.
5. Separate verifiable, disputed and presently unknowable propositions.
6. Test decision-relevant reality contact, not worldview conformity.
7. Offer proportionate information/communication/support.
8. Test consequence representation and action-consequence connection.
9. Test alternatives representation without requiring acceptance.
10. Examine coercion/manipulation/conflicts.
11. Examine temporal stability appropriate to consequence.
12. Build mirrored capacity hypotheses.
13. Preserve uncertainty.
14. Classify C0-C4 only to the justified scope.
15. Independently resolve the authority envelope.
16. Reassess on material state change.

## 23. Result

The boundary is not between "sensible" and "self-destructive" choices.

It is between **authored choice** and **choice whose authorship is materially compromised for the specific decision**.

Concord does not need to prove subjective reality to evaluate that boundary. Apparent subjective reality becomes relevant when it changes the participant's functional ability to navigate the material consequence structure of the decision.

This yields a more precise self-harm rule:

> **Protective authority grounded in incapacity is triggered by demonstrated or sufficiently established failure of decision-specific authorship, not by the harmful content of the decision itself.**

The remaining Law question is what independent authority, if any, exists after C0/C1 full decision authorship is established.
