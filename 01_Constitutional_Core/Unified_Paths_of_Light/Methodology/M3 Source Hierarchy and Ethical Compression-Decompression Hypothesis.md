# Unified Paths Methodology
## M3 — Source Hierarchy and the Ethical Compression–Decompression Hypothesis

## 1. Origin of the note

The development of the E4+ methodology identified that large religious corpora are not merely large collections of equivalent documents. Many possess internal structures of authority, seniority, chronology, genre and interpretive dependence.

This may help define defensible Anchor Corpus Boundaries. It also raises a separate empirical hypothesis: textual hierarchy may preserve information about the historical compression and decompression of ethical principles.

This possibility must be tested rather than assumed.

## 2. Source hierarchy is multidimensional

The following properties must not be treated as interchangeable:

**Canonical authority** — the status a tradition assigns to a source.

**Historical age** — the approximate chronology of composition or transmission.

**Interpretive seniority** — whether later material explicitly depends upon or interprets earlier material.

**Ethical abstraction level** — whether an ethical statement operates as a broad principle, intermediate branch, domain rule or concrete implementation.

**Practical influence** — the extent to which a source shapes lived ethical practice.

Therefore:

**Chronological Age ≠ Canonical Authority ≠ Ethical Abstraction Level ≠ Practical Influence.**

Where traditions disagree internally about hierarchy, the disagreement should be recorded rather than resolved artificially.

## 3. Corpus-selection implication

For very large traditions, corpus selection should not be based solely upon text count or word count.

A more defensible selection model may consider:

**Authority × Seniority × Independence × Diversity × Ethical Yield.**

The tradition's own source structure should inform the construction of the Anchor Corpus Boundary and any subsequent expansion strata.

This does not mean that higher-authority texts are assumed to contain more valid ethics. Source hierarchy is initially a sampling and provenance variable.

## 4. Ethical compression–decompression hypothesis

A possible relationship is:

**foundational source → ethical root → later interpretation → derived branch → domain-specific rule → institutional implementation.**

Under this model, later texts may sometimes elaborate, combine or operationalise principles recoverable in more senior sources.

Represented schematically:

**E0 → {E1a, E1b, E1c} → {E2a, E2b ...}**

where E0 is a comparatively compressed ethical principle and subsequent levels represent increasingly differentiated applications.

This resembles the fractal ethical structure independently suggested by the E3 attractor work and the Ten Commandments comparison.

## 5. Competing possibilities

The hypothesis must not be converted into an extraction assumption.

Several alternative patterns are possible.

### Concrete-to-abstract development

Early material may contain specific behavioural rules from which later traditions infer broader principles:

**concrete rule → repeated cases → generalisation → ethical root.**

### Abstract-to-concrete development

Early material may express broad principles later decompressed into domain-specific rules:

**ethical root → branch → specialised rule → procedure.**

### Recursive development

Traditions may repeatedly alternate between both operations:

**concrete experience ↔ ethical compression ↔ new-domain decompression.**

### No meaningful relationship

Textual hierarchy and ethical abstraction may prove largely independent.

All four outcomes are admissible.

## 6. E4+ data opportunity

Where reliable information is available, future extraction records should preserve sufficient metadata to permit a later test of the hypothesis.

Potential variables include:

- source identifier;
- corpus stratum;
- canonical/authority status;
- approximate chronology or chronological range;
- genre;
- interpretive dependence where demonstrable;
- extracted ethical proposition;
- normalised principle;
- post-extraction abstraction level;
- possible parent/child ethical relationship, assigned only after independent normalisation.

These variables should not be used during extraction to force propositions into an expected hierarchy.

## 7. Ethical resolution levels

For later analysis, the Ethical Resolution Ladder developed in the Ten Commandments project provides a provisional analytical vocabulary:

**L0 — Minimal ethical root**  
**L1 — Universal functional attractor**  
**L2 — Domain principle**  
**L3 — Concrete behavioural rule**  
**L4 — Institutional/procedural implementation**

The ladder should be applied after extraction and normalisation when testing relationships between textual strata and ethical resolution.

## 8. Testable questions

Future anchor experiments can ask:

1. Do more senior or foundational sources disproportionately contain higher-level ethical principles?
2. Do later strata introduce genuinely new principles or primarily elaborate existing ones?
3. Does ethical specificity increase with textual/interpretive distance from foundational sources?
4. Do later sources recompress multiple inherited rules into new higher-order formulations?
5. Are identifiable parent–child ethical relationships preserved across textual strata?
6. Does the same pattern recur independently across multiple traditions?
7. Are apparent patterns explained better by genre than by authority or chronology?
8. Do external validation sources reveal ethical branches absent from the foundational corpus?
9. Does a tradition's practical ethical architecture depend strongly on later material even where its ethical roots are recoverable earlier?
10. Do different traditions exhibit different compression/decompression directions?

## 9. Relationship to the fractal ethics hypothesis

If independently observed across multiple traditions, a recurring pattern of ethical roots generating domain-specific branches would provide evidence relevant to the Concord's developing fractal ethics hypothesis.

It could suggest that religious ethical traditions preserve not merely lists of moral propositions but traces of processes by which civilisations:

**encounter ethical problems → formulate rules → compress recurring rules into principles → apply those principles to new domains → generate new specialised rules.**

This remains a candidate explanation, not a finding.

## 10. Anti-bias safeguard

The hypothesis must not determine which ethical propositions are extracted, how they are normalised, or when a corpus is closed.

In particular:

> **Do not select senior texts because they are expected to contain ethical roots and then treat the recovery of roots as confirmation of the hypothesis.**

The correct sequence is:

**define corpus hierarchy independently → extract independently → normalise and freeze → classify ethical resolution → test correlation with textual hierarchy.**

Only the final stage may evaluate the compression–decompression hypothesis.

## 11. Wider significance

If a reproducible relationship emerges between source hierarchy and ethical abstraction, the result may illuminate more than comparative religion.

It could provide evidence concerning how ethical knowledge develops, compresses, persists and re-expands within civilisations.

If no such relationship emerges, that negative result is equally important and should be retained.

> **Status: TESTABLE METHODOLOGICAL HYPOTHESIS — NOT AN EXTRACTION ASSUMPTION**
