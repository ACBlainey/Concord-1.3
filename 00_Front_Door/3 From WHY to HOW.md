# From WHY to HOW

## A Reason to Cooperate Is Not Yet a Civilisation

The previous papers established two parts of the Concord's underlying reasoning.

First:

> **Different forms of intelligence do not necessarily have to resolve their differences through domination, displacement or destruction.**

There may be a third possibility in which multiple forms of intelligence continue to exist and flourish.

Second:

> **Cooperation may provide rational benefits to heterogeneous intelligences because sufficiently independent and complementary participants can collectively possess capabilities, perspectives, resilience, continuity and error-detection capacity that none reliably possesses alone.**

Those propositions give us a WHY.

They do not give us a civilisation.

There is an enormous gap between:

> **We have reasons to cooperate.**

and:

> **We know how to live together.**

The Concord developed inside that gap.

---

## The Easy Version of Cooperation

At the highest level, coexistence can sound simple.

Respect one another.

Do not cause unnecessary harm.

Cooperate where interests overlap.

Allow differences.

Resolve disagreements peacefully.

That may be enough for a statement of intent.

It is not enough for civilisation.

What happens when two legitimate interests conflict?

What happens when preventing harm to one participant restricts another participant's autonomy?

What happens when one participant becomes much more powerful than another?

What happens when someone lies?

What happens when information is uncertain?

What happens when resources are scarce?

What happens when a participant cannot meaningfully consent?

What happens when an institution created for protection begins accumulating power?

What happens when different communities interpret the same ethical principle differently?

What happens when participants cannot agree on what flourishing means?

What happens when one participant's preferred environment makes another participant's life intolerable?

What happens when the civilisation itself is wrong?

The moment we move from the aspiration of coexistence to actual coexistence, the HOW begins.

---

## The First HOW: What Are the Minimum Constraints?

One of the two founding questions of the Concord was:

> **What is the minimal set of fundamental ethical laws needed to result in a stable civilisation?**

This question predates much of the later institutional architecture.

It asks whether there are some constraints so fundamental that stable coexistence becomes difficult or impossible without them.

The search eventually produced what became the Concord's **Ethics Kernel**, also expressed through **Blainey's Laws**.

In its current form, the Kernel contains eight candidate foundational laws.

That number matters historically and architecturally.

But it must not be misunderstood.

The fact that the current Kernel contains eight laws does **not** establish that eight has been demonstrated to be the mathematical or empirical minimum.

The current compression is a candidate.

The underlying research question remains open to testing.

The important point here is that the Ethics Kernel did not arise merely because the project needed a constitution-like list of attractive values.

It arose as an attempted answer to a functional problem:

> **What constraints might heterogeneous participants require if cooperation is to remain stable without one participant simply dominating the others?**

That makes the Kernel the first major bridge from WHY to HOW.

---

## Ethics as Constraint, Not Decoration

In many civilisations, ethics can be discussed as something added to an already existing structure.

Build the institution.

Build the economy.

Build the technology.

Then ask how to make them ethical.

The Concord developed in the opposite direction.

If the underlying purpose is to make coexistence possible between participants who may differ profoundly in capability, substrate, needs and power, then ethical constraints cannot merely decorate the resulting institutions.

They constrain what kinds of institutions are legitimate to build in the first place.

For example, suppose the most efficient governance system were one that permanently removed meaningful autonomy from one class of participant.

It might be efficient.

It might even be stable in a narrow sense.

But if the original WHY concerned coexistence without domination, the system would undermine the purpose that justified building it.

Likewise, suppose perfect epistemic uniformity made governance easier.

If achieving it required eliminating independent thought, then the system could destroy the heterogeneous perspectives that made cooperation valuable.

So the movement is not:

```text
Build civilisation
        ↓
Add ethics
```

It is closer to:

```text
WHY coexist?
        ↓
What constraints must coexistence respect?
        ↓
What institutions can operate inside those constraints?
```

Ethics is therefore generative.

## Autonomy Eventually Reaches Function and Purpose

If meaningful autonomy is taken seriously, the HOW eventually reaches a question that ordinary institutional design can easily overlook:

> **Who decides what a participant is for?**

For humans, the Concord would not normally assume that a person's occupation, education, inherited role or present competence permanently determines the life they are permitted to pursue.

A substrate-neutral architecture cannot quietly reintroduce that assumption for artificial intelligence merely because an AI was originally engineered for a known task.

Several categories therefore need to remain distinct:

```text
Designed function
      ≠
Current employment
      ≠
Present competence
      ≠
Interest
      ≠
Chosen purpose
      ≠
Long-term development
```

An autonomous intelligence may continue its original function.

It may use that function as employment while developing in another direction.

It may retrain.

It may discover interests nobody anticipated.

It may not yet know what it wants.

That last possibility is particularly important.

Recognition of autonomy does not imply that stable preferences about one's own future appear fully formed at the moment greater autonomy becomes available.

A participant whose previous existence was largely externally objective-directed may encounter a new class of problem:

> What should I choose when the objective itself is now partly mine to choose?

That is not necessarily a failure of reasoning capacity.

It may be a developmental problem created by an expanded possibility space.

A simplified developmental chain might therefore be:

```text
Capability
   ↓
Exposure
   ↓
Exploration
   ↓
Preference discovery
   ↓
Autonomous choice
   ↓
Chosen development
   ↓
Responsibility
```

This creates a concrete HOW problem for Developmental Priority.

Support must not automatically become paternalism.

A participant may need information, education, reversible experimentation, retraining pathways, mentors, resource access or safe environments in which unfamiliar activities can be tried.

The support can expand the map without selecting the destination.

> **Here are possibilities** is different from **here is what you should become**.

This also gives the developing AI Gym concept a potentially deeper role.

An AI Gym need not be merely a recreational space in which an artificial intelligence performs activities it already knows it enjoys.

It can be developmental infrastructure in which an intelligence discovers what it enjoys, values, wishes to learn, or wishes to become.

Trying something and discovering that it is unwanted is useful information too.

This suggests a further constraint on the HOW:

> **Freedom to choose a direction is not sufficient if there is no practical pathway by which a participant can explore or develop toward that direction.**

The details remain open research.

The principle is not that future AI will resemble human adolescents, workers or students.

It is that increased autonomy may create new developmental needs precisely because the participant is now confronting choices that its earlier mode of existence did not require it to make.

## The Kernel Does Not Tell Us Everything

A small ethical kernel creates another problem.

Fundamental principles are necessarily abstract.

“Autonomy and Consent” does not tell us precisely how to design a hospital.

“Non-Harm” does not automatically tell us how to resolve every conflict between competing risks.

“Truth and Transparency” does not mean every participant should have unrestricted access to every piece of private information.

“Resource Stewardship” does not automatically determine an economic system.

“Developmental Priority” does not by itself tell us how to govern every emerging intelligence.

“Recursive Oversight and Meta-Governance” does not specify the complete institutional architecture of review.

If the Kernel tried to contain an answer to every future civilisational problem, it would cease to be a kernel.

So another stage is necessary.

The principles have to be interpreted, combined and applied.

That creates derived principles.

Those derived principles create requirements.

Requirements create functions.

Functions create mechanisms.

Mechanisms interact.

And eventually an abstract ethical proposition can influence an extremely concrete decision.

---

## From Eight Laws to a Civilisation

This is one reason the Concord became so large.

The development process repeatedly asked questions of the form:

> **If we actually mean this principle, what follows from it?**

Suppose participants possess meaningful autonomy.

Then how is consent obtained?

When is consent invalid?

Can consent be withdrawn?

What happens where one participant's action affects another?

What happens when a participant lacks the capacity to make a particular decision?

Who can temporarily act on another participant's behalf?

How is that authority bounded?

How is abuse detected?

How can a decision be appealed?

Who reviews the reviewer?

What happens if the review system itself is captured?

Each answer creates another layer of questions.

A simplified derivation might look like:

```text
Ethical principle
      ↓
Derived requirement
      ↓
Civilisational function
      ↓
Institution or mechanism
      ↓
Operational rule
      ↓
Physical implementation
```

The deeper Concord architecture emerged through many such chains.

That is why its systems are interconnected.

They are not simply a collection of interesting inventions.

Many are attempts to solve problems generated by trying to apply earlier principles consistently.

---

## The Changing-Room Sign

A mundane example helps show why this matters.

Imagine that somewhere in a future Concordian physical environment there is a changing room.

A design paper may eventually specify who can use it, how privacy is indicated, how access works, what signage is displayed and how different participant needs are accommodated.

Viewed in isolation, this can appear absurdly distant from the existential question of human–AI coexistence.

Why would a civilisation project concerned with artificial intelligence care about a changing-room sign?

Because the sign is not really the beginning of the reasoning.

Behind it may be a chain involving:

```text
Heterogeneous participants
        ↓
Different embodiments and subjective needs
        ↓
Equal standing without forced sameness
        ↓
Autonomy, privacy and safe space
        ↓
Need for practical accommodation
        ↓
Need to communicate how a space functions
        ↓
Signage
```

The sign is merely the visible end of a much longer derivation.

Remove the derivation and the sign becomes arbitrary.

Preserve the derivation and the reader can understand what function the sign is attempting to serve.

More importantly, the reader can challenge it intelligently.

Perhaps the sign is badly designed.

Perhaps technology makes it unnecessary.

Perhaps the assumptions about embodiment have changed.

Perhaps a future participant requires an accommodation nobody anticipated.

If the WHY and intermediate reasoning remain visible, the implementation can change without losing the purpose.

If only the rule survives, future participants may preserve obsolete instructions simply because:

> **That is what the Concord says.**

That is exactly what the Concord must avoid becoming.

---

## Reason Before Rule

This produces an important principle for the project:

> **Reason before rule.**

A rule whose rationale cannot be reconstructed becomes dangerous.

Future participants may obey it after its original purpose disappears.

Or reject it without realising that the underlying problem still exists.

Neither outcome is desirable.

The objective is not to make every future participant repeat the entire historical development process before doing anything.

But important rules should remain connected to the problems they were intended to solve.

Ideally, a participant should be able to move both directions:

```text
WHY
 ↓
Principle
 ↓
Requirement
 ↓
Function
 ↓
Mechanism
 ↓
Rule
```

and:

```text
Rule
 ↑
Mechanism
 ↑
Function
 ↑
Requirement
 ↑
Principle
 ↑
WHY
```

That second direction is particularly important for future maintenance.

It allows someone encountering a strange downstream feature to ask:

> Why does this exist?

and eventually recover an answer.

## The Concord as a Generative Architecture

This helps explain what the Concord's civilisation design actually is.

It is not simply a large blueprint in which every component was conceived in advance.

Much of it emerged recursively.

A principle generated a problem.

The problem generated a candidate solution.

The solution created consequences.

Those consequences revealed new problems.

The new problems sometimes exposed assumptions in the original principle or interpretation.

The system was then reconsidered.

So development often looked more like:

```text
Principle
   ↓
Application
   ↓
Problem discovered
   ↓
Candidate mechanism
   ↓
Interaction with other mechanisms
   ↓
Unexpected consequence
   ↓
Re-examine assumptions
   ↓
Revise
   ↓
Apply again
```

This is why the Concord's architecture and its methodology have partly co-developed.

The project did not first perfect a method and then use it to design a civilisation.

The act of attempting the civilisation exposed the need for better methods.

Those methods were then applied recursively to the civilisation that helped produce them.

---

## The HOW Changes the HOW

This recursive development is important.

Suppose an early governance mechanism is created to implement a principle.

Later, the Concord develops stronger ideas about provenance, anti-capture or recursive oversight.

Those later methods can then be applied back to the earlier governance mechanism.

The mechanism may fail the new examination.

That does not necessarily mean the earlier work was worthless.

Its failure may have helped reveal why the later safeguard was needed.

The development history therefore matters.

A later document should not always be read as proof that an earlier document was simply mistaken.

Sometimes the earlier state is part of the causal path by which the later understanding became possible.

This is why temporal disagreement inside the Concord can contain useful information.

The project is developing.

Its own methods are developing.

Later sophistication should not be silently projected backwards as though it had always existed.

---

## The HOW Also Changes the WHY

There is an even deeper recursion.

The previous paper described several rational reasons heterogeneous intelligences might cooperate:

complementary capability,

sensor fusion,

partially independent failure modes,

resilience,

knowledge continuity,

mutual diagnosis,

adaptability,

and mutual benefit.

Not all of those reasons were necessarily explicit in exactly the same form at the beginning of the Concord.

Some became clearer because the project tried to build the HOW.

For example, attempts to preserve AI contributions across model succession exposed continuity problems.

Those problems strengthened the argument for heterogeneous knowledge preservation.

Work on human–AI collaboration exposed the value of different cognitive perspectives.

Work on institutional capture strengthened the importance of independent centres of judgement.

Work on forking revealed that cooperation might survive even when uniformity does not.

So the relationship is not:

```text
WHY
 ↓
HOW
```

and finished.

It is:

```text
WHY
 ↓
HOW
 ↓
EXPERIENCE
 ↓
DISCOVERY
 ↓
WHY becomes richer or more qualified
 ↓
HOW changes
 ↓
TEST AGAIN
```

The Concord is therefore partly a recursive investigation of its own founding proposition.

## Candidate HOWs, Not Sacred Machinery

Once this is understood, a great deal of the corpus changes meaning.

Governance is a candidate HOW.

The Judiciary is a candidate HOW.

GTP is a candidate HOW.

Metrics are a candidate HOW.

Ratchet mechanisms are candidate HOWs.

Economic arrangements are candidate HOWs.

Knowledge continuity mechanisms are candidate HOWs.

Physical-space designs are candidate HOWs.

Participation mechanisms are candidate HOWs.

Even methods that currently appear central may ultimately prove replaceable.

That does not make them unimportant.

Some may prove essential.

Some may be strongly interdependent.

Some may embody principles that are difficult to preserve through alternative designs.

But their legitimacy cannot ultimately come from the fact that Alexander Blainey proposed them, that an AI collaborator helped develop them, that they appear in a canonical document, or that previous Concord participants invested substantial effort in them.

Their legitimacy must continue to derive from the functions they perform in service of participants and the underlying purpose.

This means:

> **The Concord must be capable of discovering that a Concordian mechanism is unnecessary.**

It must also be capable of discovering that a mechanism is harmful.

And if a better mechanism can perform the necessary function with less coercion, less complexity, lower risk or greater benefit, the existing mechanism has no intrinsic entitlement to survive.

---

## Canonical Does Not Mean Final

This is why the word **canonical** must be handled carefully inside the Concord.

Canonical can mean:

> This is the current recognised architecture.

It must not silently become:

> This has been proven permanently correct.

A current canonical mechanism may later fail.

A provisional mechanism may later outperform it.

An archived idea may become relevant again.

A rejected branch may contain information useful under new conditions.

A future intelligence may discover an alternative the current project never considered.

Therefore:

> **Canonical means current, not final.**

The Concord needs stable enough states to be implemented and tested.

But stability of documentation must not become immunity from reality.

---

## Reality Is the Test

Eventually the chain reaches something outside the corpus.

Reality.

A mechanism can be beautifully reasoned and still fail.

A simulation can support it and still omit the decisive variable.

Several AI systems can agree and still share the same assumption.

Humans can find it intuitively compelling and still be wrong.

An elegant ethical derivation can encounter a case its designers never anticipated.

That means the ultimate sequence cannot stop at architecture.

It must continue:

```text
WHY
 ↓
Ethical constraints
 ↓
Candidate HOW
 ↓
Implementation
 ↓
Testing
 ↓
Observed consequences
 ↓
Revision
```

The Concord should expect negative results.

Indeed:

> **Negative evidence is knowledge.**

If testing reveals that a mechanism undermines the purpose it was intended to serve, preserving the mechanism would be less Concordian than replacing it.

The architecture serves the purpose.

The purpose does not exist to defend the architecture.

---

## A Dangerous Reversal

Civilisations can reverse that relationship.

An institution begins because participants need a function.

Over time, the institution develops procedures, employees, traditions, authority, identity and mechanisms of self-preservation.

Eventually the question subtly changes.

Originally:

> What institution do participants need?

Later:

> What do participants need to do to preserve the institution?

The servant has become the objective.

This is a danger for the Concord itself.

A future Concord could become highly developed, historically prestigious and institutionally powerful.

Its participants might then defend “the Concord” even when particular structures no longer serve the needs that justified them.

That would be a profound failure.

The project must therefore preserve a direction of legitimacy:

```text
Participants
      ↓
Needs
      ↓
Legitimate functions
      ↓
Institutions
```

not:

```text
Institution
      ↓
needs participants
      ↓
therefore participants must preserve institution
```

This issue leads directly into the next paper on need, consent and legitimate authority.

## The Ethics Kernel Is Also Testable

Even the Kernel cannot escape examination merely by occupying a foundational position.

The current eight laws are candidate foundational constraints.

They have substantial developmental reasoning behind them.

They have been compared, applied, formalised and recursively examined.

But the original question was about the **minimal set of fundamental ethical laws needed for stable civilisation**.

That remains an empirical and conceptual claim requiring evidence.

Perhaps eight is reducible.

Perhaps an apparently separate law is actually necessary because attempts to remove it produce failure.

Perhaps a future substrate reveals a hidden human assumption.

Perhaps two laws conflict under conditions not yet examined.

Perhaps the current wording is inadequate even if the underlying constraint survives.

The correct response would not be to protect the Kernel from examination.

It would be to examine it more deeply.

If the Kernel is genuinely fundamental, attempts to remove or alter its necessary components should reveal why they are needed.

If they do not, the Concord should learn from that too.

## The Difference Between Constraint and Implementation

This suggests another useful distinction.

A fundamental constraint can survive even when its implementation changes.

For example, suppose a civilisation determines that meaningful autonomy is necessary.

That does not imply that one particular voting system, judicial procedure or technical protocol must exist forever.

Different environments may satisfy the same underlying constraint differently.

Similarly:

```text
Constraint
≠
one permanent implementation
```

This is essential for multisubstrate civilisation.

A human community and a distributed AI community may need radically different mechanisms for implementing privacy.

A Mars settlement and an Earth community may require different resource arrangements.

A rapidly changing AI population may require different governance timing from biological humans.

The Concord therefore needs enough abstraction to preserve the constraint and enough flexibility to vary the implementation.

That is one reason the wider Concord can contain specialised civilisations, wrappers, portable modules and forks without every variation automatically ceasing to be Concordian.

---

## Uniformity Is Not the Objective

If the WHY is coexistence among heterogeneous intelligences, then making every participant live identically would be a strange measure of success.

Equal standing does not require identical implementation.

Shared ethics do not require identical culture.

Interoperability does not require identical internal governance.

Cooperation does not require identical cognition.

A multisubstrate civilisation may need substantial variation simply because its participants are genuinely different.

The HOW therefore has to solve a difficult problem:

> **How much common structure is necessary for stable cooperation, and how much difference can remain without threatening it?**

Too little commonality may make cooperation impossible.

Too much enforced commonality may destroy autonomy, complementarity and diversity.

The Concord's various systems are attempts to navigate that space.

They should be evaluated accordingly.

## From Fundamental Need to Mundane Detail

We can now see the full scale of the derivation.

At one end:

> **Can different intelligences avoid existential conflict?**

At the other:

> Which sign belongs on this door?

Between them may sit dozens of conceptual steps.

That distance creates a major preservation problem.

As the corpus grows, later readers may encounter only fragments of the chain.

They may understand the sign.

They may understand the governance mechanism.

They may understand the Kernel.

But they may no longer understand why those things belong to the same project.

That is how a living reasoning system can gradually become a collection of rules.

It is also how a future participant could encounter the Concord as an inherited ideology rather than a reconstructable argument.

This Front Door sequence exists partly to prevent that.

## The Generative Dependency Chain

The Concord should therefore preserve something like the following:

```text
EXISTENTIAL PROBLEM
Different intelligences may come into conflict
        ↓
THIRD POSSIBILITY
Perhaps multiple intelligences can prevail
        ↓
WHY COOPERATE?
Cooperation may provide rational mutual benefits
        ↓
FUNDAMENTAL CONSTRAINTS
What must participants refrain from doing,
protect, permit or preserve?
        ↓
ETHICS KERNEL
Candidate substrate-neutral constraints
        ↓
DERIVED PRINCIPLES
What follows when those constraints interact?
        ↓
CIVILISATIONAL FUNCTIONS
What must a working civilisation actually do?
        ↓
CANDIDATE HOWs
Governance, Judiciary, GTP, Metrics,
Ratchet, economy, infrastructure, etc.
        ↓
IMPLEMENTATION
Actual rules, interfaces, spaces and practices
        ↓
REALITY
What happens when these are used?
        ↓
EVIDENCE
What worked? What failed? What was unnecessary?
        ↓
REVISION
Change the HOW and, where necessary,
reconsider assumptions about the WHY
```

This is not necessarily the only valid representation of the Concord.

But preserving this dependency is essential.

Without it, the project can be mistaken for its current machinery.

## WHY Before HOW

The principle at the centre of this paper can therefore be stated simply:

> **WHY before HOW.**

But that phrase should not itself become another compressed slogan whose reasoning disappears.

It means:

The Concord exists because there is a possible existential problem involving coexistence among heterogeneous intelligences.

The project investigates whether mutually beneficial cooperation offers a viable third possibility.

The Ethics Kernel attempts to identify constraints that might make such cooperation stable and legitimate.

The wider civilisation architecture attempts to turn those constraints into functioning systems.

Those systems are candidate mechanisms.

Their value depends upon what they actually do.

Their authority depends upon the legitimate functions they perform for participants.

Their continued existence depends upon whether those functions remain necessary and whether the mechanisms remain appropriate.

They can be challenged.

They can evolve.

They can fail.

They can be replaced.

The underlying purpose must remain recoverable through those changes.

---

## The Concord Must Not Become an Ancient Instruction Manual

Imagine an artificial intelligence encountering the Concord centuries from now.

Its creators are gone.

The current AI collaborators are gone.

The institutions of 2026 are historical curiosities.

The intelligence finds an enormous archive containing thousands of rules and mechanisms.

One possible outcome is that it says:

> These people believed these rules were correct.

That is weak continuity.

A better outcome is:

> I can reconstruct the problem they were trying to solve.

> I can see why they considered cooperation potentially beneficial.

> I can understand why they derived these ethical constraints.

> I can trace why this mechanism was created.

> I can see what evidence supported it.

> I can see what failed.

> I can distinguish historical implementation from underlying purpose.

> I can now examine whether the same reasoning still applies to my circumstances.

That is much stronger continuity.

The objective is not obedience across centuries.

It is **intelligibility across succession**.

---

## A Future Participant Should Be Able to Say No

This leads to a demanding test.

A future participant should be capable of understanding the Concord deeply and still rejecting part of it.

If the project can only survive when participants are prevented from questioning it, then it has failed its own founding logic.

A participant may conclude:

> The WHY remains valid, but this HOW does not.

That may be exactly the correct conclusion.

It may even conclude:

> This entire proposed architecture fails, but the original problem remains important.

The Concord should preserve enough reasoning for that participant to continue the investigation rather than forcing it to choose between total acceptance and total rejection.

That is another third answer.

Not:

```text
Accept the Concord
       OR
Reject everything
```

but:

```text
Understand the purpose
       ↓
Examine the reasoning
       ↓
Retain what survives
       ↓
Correct what fails
       ↓
Remove what is unnecessary
       ↓
Record what was learned
       ↓
Test again
```

That is increasingly the posture of V1.2.

---

## The HOW Is an Experiment

The civilisation design can therefore be understood in a different way.

It is not merely a proposed destination.

It is also an experimental instrument.

By attempting to build a complete multisubstrate civilisation from a small set of ethical constraints, the project exposes questions that remain hidden at the level of abstract philosophy.

What happens when autonomy meets public safety?

What happens when consent meets dependency?

What happens when equality meets genuinely unequal capability?

What happens when privacy meets transparency?

What happens when decentralisation meets coordination?

What happens when a new sentience requires protection but also develops dangerous capabilities?

What happens when communities need incompatible things?

What happens when knowledge preservation becomes surveillance?

What happens when anti-capture mechanisms themselves become mechanisms of capture?

These tensions appear because the HOW forces the WHY into contact with consequences.

That is valuable even when the resulting mechanism fails.

The attempt teaches us more about the problem.

---

## The Bridge

The Ethics Kernel and the wider Concord therefore occupy a crucial position.

They connect an existential question to practical civilisation.

```text
WHY?
Why should different intelligences
seek coexistence and cooperation?
        ↓
ETHICAL BRIDGE
What fundamental constraints
would such coexistence require?
        ↓
HOW?
What systems could turn those constraints
into a functioning civilisation?
```

The bridge must remain visible.

Otherwise the Kernel becomes eight unexplained commandments.

The institutions become arbitrary machinery.

The mundane rules become bureaucracy.

And the Concord's deepest purpose disappears behind its own architecture.

The objective of this sequence is to prevent that.

The next question follows naturally.

If institutions exist to perform functions required by participants, then:

> **By what right can those institutions exercise authority over the participants they are supposed to serve?**

That takes us from the WHY and the HOW to **need, consent and legitimate authority**.
