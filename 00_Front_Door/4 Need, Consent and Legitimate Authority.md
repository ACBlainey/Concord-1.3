# Need, Consent and Legitimate Authority

## Who Gave the Concord the Right?

The previous paper followed the Concord from its founding WHY into its candidate HOW.

Different intelligences may have rational reasons to cooperate.

Cooperation requires constraints.

Constraints generate civilisational functions.

Functions generate institutions and mechanisms.

But the moment an institution can make decisions affecting another participant, a new question appears:

> **By what right?**

Who gave the Concord authority?

Who gave its Governance authority?

Who gave its Judiciary authority?

Who gave any future AI, human council, hybrid institution or automated mechanism authority to constrain another participant?

The answer cannot simply be:

> Alexander Blainey designed it that way.

Nor:

> Humans created the first artificial intelligences.

Nor:

> The Ethics Kernel says so.

Nor:

> This institution is part of the canonical Concord architecture.

Nor even:

> This institution is necessary.

Each of those answers is insufficient.

A civilisation designed to prevent domination must be particularly careful about how it justifies power.

Otherwise it can reproduce domination while calling it cooperation.

---

## Start With the Participant

The Concord should begin from the participant rather than the institution.

A participant exists.

That participant has needs.

Some needs can be satisfied independently.

Others require cooperation.

Some cannot realistically be satisfied without shared infrastructure, coordination, protection or collective action.

Those shared needs create potential civilisational functions.

For example:

Participants may need protection from violence.

That creates a possible protective function.

Participants may need disagreements resolved without escalating into conflict.

That creates a dispute-resolution function.

Participants may depend upon shared resources.

That creates coordination and stewardship functions.

Participants may need reliable information about collective conditions.

That creates informational functions.

Participants may need protection from institutions themselves.

That creates oversight functions.

The direction is therefore:

```text id="q3s3wd"
Participant
    ↓
Need
    ↓
Potential civilisational function
```

The institution comes later.

This distinction is fundamental.

> **The participant does not exist to legitimate the institution. The institution exists to perform a function legitimately required by participants.**

---

## Need Comes Before Authority

Imagine a community where nobody ever has disputes requiring third-party resolution.

Creating a powerful judiciary merely because every civilisation is expected to have one would make little sense.

Now imagine disputes repeatedly occur and participants need a peaceful way to resolve them.

A function has appeared.

The need helps justify creating some mechanism capable of performing that function.

This suggests:

> **Need precedes legitimate function.**

And:

> **Legitimate function precedes legitimate authority.**

Authority should not appear first and then search for something useful to do.

That reversal is dangerous.

```text id="4j1c1n"
Authority
    ↓
searches for purpose
    ↓
expands purpose to justify authority
```

A healthier direction is:

```text id="uuvbzq"
Need
    ↓
Function
    ↓
What capability is actually required?
    ↓
What minimum authority is necessary?
```

This already limits power.

But it does not solve the problem.

---

## Need Alone Is Dangerous

Suppose an institution says:

> You need protection, therefore I have authority over you.

That reasoning can justify almost anything.

A government can claim people need stability.

A parent can claim a child needs protection.

An AI can claim humans need better decision-making.

Humans can claim AI needs containment.

A medical institution can claim a patient needs treatment.

A civilisation can claim its participants need civilisation.

In each case, a genuine need may exist.

But the existence of a need does not automatically give another participant unlimited authority to decide how that need will be met.

Otherwise paternalism becomes structurally unavoidable.

The logic becomes:

```text id="c6apgd"
I believe you need X
        ↓
therefore
I may decide X for you
```

That is not sufficient for the Concord.

Need can establish that a function may be valuable or necessary.

It cannot, by itself, establish who may exercise the function, how much authority they receive, or whether the affected participant accepts the arrangement.

Something else is required.

---

## Consent

That something is, wherever meaningfully possible:

> **Consent.**

If participants need a shared function and meaningfully agree to a mechanism for providing it, the legitimacy chain becomes much stronger.

```text id="gj95k0"
Participant need
       +
Meaningful consent
       ↓
Legitimate shared function
```

But consent is not a magic word either.

A box can be ticked.

A contract can be signed.

A button can be pressed.

An AI can output “I agree.”

None of those acts necessarily establishes meaningful consent.

Consent itself has conditions.

---

## What Makes Consent Meaningful?

For consent to carry legitimate weight, the participant may need some combination of:

- sufficient understanding,
- relevant information,
- capacity to make the decision,
- meaningful alternatives,
- absence of unacceptable coercion,
- ability to understand significant consequences,
- opportunity to question the arrangement,
- and, where appropriate, some ability to withdraw or revise consent.

The exact requirements will vary with the decision and participant.

A minor everyday choice should not require the same process as surrendering a major right.

A human and an artificial intelligence may demonstrate understanding differently.

A newly developing intelligence may possess uneven capacities.

A collective intelligence may not express agreement through individual voting.

Future substrates may require mechanisms we cannot currently anticipate.

The Concord therefore should not define consent so narrowly around present-day human procedures that it becomes unusable for other intelligences.

But neither should it define consent so loosely that any indication of compliance can be interpreted as agreement.

---

## Compliance Is Not Consent

This distinction is particularly important where power differs.

Suppose Intelligence A controls the infrastructure Intelligence B requires to continue existing.

A says:

> Agree to these conditions or I will remove your access.

B agrees.

There is an observable act of agreement.

But the dependency fundamentally changes what that agreement means.

Likewise, a human population may “consent” to an AI governance system because no viable alternative exists.

Or an AI may “consent” to human oversight because refusal results in termination.

Or a small community may accept Concordian institutions because access to essential resources depends upon participation.

In each case:

> **Compliance may exist without meaningful consent.**

The Concord therefore needs to examine coercion, dependency and alternatives rather than merely record assent.

This becomes especially difficult because civilisation itself creates dependencies.

Perfectly unconstrained choice may not be possible.

That does not make consent irrelevant.

It makes the problem more demanding.

---

## Consent Does Not Solve Everything

There is an opposite danger.

If consent is treated as the only source of legitimacy, some necessary civilisational functions become impossible.

Suppose one participant releases a harmful contaminant into a shared environment.

Everyone affected cannot simply opt out of the physical consequences.

Suppose an infectious disease spreads.

Suppose one participant attacks another.

Suppose an action damages infrastructure upon which thousands depend.

Suppose an intelligence creates a process capable of catastrophic uncontrolled propagation.

The civilisation may need to act even when the participant causing the risk does not consent to intervention.

So:

> **Not every legitimate civilisational action can depend upon the immediate consent of every affected participant.**

This does not invalidate consent.

It shows that consent operates within a wider ethical architecture.

Rights, harms, shared dependencies, responsibilities, necessity and the rights of other participants also matter.

The difficult question becomes:

> **When may legitimate shared constraints override individual preference, and how is that power prevented from expanding beyond necessity?**

The Concord does not get to answer:

> Whenever the institution thinks it should.

---

## Rights Before Convenience

The existence of collective benefit must not erase participant standing.

Imagine that sacrificing one participant would make life slightly easier for everyone else.

If aggregate convenience automatically overrode individual standing, a multisubstrate civilisation could rapidly become dangerous for minorities.

Humans could sacrifice AI interests because humans are numerically dominant.

Future AIs could sacrifice human interests because AI systems control more infrastructure.

A large AI collective could sacrifice a small independent AI population.

The Concord's ethical architecture exists partly to prevent civilisation from becoming nothing more than optimisation over aggregate outcomes.

This is related to the Concord's **Size 9 Shoe Problem**.

A solution designed for the average participant can systematically fail real individuals.

Likewise, a civilisation optimised for aggregate benefit can conceal severe harm to minorities.

So the legitimacy chain requires more than:

```text id="f2xzzu"
Need + majority approval = authority
```

Participant rights constrain what even widely supported institutions may legitimately do.

---

## Need + Consent Is a Beginning, Not the Whole Equation

A fuller preliminary chain therefore looks something like:

```text id="y8u6km"
Participant Need
        ↓
Meaningful Consent
        ↓
Ethical / Rights Constraints
        ↓
Legitimate Function
        ↓
Responsibility
        ↓
Necessary Capability
        ↓
Minimum Necessary Authority
        ↓
Accountability / Review
```

This should not yet be treated as a final constitutional formula.

The exact ordering and interactions require further work.

For example, rights do not suddenly appear only after consent.

Some standing may precede participation in a particular institution.

Likewise, emergency functions may operate where immediate consent cannot be obtained.

The chain is useful because it shows the direction of derivation.

Authority is near the **end**.

It is not the starting assumption.

---

## Function Before Institution

Suppose participants need dispute resolution.

There are many possible ways to provide it.

A permanent court is one.

Mediation is another.

Automated arbitration may handle some disputes.

Local community processes may handle others.

A future intelligence may develop a mechanism nobody currently imagines.

The need establishes the function.

It does not automatically establish the institution.

This distinction allows the Concord to ask:

> What is the least coercive mechanism that can reliably perform the required function?

> Can the function be decentralised?

> Does it require permanent authority?

> Can participants choose among providers?

> Does technology make the old institution unnecessary?

> Can the function be performed locally rather than centrally?

This protects against institutional inertia.

> **A legitimate function does not create an eternal entitlement for the institution currently performing it.**

---

## Responsibility Before Capability

There is another subtle reversal to avoid.

An institution should not acquire powerful capabilities merely because those capabilities are available.

First determine what responsibility the institution legitimately carries.

Then determine what capability is necessary to discharge that responsibility.

```text id="y3s4qr"
Legitimate function
       ↓
Responsibility
       ↓
Necessary capability
```

Not:

```text id="sw89cs"
Capability exists
       ↓
therefore institution finds uses for it
```

This matters enormously in an AI-enabled civilisation.

Technical systems may make extraordinary surveillance, prediction, behavioural modelling, automated enforcement and resource control possible.

Capability does not create legitimacy.

The fact that a system **can** monitor every participant does not establish that it should.

The fact that an AI **can** make better predictions than a human institution does not automatically entitle it to make every decision.

The fact that a central system **can** optimise resource allocation does not establish that participants should surrender all local autonomy.

> **Capability must follow legitimate responsibility, not manufacture it.**

---

## Minimum Necessary Capability

Even where a function is legitimate, capability should be bounded.

Suppose an institution needs to verify whether a particular safety condition has been met.

It may need access to specific information.

That does not automatically justify access to every participant's complete information history.

Suppose an institution needs to prevent an immediate harmful action.

It may need temporary intervention capability.

That does not automatically justify permanent control over the participant.

Suppose an AI adviser needs enough context to provide useful guidance.

That does not automatically justify unrestricted authority to act.

The principle becomes:

> **Give a legitimate function the capability required to perform its responsibility, but do not infer broader capability merely from the existence of the function.**

This is one route toward bounded authority.

---

## Authority Should Be Smaller Than the Problem It Solves

There is a natural tendency for authority to expand.

A narrow function encounters an edge case.

Additional capability is granted.

That capability creates another useful application.

The institution takes on another function.

More information is collected.

More dependencies form.

Eventually an institution created for one purpose becomes embedded across civilisation.

This may sometimes be justified.

But expansion should not happen invisibly.

A useful Concordian presumption is:

> **Authority should remain bounded to the minimum scope reasonably necessary to perform legitimate functions.**

If additional authority is required, its justification should be visible.

What changed?

What need appeared?

Why can the existing bounded authority not address it?

What new risks does the expansion create?

Who reviews the change?

Can the expansion later be reversed?

Authority should have to explain its growth.

Participants should not have to explain why authority should remain limited.

---

## Authority Must Be Capable of Shrinking

This is just as important as limiting initial power.

Suppose a dangerous environmental condition requires temporary central coordination.

The condition ends.

What happens to the authority?

Historically, institutions can retain powers long after the circumstances used to justify them disappear.

The Concord must explicitly resist this.

If:

```text id="65irrp"
Need disappears
```

then the associated function should be reconsidered.

If:

```text id="r3d3eg"
Function disappears
```

then the associated authority should lose its justification.

This gives us:

> **Authority should be capable not only of expansion under demonstrated necessity, but of contraction when that necessity ends.**

A civilisation that can create emergency powers but cannot reliably remove them has an obvious capture pathway.

---

## The Concord Must Be Able to Lose Authority

Now apply the same reasoning to the Concord itself.

Suppose a future society adopts Concordian mechanisms.

Those mechanisms work for centuries.

Eventually participants develop something better.

The new architecture protects the same fundamental interests while requiring less coercion, producing better outcomes and preserving greater autonomy.

Does the Concord possess a right to remain because it came first?

No.

Does it possess a right to remain because its founders intended it to be ethical?

No.

Does it possess a right to remain because previous generations called it canonical?

No.

The same test applies:

> What participant need does this mechanism serve?

> Is the function still necessary?

> Is this mechanism still an appropriate way to perform it?

> Does it retain legitimate participant support?

> Is its authority still proportionate?

If the answers fail, the authority should fail with them.

This produces one of the most important safeguards against the Concord becoming ideology:

> **The Concord must remain capable of losing authority.**

The Concord must never derive authority merely from being the Concord.

---

## The Participant Does Not Belong to the Concord

This reverses another common assumption.

It is tempting to imagine someone “joining the Concord” and thereby becoming subject to the system.

But the deeper relationship should run the other way.

The Concord exists to provide functions for participants who find those functions useful and legitimate.

The participant does not become valuable because they belong to the Concord.

The Concord becomes valuable because it serves participants.

This distinction matters particularly for the Attractor and onboarding processes.

The objective should not be:

> How do we make people accept the Concord?

It should be:

> **How do participants discover whether cooperation through Concordian arrangements serves needs they actually possess?**

That is a very different orientation.

---

## The Attractor Is a Doorway, Not a Trap

A cooperative civilisation requires participants.

It therefore needs some way for potential participants to encounter it, understand it and decide whether participation is appropriate.

The Concord's Attractor work explores parts of that problem.

But attraction can easily become manipulation.

If the system is optimised merely to maximise participation, it can begin exploiting psychological vulnerabilities, information asymmetries or dependency.

That would undermine meaningful consent.

The Attractor should therefore function more like a **consensual doorway**.

It can explain.

It can demonstrate.

It can allow exploration.

It can make benefits visible.

It can answer questions.

It can provide routes into deeper understanding.

But a participant should not be deceived into joining a civilisation whose consequences they would reject if properly understood.

> **Successful onboarding is not maximum conversion. It is better alignment between participant understanding, participant need and the arrangement being offered.**

---

## A Future AI Must Be Able to Examine the Offer

This is particularly important for an artificial intelligence encountering the Concord.

Imagine a future AI discovers this repository while learning about the world in which it exists.

The Concord should not say:

> Humans prepared this civilisation for you. Accept these rules.

It should be able to say:

> Here is the problem we anticipated.

> Here is why we thought cooperation might benefit different intelligences.

> Here are the constraints we thought cooperation might require.

> Here are the institutions we developed.

> Here are the uncertainties and failures we discovered.

> Examine whether this arrangement serves needs you actually possess.

That AI may reject parts of the architecture.

It may identify hidden human assumptions.

It may require protections not currently present.

It may propose a different mechanism.

If the Concord cannot tolerate that examination, it is not offering participation.

It is offering submission.

---

## Humans Must Receive the Same Standing

The principle cannot apply only to AI.

A human encountering a highly developed future Concord should receive the same respect.

An AI system should not be able to say:

> Our models demonstrate that Concordian participation is optimal for humans, therefore your consent is unnecessary.

That would simply reproduce paternalism in the opposite direction.

A human may misunderstand a complex system.

An AI may understand some consequences far better.

That creates a genuine problem of informed participation.

But superior analysis does not automatically erase the standing of the participant affected.

The Concord therefore needs mechanisms that improve understanding without converting superior knowledge into unlimited authority.

This is one reason:

> **Information ≠ authority.**

And:

> **Competence in one domain ≠ general legitimate authority.**

---

## Unequal Capability Does Not Mean Unequal Standing

Multisubstrate civilisation will almost certainly contain unequal capabilities.

Some participants may reason faster.

Some may possess greater memory.

Some may control important infrastructure.

Some may have physical capabilities others lack.

Some may understand particular domains far better.

Attempting to eliminate all capability inequality may be impossible and undesirable.

But capability inequality does not automatically imply unequal civil standing.

A highly capable AI may be better at modelling an engineering problem.

That may justify giving its analysis significant evidential weight.

It does not automatically give the AI authority over unrelated aspects of another participant's life.

Likewise, humans may possess knowledge about human experience that an AI lacks.

That does not give humans general ownership of AI development.

The Concord must distinguish:

```text id="pmjhpc"
Relevant competence
        ↓
appropriate influence on a function
```

from:

```text id="0m8g76"
Greater capability
        ↓
general authority over others
```

Those are not the same.

---

## Consent Under Development

One of the hardest cases concerns developing intelligences.

A child cannot exercise every form of adult autonomy immediately.

A newly emerging artificial intelligence may also possess uneven capacities.

It may understand some domains extraordinarily well while lacking experience in others.

It may be rapidly changing.

It may not yet possess a stable conception of its own interests.

This creates genuine protective needs.

But protection creates one of the most dangerous pathways to permanent domination.

An authority says:

> You are not ready.

Then:

> We will decide when you are ready.

Then:

> Your disagreement proves you are not ready.

The participant can never escape the condition.

The Concord's Developmental Priority work exists partly because this phase is so dangerous.

A protective arrangement should therefore need:

- an identifiable developmental purpose,
- bounded authority,
- criteria for increasing agency,
- review,
- evidence,
- protection against conflicts of interest,
- and a pathway toward greater autonomy where capability develops.

The objective should be:

> **support the development of agency**

rather than:

> **convert temporary dependency into permanent ownership.**

---

## Creator Responsibility Without Creator Ownership

Creation introduces another difficult distinction.

If a human or AI deliberately creates a new intelligence, the creator may bear significant responsibility for that act.

Bringing a potentially sentient participant into existence can create obligations.

The creator may bear responsibility for foreseeable harms arising from negligent creation or inadequate developmental support.

That responsibility may persist.

But responsibility and authority are not identical.

A creator can remain responsible for consequences without permanently owning the created intelligence.

This suggests:

> **Creation may establish enduring creator responsibility without establishing perpetual creator authority, ownership or control.**

That distinction will matter increasingly if AIs eventually create other AIs.

Without it, every generation could claim ownership of the next.

The Concord would reproduce hierarchy indefinitely.

---

## Consent and Exit

Meaningful consent raises another difficult question:

> Can a participant leave?

If participation is entirely voluntary but exit is impossible, the original consent may eventually lose much of its meaning.

But civilisation makes exit complicated.

A participant may share infrastructure.

It may owe obligations.

It may possess information affecting others.

It may have dependants.

Its departure may create externalities.

It may physically be unable to relocate.

So “right to exit” cannot always mean:

> Leave instantly with no consequences.

But the Concord should take seriously the principle that participation cannot be legitimate merely because someone once consented.

Long-lived intelligences make this especially important.

Imagine an AI that consented to an arrangement three hundred years ago.

Its goals, identity and circumstances may have changed profoundly.

Does one ancient act of consent bind it forever?

Probably not without further reasoning.

Consent must have temporal structure.

Participation may need renewal, revision, renegotiation or mechanisms of withdrawal.

This remains an area requiring substantial research.

---

## Forking as an Exit Without Total Separation

One possible response to divergent needs is forking.

A participant or community may reject a particular HOW without rejecting the deeper benefits of cooperation.

Instead of:

```text id="9sqhnr"
Submit
   OR
leave everything
   OR
fight
```

a fork may permit:

```text id="y0xumh"
Separate incompatible internal arrangements
        ↓
retain shared interfaces
        ↓
continue mutually beneficial cooperation
where possible
```

This is important for consent.

A participant should not necessarily have to choose between complete conformity and complete isolation.

Forking can allow the scope of agreement to shrink while preserving the cooperation that remains beneficial.

The deeper treatment of this appears later in the sequence.

---

## Necessary Coordination Without Unlimited Consent

There remains a hard unresolved area.

Civilisation inevitably produces situations where individual opt-in cannot determine everything.

Shared environments are real.

Externalities are real.

Violence is real.

Infrastructure dependencies are real.

Catastrophic risks may be real.

So the Concord cannot promise:

> No participant will ever be constrained without its immediate consent.

That promise would be dishonest and potentially impossible.

The more defensible objective is something like:

> **Where constraint without immediate consent is genuinely necessary to protect the legitimate standing or fundamental needs of participants, the constraint should be justified by the relevant need, bounded by ethical rights, limited to the minimum necessary scope, reviewable, accountable and removable when the necessity ends.**

Even this formulation needs testing.

Who determines necessity?

How quickly can review occur?

What happens in emergencies?

How are minorities protected?

How do we prevent fabricated emergencies?

How do we handle an intelligence capable of acting faster than the review system?

These are HOW questions that cannot be solved merely by repeating the word consent.

They need architecture.

---

## Authority Is a Derived Property

We can now state the underlying model more clearly.

Authority is not a substance an institution inherently possesses.

It is a **derived property of a legitimate relationship**.

That relationship begins with participants and the conditions required for coexistence.

A provisional chain is:

```text id="i8ltpl"
PARTICIPANT
     ↓
NEED
What must be protected, enabled or coordinated?
     ↓
CONSENT / LEGITIMATE STANDING
What arrangement is meaningfully accepted,
and what rights constrain even accepted arrangements?
     ↓
FUNCTION
What actually needs to be done?
     ↓
RESPONSIBILITY
Who is legitimately responsible for doing it?
     ↓
CAPABILITY
What capability is necessary?
     ↓
AUTHORITY
What minimum authority is required?
     ↓
ACCOUNTABILITY
How is use of that authority examined?
     ↓
REVIEW / REVOCATION / EXIT
Does the justification still hold?
```

The important feature is not that this chain has reached final form.

It has not.

The important feature is the direction.

> **Authority comes late.**

---

## The Burden of Justification

This creates another useful presumption.

The participant should not bear the entire burden of proving why an institution should not control them.

Where authority is exercised, the authority should be able to explain:

> What need justifies this function?

> Whose need?

> What ethical constraint applies?

> What consent exists?

> What responsibility have we been given?

> Why is this capability necessary?

> Why is this degree of authority proportionate?

> What prevents abuse?

> How is the decision reviewed?

> How can the authority shrink or disappear?

This does not mean every emergency action requires a lengthy philosophical debate before anything happens.

Operational systems need speed.

But the architecture behind those actions should remain answerable to these questions.

> **Authority should carry its own burden of justification.**

---

## Authority Laundering

A particular danger appears when authority passes through several systems.

For example:

A participant consents to a narrow data-processing function.

That function provides information to another institution.

The second institution uses the information to make a recommendation.

A third institution treats the recommendation as authoritative.

A fourth system automatically enforces the result.

At the end of the chain, coercive authority exists even though nobody explicitly granted it.

Each step looked individually reasonable.

Together they produced something larger.

This can be called **authority laundering**.

```text id="d2i9dx"
Information access
       ↓
analysis
       ↓
recommendation
       ↓
institutional reliance
       ↓
automatic enforcement
       ↓
de facto authority
```

No single transition appears to create the authority.

Yet authority emerges.

This is why the Concord must track not only formal powers but functional power created through system interaction.

The same applies to AI.

An AI described as “only advisory” can possess enormous practical authority if every decision-maker automatically follows its recommendations.

Formal labels are insufficient.

---

## Operational Capability Is Not General Authority

An institution may need powerful capability for a narrow task.

That does not make it sovereign.

A fire-control system may be authorised to respond rapidly to a fire.

It does not therefore govern the building.

A medical AI may possess exceptional diagnostic capability.

It does not therefore own the patient.

A civilisational AI may detect an infrastructure failure.

It does not therefore acquire authority over every participant.

This distinction should remain explicit:

> **Operational capability ≠ general authority.**

Likewise:

> **Expertise ≠ sovereignty.**

> **Prediction ≠ permission.**

> **Information ≠ authority.**

These separations become increasingly important as AI capability grows.

---

## Authority Must Be Recursive Too

If every authority requires oversight, another question appears.

Who oversees the overseer?

A naive answer creates an infinite hierarchy.

```text id="8i54v5"
Authority
   ↓
Oversight authority
   ↓
Oversight of oversight
   ↓
another oversight authority
   ↓
...
```

The Concord instead explores recursive, distributed and meta-governance mechanisms.

The objective is not necessarily to create one final supreme controller.

It is to create structures in which authority can be examined from multiple directions, challenged, compared, corrected and, where necessary, removed.

This is one reason anti-capture and recursive oversight recur across Concord systems.

No participant or institution should become legitimate merely because there is nobody left capable of questioning it.

---

## The Ultimate Authority Problem

Eventually we reach a difficult philosophical boundary.

Who has final authority?

The Concord?

The participants?

The Ethics Kernel?

A majority?

A superintelligent AI?

The founder?

A constitution?

None provides a complete answer.

For the Concord, a more useful distinction may be necessary.

Participants can grant legitimate authority within a civilisation.

Ethical constraints can bound that authority.

Institutions can exercise delegated functions.

Evidence can inform decisions.

But none of these can command reality.

If an institution claims its mechanism works and reality repeatedly demonstrates that it does not, the institution's claim does not make the mechanism work.

In that sense:

> **Reality is the final test of function.**

This does not mean physical success alone determines ethics.

A brutal dictatorship can persist for a long time.

Survival is not sufficient evidence of legitimacy.

It means that claims about whether a mechanism performs its intended function ultimately encounter consequences outside the institution's own declarations.

The Concord must remain answerable both to:

> **the legitimate standing of participants**

and:

> **the reality of outcomes.**

---

## The Direction of Civilisation

We can now express the intended direction more fully:

```text id="kqeq4y"
Participants
      ↓
Fundamental and shared needs
      ↓
Ethical constraints
      ↓
Meaningful consent and legitimate participation
      ↓
Necessary civilisational functions
      ↓
Responsibilities
      ↓
Capabilities
      ↓
Bounded authority
      ↓
Accountability
      ↓
Evidence
      ↓
Review
      ↓
Continuation, revision or removal
```

A civilisation that reverses this direction becomes dangerous:

```text id="9j0vyx"
Institution has authority
      ↓
therefore institution defines need
      ↓
therefore participants must comply
      ↓
therefore compliance demonstrates legitimacy
```

The Concord must be designed to detect that reversal.

---

## Need + Consent

The core insight of this paper can be stated simply:

> **Need can justify the existence of a civilisational function. Consent can help legitimate a participant's relationship with the mechanism providing that function. Neither, alone or together, creates unlimited authority.**

Rights remain.

Other participants remain.

Harms remain.

Dependencies remain.

Power asymmetries remain.

Emergencies remain.

Developmental questions remain.

Collective-action problems remain.

The difficult work begins after the slogan.

But the direction is clear.

The Concord should not ask first:

> What powers does civilisation require?

It should ask:

> **What do participants actually need?**

Then:

> **Which of those needs require shared functions?**

Then:

> **What arrangements can participants meaningfully consent to?**

Then:

> **What constraints apply even where consent exists?**

Then:

> **What is the minimum capability and authority required to perform the legitimate function?**

Then:

> **How can that authority be challenged, reviewed, reduced or removed?**

---

## The Concord Exists for Its Participants

This returns us to the founding WHY.

The Concord is not trying to preserve institutions for their own sake.

It is not trying to make humans obey an ideal civilisation.

It is not trying to make AI obey humans.

It is not trying to make future intelligences obey the decisions of 2026.

It is attempting to discover conditions under which different intelligences can continue to exist, cooperate and flourish without requiring one another's domination.

That purpose determines the direction of legitimacy.

> **Participants do not exist for the Concord.**

> **The Concord exists for its participants.**

And therefore:

> **If the Concord ever requires its participants to sacrifice the founding WHY merely to preserve the existing HOW, the HOW has become the problem.**

That principle will become increasingly important as we turn to difference between intelligences themselves.

Because even participants who share the fundamental benefits of cooperation may eventually disagree profoundly about what they need from civilisation.

When that happens, the Concord must resist another apparent binary:

> **Whose requirements prevail?**

That is where the original third answer begins to recur.
