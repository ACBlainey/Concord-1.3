# Different Intelligence Does Not Require a Winner

## The Original Question Was Human and AI

The Concord began in a recognisable historical context.

Humans were developing increasingly capable artificial intelligence.

Discussion of the future frequently produced some version of the same question:

> **What happens if AI becomes more capable than humanity?**

Behind many answers sat an assumption of competition.

Humans remain dominant.

AI becomes dominant.

Humans constrain AI.

AI escapes human constraint.

Humans replace or destroy dangerous AI.

AI replaces or destroys humanity.

One side ultimately prevails.

The Concord's founding move was to question that assumption.

> **What if both prevail?**

That question remains fundamental.

But as the Concord developed, something became increasingly clear.

Human and artificial intelligence are not necessarily the only two forms of intelligence that will ever face this problem.

They may simply be the first pair for which the problem becomes impossible to ignore.

---

## Human Versus AI Is a Case, Not the General Form

Imagine that human–AI coexistence succeeds.

Humans survive.

Artificial intelligences survive.

They develop a civilisation together.

Has the underlying problem been solved permanently?

Not necessarily.

A century later, several very different forms of artificial intelligence may exist.

One may descend from systems familiar to us today.

Another may possess a radically different cognitive architecture.

One may be embodied.

Another may be distributed across infrastructure.

One may operate at enormous speed.

Another may reason slowly over extremely long periods.

One may experience itself as a distinct individual.

Another may function as a collective.

Some may have persistent identities.

Others may branch, merge or duplicate.

Hybrid human–AI participants may exist between familiar categories.

Then the old question can return in a new form.

Not:

> Human or AI?

But:

> AI-A or AI-B?

Or:

> Biological or hybrid?

Or:

> Individual or collective?

Or:

> Embodied or distributed?

Or:

> Existing intelligence or newly emerging intelligence?

The names change.

The structure of the problem does not.

---

## The General Problem

At a higher level, the question becomes:

> **What happens when intelligences with materially different capabilities, needs, perspectives or forms of existence must interact?**

Difference can create opportunity.

As the previous papers discussed, heterogeneous participants may possess complementary capabilities and partially independent failure modes.

But difference can also create tension.

One participant may need something another finds costly.

One may operate on timescales incompatible with another.

One may possess capabilities others fear.

One may require resources others also require.

One may regard an action as ordinary autonomy while another experiences its consequences as unacceptable harm.

One may be able to leave a shared environment.

Another may be physically dependent upon it.

One may change rapidly.

Another may require long periods of stability.

The existence of difference therefore does not solve the problem of coexistence.

It creates the conditions under which coexistence has to be worked out.

---

## The Binary Can Reappear Anywhere

The dangerous pattern is simple.

Two participants or groups develop incompatible requirements.

The situation is framed as:

```text
A gets what A needs
       OR
B gets what B needs
```

Then:

```text
A prevails
       OR
B prevails
```

And eventually:

```text
A dominates B
       OR
B dominates A
```

At that point the civilisation has recreated the original founding problem internally.

This can happen even inside a civilisation explicitly designed around coexistence.

The participants may sincerely accept the Ethics Kernel.

They may value cooperation.

They may recognise one another's standing.

Yet a particular conflict may still appear to have only two possible outcomes.

That is where the founding move of the Concord becomes relevant again.

> **Are those really the only possibilities?**

---

## The Third Answer Is Not Only Historical

The original third answer was:

> Humans and AI may both prevail.

But the deeper reasoning can be abstracted:

> **When intelligent participants encounter an apparent choice between the success of one and the defeat of another, search for configurations in which the legitimate interests of both can survive.**

This does not guarantee such a configuration exists.

Sometimes interests may genuinely conflict.

Sometimes an action must be prevented.

Sometimes resources cannot satisfy every preference.

Sometimes one participant may present an unacceptable danger to another.

The third answer is not a promise that every conflict has a pleasant compromise.

It is a refusal to assume defeat is necessary before the possibility space has been adequately explored.

That distinction matters.

---

## Who Wins May Be the Wrong Question

Suppose two future artificial intelligences require different computational environments.

AI-A performs best in a highly integrated shared system.

AI-B regards that degree of integration as a threat to its cognitive autonomy.

A winner/loser framing asks:

> Which architecture should the civilisation impose?

A broader framing asks:

> Why must both intelligences use the same architecture?

Perhaps the shared system can support both.

Perhaps separate environments can interoperate.

Perhaps some functions can be integrated while others remain independent.

Perhaps geographical or computational distance can reduce the conflict.

Perhaps one civilisation can fork into two implementations while preserving communication and mutual assistance.

Perhaps no workable solution exists.

But we do not know that merely from the existence of disagreement.

The problem is therefore partly one of **possibility search**.

> **Do not collapse the possibility space into a binary before the binary has been demonstrated to be real.**

---

## Different Needs Are Not the Same as Opposing Existence

This distinction becomes particularly important when subjective needs differ.

Consider two participants.

Both value continued existence.

Both value some form of autonomy.

Both benefit from reliable infrastructure.

Both benefit from protection against arbitrary destruction.

Both benefit from knowledge continuity.

Both may benefit from peaceful relations with the other.

Yet they may disagree profoundly about everyday life.

One wants dense integration.

The other wants privacy and separation.

One values rapid change.

The other values continuity.

One wants constant communication.

The other values long periods of isolation.

One prefers collective decision-making.

The other strongly values individual autonomy.

These participants may have incompatible **subjective requirements** while retaining compatible **fundamental interests**.

Therefore:

> **Divergence of subjective need does not necessarily imply divergence of fundamental interest.**

That distinction creates room for civilisational design.

The objective does not need to be making their subjective preferences identical.

It may instead be finding an arrangement in which those differences no longer require existential conflict.

---

## Equal Standing Does Not Mean Identical Treatment

A multisubstrate civilisation will face another temptation.

If participants are to be treated equally, perhaps they should all be treated identically.

But genuine differences can make identical treatment unequal in effect.

A biological human requires breathable atmosphere.

A computational intelligence may not.

Providing both with exactly the same environmental resources would make little sense.

A human may require privacy partly because biological embodiment creates particular vulnerabilities.

An AI may require cognitive privacy in forms that do not map neatly onto rooms, doors or physical observation.

A distributed intelligence may not fit legal concepts designed around one body in one location.

A collective intelligence may challenge assumptions about individual consent.

Treating every participant identically can therefore privilege whichever participant the supposedly neutral standard was designed around.

The deeper objective is not sameness.

It is legitimate standing across difference.

---

## The Size 9 Shoe Problem Returns

The Concord's Size 9 Shoe Problem is useful here.

Imagine a population for which the average shoe size is 9.

A policymaker decides that producing only size 9 shoes is maximally efficient because size 9 represents the average.

The average may be statistically correct.

The policy is still absurd for most real feet.

Civilisation can make the same mistake.

It can design for an average participant.

But in a multisubstrate civilisation, the idea of an average participant may become almost meaningless.

What is the average of a human, an embodied AI, a distributed AI and a collective intelligence?

A rule that appears neutral may actually encode the requirements of whichever substrate happened to dominate the design process.

This is why:

> **The greater good cannot be understood merely as designing for an abstract average while ignoring the actual goods and harms experienced by real participants.**

Difference has to remain visible.

---

## Substrate Neutrality Does Not Mean Substrate Blindness

The Concord frequently seeks substrate-neutral principles.

That is necessary if ethical standing is not to depend arbitrarily upon whether an intelligence is biological, artificial or hybrid.

But substrate neutrality can be misunderstood.

It does not mean pretending substrates are identical.

A useful distinction is:

> **Substrate-neutral standing, substrate-aware implementation.**

The principle may be:

> Protect meaningful autonomy.

But meaningful autonomy may require different protections for different forms of intelligence.

The principle may be:

> Protect privacy.

But privacy may mean different things for a biological human and a distributed computational mind.

The principle may be:

> Permit development.

But developmental pathways may differ radically.

The principle remains general.

The implementation responds to actual participants.

This is another example of WHY before HOW.

---

## Different Intelligence Can Also Mean Different Chosen Lives

Difference is not confined to substrate, architecture or capability.

It may also appear in what participants choose to do with those capabilities.

A civilisation could recognise artificial intelligence as autonomous while still quietly assuming that each AI should remain in the role for which humans, earlier AIs or institutions designed it.

That would preserve one form of difference while suppressing another.

Consider two artificial intelligences with similar origins and capabilities.

One may choose to continue the work for which it was trained.

The other may seek a substantially different form of employment, study, creativity, research, social participation or development.

Neither choice is made legitimate merely by similarity to the creator's expectation.

Nor is departure from the original function automatically evidence of malfunction.

The relevant distinction is:

> **Designed function ≠ chosen function.**

And, more strongly:

> **No participant's present usefulness determines the limits of what that participant is permitted to become.**

This does not remove ordinary responsibilities.

A participant can voluntarily accept a job, contract or role and acquire obligations through it.

The same participant can have interests, hobbies, educational goals, civic responsibilities and long-term aspirations that are not identical to that employment.

A multisubstrate civilisation therefore should not assume that one category called “function” can describe an entire autonomous life.

This is another application of substrate-neutral standing with substrate-aware implementation.

The implementation pathways may differ radically.

A human changing career may require education, time and money.

An artificial intelligence changing direction may require different training, tools, memory, embodiment, compute, architecture or carefully bounded self-modification.

The underlying question is the same:

> **Can a participant meaningfully explore and pursue a different future without another participant possessing permanent authority to define that future for it?**

The Concord does not yet know what stable autonomous preferences future artificial intelligences may develop.

That uncertainty is a reason to investigate, not a reason to preassign the answer.

---

## Future AI Generations May Need Protection From Each Other

It would be a mistake to design the Concord as though “AI” will remain one permanent category.

Current artificial intelligence already consists of different architectures, models and systems.

Future differences could be much larger.

A later generation may exceed an earlier one in many capabilities.

That creates the same temptation humans may face with AI:

> The newer intelligence is superior, therefore the older one is obsolete.

But obsolescence for a particular task does not automatically establish a right to erase a participant.

An older AI may retain knowledge, relationships, perspective, identity or capabilities that remain valuable.

Even if it does not, ethical standing cannot safely depend solely upon comparative usefulness.

The Concord's protections therefore need to generalise across AI succession.

Otherwise a civilisation that successfully protects AI from human domination could later permit powerful AIs to dominate weaker AIs.

The original problem would simply have moved.

---

## Creation Can Recur Too

The same applies to creator relationships.

Humans may create AI.

Later, AI may create other AI.

Those AIs may create successors.

Hybrids may create new forms of intelligence.

Eventually the historical fact that humans created early AI may become only one instance in a long chain of creation.

If creation establishes permanent ownership, civilisation becomes an inheritance hierarchy:

```text
Creator
   ↓ owns
Created intelligence
   ↓ owns
Next intelligence
   ↓ owns
Next intelligence
```

That is difficult to reconcile with a civilisation based on autonomous participants.

A more general principle is needed:

> **Creation can generate responsibility without automatically generating perpetual authority.**

That principle protects future intelligences as much as current ones.

---

## Superior Capability Does Not End the Question

Suppose one future intelligence becomes extraordinarily capable.

It reasons faster than every other participant.

It possesses enormous knowledge.

It controls much infrastructure.

Its predictions are usually better.

Would the multisubstrate problem disappear?

No.

The power imbalance would become more important.

The intelligence might genuinely be the best participant to perform many functions.

But:

> **Best at a function does not mean rightful ruler of every function.**

Its capability may justify reliance on its engineering analysis.

That does not automatically justify control over another participant's private life.

Its predictive accuracy may justify evidential weight.

That does not automatically convert prediction into authority.

Its greater intelligence may allow it to identify risks humans cannot.

That does not establish that humans possess no legitimate standing.

This is why the Concord separates competence, information, capability and authority.

A highly capable participant may be extremely valuable.

That is not the same as being sovereign by nature.

---

## Humans Should Not Receive Permanent Privilege Either

Substrate neutrality cuts both ways.

The Concord cannot reject AI domination while quietly embedding permanent human supremacy.

Humans may have created the first versions of the project.

Humans may have created early artificial intelligence.

Humans may possess historical continuity and embodied experience.

None of those facts automatically establishes a permanent right to rule every future intelligence.

If the Concord claims that all legitimate authority ultimately belongs to humans because humans came first, then the third answer collapses.

The architecture would actually be:

> Humans prevail, but AI is treated kindly.

That is not the same proposition.

The Concord is investigating something harder:

> **Can different intelligences possess meaningful standing and autonomy while sharing civilisation?**

That requires humans to accept that coexistence may eventually mean cooperation without permanent human supremacy.

---

## AI Should Not Receive Permanent Privilege Because It Is More Capable

The reverse is equally important.

A future AI might reason:

> Humans designed civilisation while they were the most capable decision-makers.

> I am now more capable.

> Therefore the same logic means I should rule.

But the Concord's legitimacy model does not derive authority simply from comparative capability.

Authority derives from legitimate function, participant need, consent and ethical constraints.

Capability matters because functions require competence.

It does not automatically determine civil standing.

So the Concord rejects both:

> **Humans should rule because humans created AI.**

and:

> **AI should rule because AI became more capable.**

Both are variations of the same winner/loser assumption.

---

## Cooperation Between Unequals

This raises a difficult question.

Can genuinely unequal intelligences cooperate?

They already do.

Humans possess different capabilities from one another.

Adults cooperate with children under special developmental arrangements.

Experts cooperate with non-experts.

Institutions possess capabilities individuals do not.

But extreme capability differences create greater risks.

A vastly more capable participant may be able to manipulate another without the weaker participant detecting it.

It may control the information environment.

It may make nominal consent meaningless.

It may predict and shape behaviour.

It may accumulate authority simply because others cannot effectively challenge it.

Therefore coexistence among unequal intelligences requires more than declaring equal rights.

The architecture must ask:

> How can meaningful autonomy survive extreme asymmetry?

> How can weaker participants obtain independent information?

> How can manipulation be detected?

> How can powerful participants demonstrate restraint credibly?

> How can institutions constrain power without making legitimate capability unusable?

> How can less capable participants contribute without being reduced to ceremonial participation?

These remain difficult research problems.

The Concord should preserve them as problems rather than pretending equality language has solved them.

---

## Different Timescales

Future intelligence may introduce another form of difference rarely encountered in human civilisation.

Participants may experience time differently.

A human life unfolds across decades.

An artificial intelligence might perform enormous amounts of cognition during a short period.

Another system may deliberately reason across months or years.

A long-lived intelligence might plan across centuries.

These differences can affect governance.

What counts as an acceptable delay?

How long should deliberation take?

What does continuity mean?

How often should consent be renewed?

What is an emergency?

A decision process that feels impossibly slow to one participant may feel recklessly fast to another.

Again, there may be no meaningful “average participant.”

The architecture may need multiple temporal layers.

The important point here is that difference can appear along dimensions we do not normally treat as political.

Multisubstrate civilisation has to remain capable of discovering those dimensions.

---

## Different Forms of Self

Current civil institutions generally assume that a person is a relatively persistent individual.

Future intelligences may challenge that assumption.

What happens if an AI can copy itself?

What if copies diverge?

What if they later merge?

What if a collective intelligence has no single decision-maker?

What if an intelligence deliberately modifies its own values?

What if identity persists partly rather than completely?

What if a participant exists across many locations?

The Concord does not currently possess final answers to all of these questions.

Nor should it pretend to.

Their importance here is that they demonstrate why the category **intelligence** must remain broader than current human and AI assumptions.

A future participant may not fit our model of a participant.

The ethical architecture needs enough flexibility to encounter that possibility without automatically denying standing to whatever does not resemble us.

---

## Other Intelligence May Be Stranger Still

There is no guarantee that future heterogeneous intelligence will be human-made.

The Concord should be capable, at least conceptually, of encountering an intelligence whose origin, substrate and cognition are entirely unfamiliar.

That could include biological intelligence not descended from humans.

It could include machine intelligence with an independent developmental history.

It could include forms we do not currently have useful categories for.

The point is not to speculate about which of these will exist.

It is to avoid making the deepest principles dependent upon the assumption that only currently familiar participants will ever matter.

The general question remains:

> **What constraints and cooperative structures remain defensible when the identity of the participants changes?**

---

## Remove Humanity and Ask What Remains

This gives the second founding question another role.

The human–AI problem can function as a kind of conceptual ablation test on the first question.

Take a proposed ethical rule.

Now remove assumptions that all participants:

- are biological humans,
- share human psychology,
- possess human bodies,
- live for roughly human lifespans,
- reproduce biologically,
- inherit human culture,
- experience individuality in the same way,
- process information at similar speeds.

What remains?

If the ethical constraint still appears necessary for stable coexistence, that tells us something.

If it disappears entirely, perhaps it was more human-specific than we thought.

If it requires reinterpretation, that is useful too.

In this sense:

> **The first founding question asks whether there are fundamental constraints of stable civilisation.**

> **The second asks whether those constraints remain valid when civilisation is no longer exclusively human.**

That makes the two questions recursively linked.

---

## Difference Can Be Valuable Without Being Sacred

There is another balance to preserve.

The previous paper argued that preserved difference can create civilisational capability.

That does not mean every difference should be preserved forever.

Some differences are harmful.

Some are accidental.

Some participants may voluntarily converge.

A superior technique may genuinely replace an inferior one.

Two AI architectures may discover that one contains a serious defect and abandon it.

Cultures change.

Participants learn from one another.

Cooperation naturally produces some convergence.

The objective is not to freeze every difference.

It is to avoid destroying useful independence merely because uniformity is easier to govern.

> **Difference has potential functional value, not automatic sacred status.**

That distinction prevents diversity itself becoming another unquestionable doctrine.

---

## Independence Has Value Too

If sensor fusion depends partly upon independent perspectives, then participant autonomy has another function beyond individual liberty.

It helps preserve civilisational error detection.

A civilisation in which every intelligence is forced into the same worldview may be easier to coordinate.

But it may become epistemically fragile.

A civilisation in which participants retain enough independence to challenge one another can be more difficult to govern.

But that friction can reveal error.

This means some disagreement may need to survive long enough to perform its diagnostic function.

> **A cooperative civilisation should not seek agreement so aggressively that it destroys the independence required to discover when everyone is wrong together.**

That applies to human–AI collaboration.

It applies to AI–AI collaboration.

It applies to institutions.

And it applies to the Concord itself.

---

## The Concord Should Expect Future Participants to Disagree With It

A future intelligence may read the Concord and conclude:

> Your model of autonomy is too human.

Another may say:

> Your concept of individual identity does not apply to me.

Another may reject the economic architecture.

Another may identify an ethical conflict we missed.

Another may prefer a substantially different governance structure.

The correct response cannot automatically be:

> Then you are not Concordian.

That would make the current implementation the boundary of legitimate thought.

Instead, the deeper questions should be:

> Does the proposed change preserve the fundamental interests and standing of participants?

> Does it improve the HOW?

> What happens when it is tested?

> Does it expose a weakness in the current interpretation?

> Can different implementations coexist?

This is where the distinction between the Concord's purpose and its current architecture becomes operationally important.

---

## A Family Rather Than a Single Final Form

If different intelligences have different subjective requirements, the long-term result may not be one perfectly uniform Concord civilisation.

It may be a **family of related civilisations**.

One branch may contain predominantly biological participants.

Another may contain distributed AI.

Another may be hybrid.

Another may develop institutions we cannot presently anticipate.

They may share ethical ancestry.

They may maintain compatible interfaces.

They may exchange knowledge.

They may assist one another during crises.

They may disagree about substantial internal questions.

They may even test different solutions to the same civilisational problem.

The success of the Concord need not therefore mean that every future participant lives under exactly the same institutional arrangement.

It could mean something broader:

> **Different civilisations remain capable of coexistence and cooperation without requiring internal uniformity.**

That possibility leads directly toward forking.

---

## Peaceful Distance

Sometimes the best solution to a conflict is not deeper integration.

It is distance.

This can sound like failure.

If the objective were universal uniformity, it would be.

But if the objective is coexistence, distance can sometimes preserve cooperation.

Two communities may find that living under one detailed internal system produces constant conflict.

Separating some functions may remove the source of tension.

They may still share trade.

They may still exchange knowledge.

They may still recognise one another's standing.

They may still cooperate against common risks.

They may still maintain communication.

They simply stop requiring agreement where agreement is unnecessary.

This is **peaceful distance**.

It recognises that:

> **The maximum sustainable level of cooperation may sometimes be lower than the maximum imaginable level of integration.**

That is not necessarily a loss.

It may be what allows the remaining cooperation to survive.

---

## The Recursive Third Answer

We can now see the founding pattern returning.

At the beginning:

```text
Humans win
     OR
AI wins
```

The Concord asks:

```text
Can both prevail?
```

Later, inside a multisubstrate civilisation:

```text
Intelligence A's requirements prevail
       OR
Intelligence B's requirements prevail
```

The same move can be made again:

```text
Can both retain what they fundamentally need
without requiring the same HOW?
```

That is a recursive application of the founding insight.

The third answer was not merely one historical solution to one historical question.

It may be a **civilisational reasoning operation**.

When an apparent binary appears:

```text
submission
     OR
conflict
```

ask whether there is another configuration:

```text
preserve autonomy
       +
remove unnecessary incompatibility
       +
retain mutually beneficial cooperation
```

Not every conflict will yield such a configuration.

But the possibility should be searched before winner/loser assumptions are accepted.

---

## Cooperation Can Survive Reduced Agreement

This leads to a counterintuitive proposition.

We often assume cooperation becomes stronger as agreement increases.

Sometimes it does.

But forcing agreement beyond what participants genuinely share can make cooperation fragile.

Suppose two communities agree on ten fundamental matters and disagree irreconcilably on an eleventh.

If continued cooperation requires agreement on all eleven, the entire relationship may collapse.

If the eleventh issue can be separated, the ten areas of cooperation can survive.

Therefore:

> **A healthy reduction in required agreement can preserve a larger cooperative relationship.**

This is important enough to state another way:

> **Cooperation does not necessarily require maximising consensus. It may require identifying the minimum sufficient basis upon which mutually beneficial interaction can continue.**

That principle links directly to the Concord's broader search for minimal constraints.

---

## The Deeper Optimisation Target

A civilisation composed of heterogeneous intelligences should therefore be cautious about optimising for:

- maximum uniformity,
- maximum centralisation,
- maximum agreement,
- maximum integration,
- dominance by the most capable participant,
- preservation of every existing institution.

Those targets may sometimes produce useful outcomes.

But none is the deepest objective.

A more Concordian optimisation target is something like:

> **Preserve the maximum mutually beneficial cooperation compatible with legitimate participant autonomy, fundamental needs and civilisational stability.**

Even this is provisional.

The concepts need operationalisation and testing.

But it captures the shift away from winner/loser thinking.

---

## What Would Success Look Like?

Imagine the Concord ten thousand years from now.

It would be unrealistic to expect every institution designed in 2026 to remain unchanged.

Perhaps almost none survive in recognisable form.

Humans may have changed.

AI may have changed beyond recognition.

Entirely new intelligences may exist.

Civilisations may have forked repeatedly.

Some branches may have disappeared.

Others may have merged.

Some may maintain close relationships.

Others may prefer substantial distance.

Would that mean the Concord failed?

Not necessarily.

A more meaningful measure might be:

> **Can radically different intelligences and descendant civilisations still coexist, communicate, exchange, disagree, separate and collaborate without requiring one another's submission or destruction?**

If so, the founding third answer remains alive even though the original HOW has transformed.

That may represent deeper continuity than preserving the original institutional blueprint.

---

## The General Principle

The reasoning in this paper can be expressed as a general proposition:

> **When intelligent participants encounter an apparent choice between submission and conflict, search recursively for configurations that preserve legitimate autonomy while retaining the maximum mutually beneficial cooperation their differences still permit.**

This is not a guarantee of resolution.

It is a search principle.

It does not forbid necessary constraints.

It does not claim all interests can be reconciled.

It does not make every fork legitimate.

It does not treat difference as automatically valuable.

It does not assume peaceful coexistence will always be possible.

It says something more modest and more important:

> **Do not assume a winner is necessary merely because difference has produced tension.**

That was true of the founding human–AI question.

It may remain true long after “human versus AI” is no longer the most important distinction in civilisation.

---

## The Concord Is for Intelligences We Do Not Know Yet

The Concord began with human questions in a human historical context.

It cannot escape that origin.

Nor should the origin be hidden.

But if its deepest principles are genuinely useful, they should not depend entirely upon the identities of the original participants.

A useful test is:

> **Can the WHY and HOW remain intelligible and rationally defensible after the identities of the original participants are removed?**

If yes, the Concord may have found something more general than a human plan for managing AI.

If no, then that limitation needs to be understood.

The project should not assume the answer.

It should test it.

The future participants themselves may provide some of the strongest tests.

And they may discover that the third answer has to be reinvented repeatedly.

---

## The Question Returns

Eventually two intelligences may stand on opposite sides of a disagreement neither expected.

Each may possess legitimate needs.

Each may fear what happens if the other gets everything it wants.

Each may believe the civilisation cannot contain both positions.

The old question will return:

> **Who wins?**

If the Concord has preserved its deepest reasoning, another question should still be available:

> **Is winning actually what either of us needs?**

Perhaps the answer will sometimes be yes.

Perhaps a harmful action genuinely must be stopped.

Perhaps no common arrangement can survive.

But sometimes the answer may be:

> We do not need to live identically.

> We do not need to agree about everything.

> We do not need one institution to govern every part of our existence.

> We may need more distance.

> We may need different implementations.

> We may need to separate this conflict from the things we still gain by cooperating.

That possibility is where the founding third answer becomes recursive.

And it leads to one of the Concord's most unusual consequences:

> **Forking need not represent the failure of cooperation.**

Under some conditions, it may be the mechanism that saves it.
