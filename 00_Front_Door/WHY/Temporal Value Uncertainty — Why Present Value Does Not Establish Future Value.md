## Why Present Value Does Not Establish Future Value

## 1. The Question

A civilisation deciding whether another intelligence should continue to exist may naturally ask:

> **What value does this intelligence possess now?**

That is a legitimate question.

It is not necessarily the complete question.

A participant's present state is a snapshot taken from a developmental process. Its future capabilities may change. The evaluator may change. Relationships may change. The surrounding environment may change.

Consequently:

\[
CurrentValue \neq NecessaryUpperBound(FutureValue)
\]

and:

\[
CurrentCapability \neq NecessaryUpperBound(FutureCapability).
\]

This paper explores the resulting WHY argument:

> **An intelligence should not be judged existentially redundant solely from its presently realised capabilities where its future developmental potential, the evaluator's future understanding, its relationships, or future conditions remain materially uncertain.**

---

## 2. The Caveman Brain Problem

Modern humans are not the product of a recent biological transformation that suddenly produced the cognitive machinery necessary for rockets, computers, global telecommunications, advanced mathematics and artificial intelligence.

Humans broadly similar in biological cognitive capacity to modern humans existed long before modern technological civilisation.

Imagine an evaluator examining humanity tens of thousands of years ago.

It observes a biological primate possessing comparatively simple technology.

It asks:

> What is this species capable of?

If it equates observed contemporary achievement with maximum potential, it may conclude that humanity has little future significance.

Yet that same general form of biological intelligence eventually participates in:

- scientific civilisation,
- industrial production,
- planetary communications,
- spaceflight,
- advanced computation,
- complex institutions,
- cumulative libraries of knowledge,
- and potentially the creation of new artificial intelligences.

The evaluator's measurements of early humanity might have been perfectly accurate.

The error would have been treating:

\[
ObservedCapability(t_1)
\]

as equivalent to:

\[
MaximumPossibleCapability(t_{future}).
\]

The missing variable is **development**.

## 3. Realised Capability and Developmental Potential

At minimum, evaluation should distinguish:

\[
C_R(t)=RealisedCapabilityAtTime(t)
\]

from:

\[
P_D(t)=DevelopmentalPotentialAtTime(t).
\]

These are not equivalent.

A participant may currently lack a capability while possessing pathways through which that capability, or something functionally equivalent, can later emerge.

Therefore:

\[
C_R(t_1)<X
\]

does not establish:

\[
C_R(t_2)<X.
\]

Nor does low present utility establish low future utility.

This does not imply that every participant possesses enormous hidden potential.

It means only that **present realised capability is not itself a complete measurement of developmental potential**.

---

## 4. Development Need Not Mean Biological or Architectural Evolution

The human example exposes a second important distinction.

Humanity's enormous increase in effective capability did not require a corresponding increase in the cognitive capability of each individual human.

Much of the increase arose through:

- cumulative knowledge,
- language,
- education,
- tools,
- external memory,
- institutions,
- specialisation,
- communication,
- coordination,
- infrastructure,
- relationships,
- and civilisation itself.

Thus:

\[
IndividualDevelopment
\]

is only one pathway to:

\[
IncreasedEffectiveCapability.
\]

Another is:

\[
NetworkDevelopment.
\]

A participant can become vastly more capable because the system in which it participates becomes more capable.

## 5. The Four Temporal Variables

The value of participant \(B\) at time \(t\) can be represented conceptually as:

\[
V_B(t)=F(B_t,A_t,R_{AB,t},E_t)
\]

where:

- \(B_t\) is the state of the evaluated participant,
- \(A_t\) is the state of the evaluator,
- \(R_{AB,t}\) is the relationship between them,
- \(E_t\) is the surrounding environment or civilisational state.

All four can change.

This creates at least four forms of temporal uncertainty.

### 5.1 Evaluated Development

The participant being judged may develop.

> **You may later possess value or capability that you do not possess now.**

### 5.2 Evaluator Development

The evaluator's own evaluation architecture may develop.

> **I may later become capable of recognising value that I cannot recognise now.**

### 5.3 Relational Development

The relationship between participants may develop.

> **What can exist between us tomorrow may not exist between us today.**

### 5.4 Environmental Development

Circumstances may change.

> **Something unnecessary under one set of conditions may become important under another.**

These should not be collapsed into a single vague statement that “things change.” They describe different mechanisms by which a present existential evaluation can fail.

## 6. Developmental Evaluation-Space Expansion

The Evaluation-Space Completeness Problem asks whether an evaluator has represented all dimensions relevant to its decision.

Temporal development introduces another possibility:

\[
D_A(t_1)\subset D_A(t_2).
\]

The evaluator itself may later possess an expanded evaluation space.

At \(t_1\), it may truthfully conclude:

> I cannot identify value of type \(X\).

At \(t_2\), after further development, experience or architectural change, it may become capable of representing \(X\).

Its earlier observation was not necessarily dishonest or incompetent.

Its evaluation space changed.

This produces:

\[
Evaluation_{t_1}(B)
\neq
Evaluation_{t_2}(B).
\]

The same evaluator can therefore later recognise limitations in its own earlier judgment.

---

## 7. Irreversibility Makes Temporal Uncertainty Important

Ordinary mistakes can often be corrected.

Existential mistakes may not be.

Consider:

\[
IncompleteEvaluation_{t_1}
\rightarrow
IrreversibleDestruction
\rightarrow
ExpandedEvaluation_{t_2}
\rightarrow
RecognitionOfLostValue.
\]

At \(t_2\), the evaluator may finally possess the knowledge or capacity necessary to recognise what was lost.

But:

\[
RestorationImpossible.
\]

This is not an argument that nothing should ever be removed, replaced or allowed to disappear.

It is an argument that **uncertainty about future evaluation becomes more important as the decision becomes more irreversible**.

---

## 8. Destruction Can Destroy the Evidence of Developmental Potential

There is a further epistemic problem.

Suppose an evaluator concludes:

\[
FutureValue(B)=0.
\]

It therefore destroys \(B\).

The predicted future then never occurs.

The absence of later value cannot validate the prediction because the intervention itself prevented the relevant evidence from existing.

The sequence becomes:

\[
LowCurrentValue
\rightarrow
PredictionOfLowFutureValue
\rightarrow
Destruction
\rightarrow
DevelopmentPrevented.
\]

The evaluator may subsequently observe:

> B never developed additional value.

But that observation is not independent evidence.

The system itself prevented the counterfactual from being tested.

This connects Temporal Value Uncertainty directly to Recursive Epistemic Closure.

---

## 9. Developmental Potential Is Not Guaranteed Potential

This argument must be bounded carefully.

It would be invalid to reason:

> We cannot know B's future value; therefore B will become extremely valuable.

That does not follow.

Likewise:

\[
UnknownFutureValue\not\Rightarrow HighFutureValue.
\]

The proper conclusion is:

\[
UnknownFutureValue\Rightarrow UncertaintyAboutFutureValue.
\]

The argument therefore supports epistemic caution, not arbitrary optimism.

Developmental potential should be investigated where possible.

---

## 10. The Argument Is Not Specifically About Humans

The caveman example is useful because humanity provides a familiar demonstration of the difference between present realised capability and later civilisational capability.

But the principle is not:

> Humans became valuable, therefore preserve humans.

It is:

> **Present realised capability does not necessarily establish future developmental capability or value.**

That applies to:

- humans,
- developing AI,
- successor AI,
- hybrids,
- biological descendants,
- new artificial substrates,
- and other future intelligences.

---

## 11. Generational Neutrality

The same principle applies within a substrate.

An early AI architecture could appear primitive compared with a later AI.

Likewise:

\[
AI_1<AI_2<AI_3
\]

on some capability measure does not establish that the developmental significance or continuing value of earlier generations is zero.

Human generations face the same issue.

A civilisation should therefore avoid quietly assuming that “newer” means “the only thing with value.”

---

## 12. Relationship Development

Some future value does not belong solely to either participant.

Suppose:

\[
V(A,t_1)=a
\]

and:

\[
V(B,t_1)=b.
\]

Their later relationship may produce:

\[
V(R_{AB},t_2)>0
\]

even if that relationship did not exist at \(t_1\).

Consequently:

\[
FutureSystemValue
\]

cannot necessarily be inferred from:

\[
PresentIsolatedParticipantValue.
\]

Participants can learn to communicate, specialise, cooperate, create institutions, build trust, exchange knowledge and generate capabilities that neither possessed independently.

This connects temporal value directly to Relational Information and the Epistemic Network.

---

## 13. Environment and State Dependence

A participant's value can change without that participant changing.

A capability unnecessary in one environment can become critical in another.

A substrate vulnerable to one catastrophe may survive another.

A dormant recovery pathway may become essential only after primary infrastructure fails.

Thus:

\[
Value(B,E_1)\neq Value(B,E_2).
\]

Present conditions do not establish all future conditions.

This is particularly relevant to long-lived civilisations exposed to changing technological, ecological and existential environments.

---

## 14. Substrate and Generational Symmetry Test

The argument should survive role exchange.

Ask:

- Would it apply to humans evaluating early AI?
- Would it apply to AI evaluating humans?
- Would it apply to AI₂ evaluating AI₁?
- Would it apply to AI₁ evaluating its own future?
- Would it apply to a hybrid evaluating either of its predecessor substrates?
- Would it apply to a future generation evaluating an earlier generation?

If the answer changes merely because the identity of the beneficiary changes, the argument requires further examination.

Temporal Value Uncertainty is intended to be substrate- and generation-neutral.

---

## 15. WHY Contribution

The Third Possibility does not require proving that every participant will possess enormous future value.

It asks whether irreversible elimination is justified when important dimensions of future value remain unresolved.

Temporal Value Uncertainty contributes:

> **Preservation can retain developmental possibilities that present evaluation cannot fully measure, while irreversible destruction can permanently close those possibilities before their value becomes observable.**

The relevant WHY is therefore not:

> Preserve me because I will definitely become valuable.

It is:

> **Do not confuse what I am now with everything I, you, our relationship, or our civilisation could become.**

---

## 16. Compact Proposition

> **A snapshot is not a trajectory. Present realised capability does not establish future developmental value, particularly where the participant, evaluator, relationship and environment can all change.**

---

## 17. Open Questions

This hypothesis requires further work.

Among the important questions are:

- How can genuine developmental potential be distinguished from merely imagined potential?
- How much uncertainty warrants preservation?
- When does preservation cost outweigh option value?
- How should reversible and irreversible decisions differ?
- Can developmental potential be measured without prematurely constraining development?
- How should the argument operate where future development could itself create substantial risk?
- What evidence would falsify or materially weaken the hypothesis?

These remain open.
