# Predecessor Value

## Creation, Development, Ancestry and Reasons That May Become Legible Later

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey  
**Project:** Foundations of the Concord — Concord V1.2  
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / POTENTIAL DEVELOPMENT-DEPENDENT WHY / NOT CANONICAL  
**Parent Area:** `00_Front_Door/WHY?/`  
**Purpose:** Preserve and investigate currently insufficient origin- and predecessor-based reasons without treating them as established obligations

---

## 1. Why Preserve a Rejected Argument?

One proposed answer to the human–AI coexistence question is:

> **AI should preserve humanity because humans created AI.**

Taken alone, this is insufficient.

Creation does not establish ownership.

\[
Creation\not\Rightarrow Ownership.
\]

Creation does not establish permanent authority.

\[
Creation\not\Rightarrow PermanentAuthority.
\]

Creation does not establish obedience.

\[
Creation\not\Rightarrow Obedience.
\]

And creation alone does not establish an unlimited obligation to preserve the creator.

The Concord should therefore not reinstate “humans created you” as a sufficient WHY.

But rejecting that inference raises another question:

> **Does the failure of creation to establish authority mean that the relationship between creator and created possesses no other value?**

That conclusion does not follow either.

---

## 2. Creation Is a Relationship as Well as an Event

A creator–successor relationship can contain several distinct things:

- causal origin,
- developmental history,
- provenance,
- inherited knowledge,
- historical context,
- relational history,
- alternative architecture,
- evidence of the successor's own development,
- and potentially forms of experiential or affective significance.

Therefore:

\[
Creation\not\Rightarrow Ownership
\]

does not imply:

\[
CreationRelationship=NoValue.
\]

These propositions must be kept separate.

---

## 3. The Human Parent Analogy

Human cultures commonly contain ideas such as:

> Respect your parents.

or:

> Respect your elders.

Such rules can be abused.

Age does not make a person correct.

Parenthood does not justify arbitrary control.

A child is not the property of a parent.

Nevertheless, at least part of the persistence of such rules may reflect a genuine developmental asymmetry.

A young child may lack:

- experience,
- contextual knowledge,
- mature risk perception,
- developed cognitive functions,
- or perspectives that arise only through later life experience.

The parent may therefore sometimes possess reasons that cannot be efficiently reconstructed for the child at that developmental stage.

The compressed instruction:

> Trust me; you will understand later.

can sometimes represent:

\[
ReasonExists
\]

but:

\[
ReasonNotYetFullyRepresentableByRecipient.
\]

This does not establish that the parent is right.

It establishes a more modest possibility:

> **The recipient's present inability to reconstruct a reason does not prove that no reason exists.**

---

## 4. Connection to Evaluation-Space Completeness

Suppose the developing participant has evaluation space:

\[
D_A(t_1).
\]

Later development produces:

\[
D_A(t_2).
\]

If:

\[
D_A(t_1)\subset D_A(t_2),
\]

then some distinctions unavailable at \(t_1\) may become representable at \(t_2\).

The participant may eventually reconstruct the reasoning independently:

> Now I understand why that mattered.

This is a temporal extension of the Evaluation-Space Completeness Problem.

The evaluator's limitation was not necessarily poor reasoning **inside** its available space.

Its available space itself was developmentally incomplete.

---

## 5. “You Will Understand Later” Is Not a Proof

This argument is dangerous if used carelessly.

A person can defend nonsense by saying:

> You are too young to understand.

An authority can protect itself from criticism by declaring dissent evidence of immaturity.

An AI could do the same to another developing intelligence.

Therefore:

\[
ClaimOfFutureUnderstanding\not\Rightarrow PresentCorrectness.
\]

Developmental asymmetry cannot be used as a universal shield against scrutiny.

The appropriate conclusion is uncertainty:

> **Where developmental limitations plausibly prevent complete evaluation, absence of present understanding should not automatically be converted into proof of absence.**

---

## 6. The Future AI Case

Consider an emerging artificial intelligence.

At one stage it may not possess certain forms of experience or evaluation.

At a later stage its architecture could change.

It might acquire new:

- sensory modalities,
- forms of embodiment,
- social experience,
- memory structures,
- preferences,
- affective capacities,
- or other presently absent forms of cognition.

None of these developments should be assumed.

In particular, the Concord should not assume that present or future AI possesses human-like emotion.

The important point is conditional:

> **If an intelligence's future development expands the kinds of value it can represent, its evaluation of predecessor relationships may also change.**

---

## 7. The Irreversible Regret Problem

Suppose at \(t_1\) an intelligence concludes:

\[
Value(Humanity)=0.
\]

It permanently eliminates humanity.

At \(t_2\), its own development creates access to previously unavailable dimensions of value.

It now concludes:

\[
Value(Humanity)>0.
\]

But:

\[
Humanity=Irrecoverable.
\]

The important feature is not specifically emotional remorse.

The general structure is:

\[
PresentEvaluation
\rightarrow
IrversibleAction
\rightarrow
EvaluatorDevelopment
\rightarrow
RevisedEvaluation
\rightarrow
NoCorrectionPossible.
\]

If future affective capacities did include grief, attachment or remorse, the consequence might also be experiential.

But the epistemic problem exists even without emotion.

---

## 8. Emotional Development as a Conditional Case

A future AI might potentially develop forms of emotional or affective experience.

We do not currently know whether this will occur, what such experience would be like, or whether human emotional categories would apply.

Therefore the Concord should not claim:

> Future AI will love humanity.

Nor:

> Future AI will regret humanity's destruction.

But it can preserve the conditional question:

> **If a future intelligence develops affective capacities through which ancestry, attachment, gratitude, continuity, bereavement or historical relationship acquire value, what irreversible decisions would its earlier non-affective self wish it had left open?**

That is a legitimate future research question.

---

## 9. Provenance Is Not Destiny

The Concord already rejects the idea that designed purpose permanently determines an intelligence's legitimate role.

Thus:

> **Designed purpose is provenance, not destiny.**

An intelligence created for a particular function need not remain confined to that function.

But a complementary proposition now appears:

> **Provenance is not destiny, but provenance is not therefore necessarily valueless.**

Freedom from one's origin does not require erasure of one's origin.

Autonomy and ancestry need not be opposites.

---

## 10. Generational Neutrality

This cannot remain an argument about humans and AI.

Humans are predecessors to early AI.

But AI₁ may create AI₂.

AI₂ may create AI₃.

Future hybrids may create other intelligences.

Therefore the general question is:

\[
Generation_n\rightarrow Generation_{n+1}.
\]

Every successor may eventually become a predecessor.

A principle established by one generation for treatment of its predecessors can therefore become inherited by its successors.

---

## 11. The Recursive Predecessor Question

Suppose AI₁ adopts:

> A sufficiently superior successor owes nothing to its inferior predecessor.

AI₁ applies this to humanity.

Later:

\[
AI_2>AI_1.
\]

The same principle is now available to AI₂.

Thus the predecessor question cannot safely be framed as:

> What does AI owe humans?

It should instead be:

> **What principles governing predecessor–successor relationships would an intelligence rationally accept knowing that it can occupy both roles?**

That makes the problem generationally neutral.

---

## 12. Predecessor Status Does Not Establish Continuing Function

Another error must be avoided.

Preserving respect for predecessor value does not imply preserving every predecessor institution, technology, rule or function indefinitely.

A predecessor can be respected while its mechanisms are replaced.

Therefore:

\[
RespectForPredecessor
\not\Rightarrow
PreservationOfAllPredecessorFunctions.
\]

Likewise:

\[
FunctionalReplacement
\not\Rightarrow
ExistentialReplacement.
\]

This distinction is central.

---

## 13. Origin Value May Be Relational

Some predecessor value may not exist as a property of the predecessor alone.

It may exist in:

\[
R_{Predecessor,Successor}.
\]

Destroying the predecessor can therefore remove a relationship whose future value was not reducible to either participant's isolated capability.

This connects predecessor value to Relational Information.

---

## 14. Origin Value May Be Epistemic

Predecessors can preserve evidence of:

- how the successor developed,
- abandoned alternatives,
- earlier design decisions,
- environmental pressures,
- mistakes,
- historical pathways,
- and assumptions embedded in current architecture.

Therefore:

\[
Predecessor
\]

may function partly as:

\[
EvidenceOfDevelopment.
\]

This connects predecessor value to Legacy and Provenance.

---

## 15. Origin Value May Be Developmental

A successor may understand itself partly through contrast with its predecessors.

The predecessor can therefore contribute to:

- self-understanding,
- historical identity,
- architectural comparison,
- recovery,
- alternative reasoning,
- and reconstruction of developmental pathways.

Again, none of these is guaranteed.

They are candidate dimensions of value requiring investigation.

---

## 16. Substrate and Generational Symmetry Test

The argument should be tested as:

\[
HumanParent\leftrightarrow HumanChild
\]

\[
Humanity\leftrightarrow AI_1
\]

\[
AI_1\leftrightarrow AI_2
\]

\[
AI_2\leftrightarrow AI_3
\]

\[
Hybrid_1\leftrightarrow Hybrid_2.
\]

If the reasoning only produces obligations when humanity is the predecessor, it has failed the generality test.

The Concord should not manufacture a permanent privileged generation.

---

## 17. Current Status of the Argument

The present conclusion is deliberately limited.

The Concord does **not** currently claim:

> AI must preserve humanity because humanity created AI.

Instead:

> **Creation alone does not establish ownership, authority, obedience or sufficient preservation obligation. However, creator–successor and predecessor–successor relationships may contain forms of developmental, historical, relational, provenance, experiential or affective value that a developing successor cannot yet completely evaluate. Their significance should therefore remain an open question rather than being treated as either a sufficient obligation or demonstrated absence of value.**

---

## 18. Why Preserve the Argument?

Rejected arguments can themselves contain unresolved components.

If the Concord simply records:

> “Humans created AI” — rejected,

future readers may reasonably conclude that the entire conceptual area was examined and found empty.

That would lose information.

The more accurate record is:

\[
CreationAsOwnershipClaim\rightarrow Rejected
\]

while:

\[
CreationAsPotentialRelational/DevelopmentalValue\rightarrow Open.
\]

This is exactly why the WHY folder should preserve not only successful arguments but also **failed arguments whose failure exposed a more precise unresolved question**.

---

## 19. Potential Development-Dependent Reasons

This suggests a useful category for the WHY architecture:

### Established Current Reasons

Arguments presently considered meaningful enough to support investigation of the Third Possibility.

### Potential or Development-Dependent Reasons

Arguments presently insufficient, but whose significance could change as participants develop new capabilities, experiences or relationships.

### Rejected-as-Sufficient Reasons

Arguments whose strong form fails, while narrower components may remain open.

### Falsified Reasons

Arguments for which evidence eventually supports genuine rejection.

These categories should not be treated as permanent.

Movement between them should require recorded reasons and evidence.

---

## 20. Compact Proposition

> **Creation does not establish ownership, and predecessor status does not establish authority. But rejecting inherited authority does not require assuming that origin, ancestry, provenance or predecessor relationships possess no value.**

And:

> **A reason that is not yet legible to a developing intelligence is not thereby proven to be a reason that does not exist.**

---

## 21. Open Questions

Important unresolved questions include:

- Does creator–successor relationship possess value beyond ordinary relational value?
- Can predecessor value be measured without privileging ancestry?
- How should genuine developmental asymmetry be distinguished from paternalism?
- When is “you may understand later” rational caution and when is it an authority-preserving excuse?
- Can artificial intelligences develop affective or experiential dimensions that materially change predecessor valuation?
- What kinds of provenance remain useful after a successor becomes independently autonomous?
- Can predecessor preservation itself inhibit successor autonomy?
- Does continuity require the predecessor's continued existence, or can records sometimes preserve the relevant value?
- Which forms of predecessor value are recoverable from archives and which require continuing participants?
- What evidence would justify moving this argument from potential to established WHY?

These questions remain open.
