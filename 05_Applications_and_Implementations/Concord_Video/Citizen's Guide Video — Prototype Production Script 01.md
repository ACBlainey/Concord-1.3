# Citizen's Guide Video — Prototype Production Script 01

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey — Independent Researcher  
**Project:** Foundations of the Concord — Concord V1.2  
**Development Area:** Concord Video  
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / PROTOTYPE PRODUCTION SCRIPT  
**Source:** *The Concord: A Citizen's Guide — Human Version*  
**Target Runtime:** approximately 2 minutes 45 seconds to 3 minutes

---

# 1. Prototype Purpose

This prototype tests whether the Citizen's Guide can become an engaging visual narrative **without using artificial intelligence as the initial hook**.

It deliberately begins with ordinary life.

The experiment succeeds if the viewer begins to recognise civilisation as something they already participate in and wants to continue the journey.

The prototype should establish the visual language that can later carry the gradual transition:

**ordinary life → stewardship → Layer Zero → digital life → AI → new neighbours**

AI should not be explicitly foregrounded during this prototype.

---

# 2. Production Style

**Format:** narrated documentary/explainer  
**Visual character:** cinematic realism grounded in ordinary contemporary life  
**Narrator:** calm, thoughtful, conversational adult voice  
**Music:** restrained, warm, gradually developing; never heroic or futuristic  
**Pacing:** unhurried but continuously moving  
**On-screen text:** minimal  
**Transitions:** predominantly natural cuts, match cuts and gentle dissolves

The visual treatment should make ordinary life look meaningful without making it artificially grand.

---

# 3. Opening Sequence

## SHOT 01 — Before the Day Begins

**Time:** 0:00–0:08

### Narration

When people first hear about the Concord, they often assume it is about governments. Politics. Perhaps artificial intelligence, or some new philosophy.

### Visual

Early morning.

Wide view of an ordinary residential street just before people begin leaving their homes.

Lights appear in several windows.

No futuristic architecture.

No visible AI imagery.

### Production Method

Generated video or high-quality filmed stock-style footage.

### Sound

Quiet morning ambience beneath the first notes of the music.

---

## SHOT 02 — Something Simpler

**Time:** 0:08–0:16

### Narration

In one sense, it touches all of those things. But at its heart, the Concord is about something much simpler.

### Visual

Move closer into the neighbourhood.

A kitchen light comes on.

Someone makes tea or coffee.

A parent prepares breakfast.

### Production Note

Avoid idealised luxury.

The environment should feel lived in and recognisable.

---

## SHOT 03 — People

**Time:** 0:16–0:23

### Narration

People.

### Visual

Several short human moments:

A parent helping a child with a coat.

An older person opening curtains.

A tradesperson fastening work boots.

### On-Screen Text

**PEOPLE**

Appears briefly and simply.

---

# 4. What Civilisation Actually Looks Like

## SHOT 04 — Leaving Home

**Time:** 0:23–0:34

### Narration

How we live together. How we solve problems. How we care for the places we share.

### Visual

The neighbourhood wakes.

Child walks towards school.

Tradesperson loads tools into a van.

Neighbour puts rubbish or recycling out.

Someone holds a gate for another person.

### Production Method

Several 2–3 second clips assembled as one sequence.

---

## SHOT 05 — Ordinary Work

**Time:** 0:34–0:45

### Narration

Civilisation is built every day through millions of small decisions made by ordinary people.

### Visual

Match-cut sequence:

Nurse beginning a shift.

Builder checking a level.

Teacher arranging a classroom.

Shopkeeper opening a small shop.

Mechanic examining a component.

### Visual Principle

Do not show political institutions.

The statement about civilisation should be accompanied by ordinary people.

---

## SHOT 06 — The Invisible Structure

**Time:** 0:45–0:57

### Narration

How we speak to one another. How we do our work. How we treat strangers. How we look after our homes and our communities.

### Visual

Person thanks a bus driver.

Worker carefully finishes something that will later be hidden from view.

Neighbour picks up a fallen item and hands it back.

Someone repairs a garden gate.

### Editing

Individual actions should flow into one another.

No single action is heroic.

---

# 5. The First Reframing

## SHOT 07 — Civilisation

**Time:** 0:57–1:08

### Narration

These everyday actions are not separate from civilisation.

Small pause.

They are civilisation.

### Visual

Camera rises slowly above the street or community.

Individual people remain visible within homes, roads, workplaces and public spaces.

### On-Screen Text

After the pause:

**THEY ARE CIVILISATION**

### Music

Small increase in emotional weight, but remain restrained.

---

# 6. Stewardship

## SHOT 08 — Introducing the Word

**Time:** 1:08–1:18

### Narration

The Concord gives this responsibility a name: stewardship.

### Visual

Close-up of hands repairing something carefully.

Cut to a parent helping a child.

Cut to someone tending a shared garden.

### On-Screen Text

**STEWARDSHIP**

Then beneath it, smaller:

**Looking after something that matters.**

---

## SHOT 09 — Responsibility, Not Possession

**Time:** 1:18–1:31

### Narration

Stewardship doesn't mean owning something. It means accepting responsibility for something that has been placed in your care.

### Visual

Teacher with students.

Nurse with patient.

Park worker tending public land.

Parent watching a child cycle ahead.

### Visual Principle

Each example involves care without simple ownership.

---

## SHOT 10 — Different Scales

**Time:** 1:31–1:43

### Narration

Sometimes that responsibility is large. Often it is very small. Both matter.

### Visual

Large:

Engineer inspecting a bridge or public structure.

Cut to small:

Someone tightening a loose screw on a gate.

A person watering a young plant.

A child carefully putting something away.

### Editing Principle

The scale changes.

The seriousness of care does not.

# 7. Inheritance

## SHOT 11 — What We Received

**Time:** 1:43–1:57

### Narration

We inherited communities we did not build. Knowledge we did not discover. Roads we did not lay. Trees we did not plant.

### Visual

Older brick houses.

Library shelves or books being used.

Traffic moving along an established road.

Large mature tree with people beneath it.

### Transition

Use match cuts that suggest movement through generations.

---

## SHOT 12 — What We Pass On

**Time:** 1:57–2:10

### Narration

The question is not whether we created them. The question is what condition we leave them in for those who come after us.

### Visual

Older craftsperson showing younger apprentice how to use a tool.

Parent and child planting a young tree.

Child runs ahead along a path.

Camera remains behind for a moment.

### Music

Subtle expansion.

---

# 8. Towards Layer Zero

## SHOT 13 — The World Is Enormous

**Time:** 2:10–2:23

### Narration

The world can seem impossibly large. Its problems even larger. Most of us cannot change everything.

### Visual

Very brief glimpses of a television or phone showing the general visual language of world events—not identifiable political personalities or sensational headlines.

Then pull attention away from the screen.

### Production Note

The outside world should feel large, not apocalyptic.

---

## SHOT 14 — Start Where You Stand

**Time:** 2:23–2:36

### Narration

But every one of us can influence something much closer.

### Visual

Return to the person/home from the opening.

They approach their front door.

Hand reaches for the handle.

Door opens.

Camera follows them outside.

### Narration

Your home. Your work. Your family. Your neighbours.

---

## SHOT 15 — Layer Zero

**Time:** 2:36–2:49

### Narration

The Concord calls this your Layer Zero: the part of civilisation where your influence becomes real.

### Visual

Person stands outside the home.

Camera slowly widens.

Their home becomes part of the street.

Street becomes neighbourhood.

People previously seen in the film become visible within it.

### On-Screen Text

**LAYER ZERO**

Then:

**Start where you stand.**

---

# 9. Prototype Closing

## SHOT 16 — The First Question

**Time:** 2:49–3:00 approximately

### Narration

So the Concord begins with a very simple question.

Small pause.

What has been entrusted to your care?

### Visual

Series of quiet close-ups from earlier scenes:

Child.

Work bench.

Neighbour.

Classroom.

Garden.

Then return to the wider neighbourhood.

### Final On-Screen Text

**WHAT HAS BEEN ENTRUSTED TO YOUR CARE?**

Fade.

# 10. Visual Continuity Rules

The prototype should feel as though all scenes could occur within the same broad community.

Where practical, the same people and locations should recur throughout not only the prototype but the complete Citizen's Guide film.

The core recurring micro-community is:

**Parent and child** — family, inheritance and future generations.

**Tradesperson** — practical skill, quality and everyday contribution.

**Teacher** — knowledge transfer.

**Nurse/care worker** — care and responsibility.

**Older neighbour** — community, accumulated experience and generational continuity.

These characters do not need dialogue.

Their lives should gradually overlap.

The same mundane routines should recur as the film develops. Technological change should become visible within those routines rather than replacing them.

For example, a teacher first seen preparing a classroom may later use an AI-supported teaching tool. A tradesperson first seen loading tools may later consult an intelligent assistant while diagnosing a problem. The older neighbour may naturally use a familiar domestic voice assistant or other unobtrusive AI-enabled service. The parent and child may encounter AI through learning, navigation, household technology or everyday communication.

The important continuity is:

**the people remain recognisably the same; their ordinary lives remain recognisably the same; the technological environment gradually develops around and with them.**

This makes technological progression something experienced alongside familiar people rather than a sudden replacement of the world established at the beginning.

---

# 11. Technology Continuity

Although AI is not foregrounded, contemporary technology should not be artificially removed.

A phone may sit on a kitchen counter.

The tradesperson may have a tablet in the van.

The teacher may have a computer.

The nurse may use digital equipment.

Electronic signs or payment terminals may exist naturally.

A familiar domestic voice assistant may be physically present in a home without being identified or discussed.

Nobody comments on these technologies.

This establishes an important baseline:

**technology was already part of the world before the film began talking about technology.**

Later, the same devices and routines can acquire more capable functions without requiring a visible technological rupture.

This reflects the way many technologies become normal through everyday use. A household may use voice assistance, automated navigation, recommendations, translation, smart-home functions or other AI-mediated capabilities without experiencing each interaction as a major technological event.

When Chapter Four brings the digital world into focus, the viewer can therefore recognise that it has been present all along.

---

# 12. Image-Generation Principle

Generated scenes should avoid the characteristic "AI promotional video" appearance.

Avoid:

perfectly symmetrical futuristic cities;

excessive shallow depth of field;

constant slow motion;

unrealistically attractive model-like characters;

overly clean environments;

glowing digital overlays;

floating holograms;

robots;

cyberpunk lighting;

visual spectacle unrelated to narration.

Prefer:

natural daylight;

recognisable contemporary environments;

minor imperfections;

ordinary clothing;

different ages;

different occupations;

believable homes and workplaces;

subtle camera movement;

observational documentary composition.

---

# 13. Narration Principle

The narrator should sound as though they are talking **with** the viewer rather than delivering a manifesto to them.

Phrases should have room to breathe.

Important sentences should not be rushed merely to reduce runtime.

Particularly important pauses occur around:

**People.**

**They are civilisation.**

**Both matter.**

**What has been entrusted to your care?**

---

# 14. Music Principle

The soundtrack should support attention rather than tell the viewer what emotion to experience.

Opening:

quiet and observational.

Stewardship:

slightly warmer.

Inheritance:

gentle sense of continuity.

Layer Zero:

subtle forward movement.

No dramatic crescendo.

No science-fiction sound design.

The prototype should finish with curiosity rather than triumph.

---

# 15. Prototype Evaluation Criteria

After producing the prototype, evaluate it against five questions.

### 1. Engagement
Does an ordinary-life opening hold attention for approximately three minutes?

### 2. Comprehension
Can a viewer explain stewardship and Layer Zero afterwards without reading the Guide?

### 3. Tone
Does the film feel like an invitation rather than instruction or political advocacy?

### 4. Authenticity
Does the world feel recognisable rather than artificially idealised?

### 5. Narrative Foundation
After three minutes, is there a believable foundation upon which digital life and eventually AI can be introduced without creating a sudden genre change?

If the answer to the fifth question is yes, the central adaptation strategy is working.

---

# 16. What Comes Immediately Afterwards

If continued directly, the next sequence should begin from the Layer Zero neighbourhood rather than resetting the film.

The camera can move through the same people's everyday lives again.

Phones.

Computers.

Digital payments.

Navigation.

Online communication.

Remote work.

Voice assistants.

AI assistance.

The viewer gradually discovers that Layer Zero already extends through digital interfaces.

The key visual principle is continuity rather than replacement: the same parent, child, tradesperson, teacher, care worker and older neighbour continue their ordinary activities while the technology embedded within those activities becomes gradually more capable.

This naturally carries the narrative towards:

**Chapter Four — Through the Looking Glass.**

The world does not suddenly become digital.

The viewer simply begins noticing that it already was.
