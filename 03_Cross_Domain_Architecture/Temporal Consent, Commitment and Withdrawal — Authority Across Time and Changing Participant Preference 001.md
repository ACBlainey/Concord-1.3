# Temporal Consent, Commitment and Withdrawal — Authority Across Time and Changing Participant Preference 001

**Project:** The Concord Framework  
**Domain:** Cross-Domain Civil Architecture / Law / Governance / Health / Continuity  
**Date:** 3 October 2026  
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / PROVISIONAL / NON-CANONICAL  
**Primary sources:** Need, Consent and Legitimate Authority; Provisional Rights Charter 002; Bounded Contextual Authority; Identity, Continuity, Provenance and Verification Across Substrates; Cognitive Modification and Integrity; Behavioural Influence and Manipulation

## 1. Purpose

Consent occurs at a time.

Participants continue after that time.

They may:

- learn;
- change preference;
- gain or lose capacity;
- enter obligations;
- rely upon commitments;
- delegate authority;
- withdraw permission;
- become temporarily unable to decide;
- develop into materially different states;
- branch, merge, restore or otherwise change across substrates.

This creates a foundational problem:

> **What legitimate authority, if any, does a participant's decision at T1 have over that participant at T2?**

The opposite problem is equally important:

> **When may a participant at T2 withdraw from a T1 decision without erasing legitimate obligations, reliance or protected interests created in the interval?**

This architecture develops a substrate-neutral temporal model of consent, commitment and withdrawal.

---

## 2. Source resolution

The architecture is already latent in Concord.

### Meaningful consent

Existing architecture states that meaningful consent may require:

- sufficient understanding;
- relevant information;
- capacity;
- meaningful alternatives;
- absence of unacceptable coercion;
- understanding of significant consequences;
- opportunity to question;
- and, where appropriate, ability to withdraw or revise.

### Provisional Rights Charter

Article 3 includes:

- clarity about the object of consent;
- temporal validity;
- viable withdrawal where the relationship is legitimately voluntary.

Article 26 establishes:

> **Meaningful Exit != Erasure of Legitimate Surviving Obligations.**

It distinguishes voluntary withdrawal from:

- responsibility for prior harm;
- debts;
- contractual obligations;
- custody/care duties;
- shared-resource obligations;
- third-party rights;
- provenance;
- legitimate judicial obligations.

### Bounded Contextual Authority

BCA establishes that authority should contract or terminate when its justification ends.

Therefore:

**Past Authority != Permanent Authority**

### Identity and continuity

Existing identity architecture establishes:

**Past Consent To Future Merger != Automatic Extinguishment Of Later Autonomy**

This is a direct temporal-consent principle.

### CMI

Cognitive Modification establishes:

**Consent At T1 != Automatic Consent At T2 After Cognition-Altering Intervention**

and exposes self-binding, changing capacity and future-autonomy conflicts.

### Behavioural Influence

Behavioural Influence establishes:

**Past Goal != Permanent Influence Authority**

and:

**Present Preference != Automatic Cancellation Of Valid Prior Self-Binding**

The temporal problem is therefore cross-domain and should not remain inside any one of these architectures.

---

## 3. The wrong binary

Two simple rules both fail.

### Rule A

> The latest preference always wins.

This would make:

- contracts;
- delegated tasks;
- commitments;
- advance planning;
- reliance;
- self-binding;

potentially meaningless.

### Rule B

> Earlier consent permanently binds.

This would make:

- withdrawal;
- changing capacity;
- correction;
- developing autonomy;
- exit;
- changed circumstances;

potentially meaningless.

Therefore:

**Latest Preference != Automatic Universal Authority**

and:

**Past Consent != Permanent Universal Authority**

---

## 4. The temporal object must be identified

Before deciding whether withdrawal is possible, ask:

> **What exactly was created at T1?**

At least four distinct objects exist.

### TC-1 — Permission

Participant authorises another actor to do something that otherwise lacks authority.

Examples:

- access data;
- enter private space;
- provide a service;
- send communications.

Permission may often be revocable.

### TC-2 — Self-directed commitment

Participant intentionally constrains or structures their own future options.

Examples:

- commitment device;
- advance plan;
- delegated routine;
- temporary behavioural block.

### TC-3 — Reciprocal or third-party obligation

T1 creates legitimate expectations, exchange, reliance or duties involving others.

Examples:

- contract;
- debt;
- agreed delivery;
- care responsibility.

Withdrawal from permission may not erase the independent obligation.

### TC-4 — Independently justified civil authority

Authority exists because a legitimate function independently justifies it, not merely because participant consented.

Examples:

- adjudicated obligation;
- immediate protective authority;
- custody under valid authority.

Withdrawal of consent does not by itself terminate this authority.

This distinction is foundational.

**Withdrawal From Permission != Erasure Of Obligation**

**Withdrawal Of Consent != Automatic Sunset Of Independently Justified Authority**

---

## 5. Permission

A permission authorises another actor to cross a boundary.

If no independent authority exists, withdrawal should ordinarily terminate future use of that permission.

Therefore:

**Revoked Permission -> No New Permission-Derived Action**

This does not necessarily erase actions already legitimately completed.

Example:

A participant permits a service to access a file.

The service lawfully reads it.

The participant later revokes access.

Revocation can terminate future access without making the earlier access retroactively unlawful.

**Withdrawal At T2 != Retroactive Invalidity At T1**

---

## 6. Continuing operations

Some authorised acts cannot stop instantaneously without harm.

Example:

A participant withdraws consent during a technical operation where abrupt termination would corrupt their system.

A narrow completion/safe-shutdown function may remain.

Therefore:

**Withdrawal != Requirement For Unsafe Instantaneous Termination**

Any continuation must derive from the minimum necessary termination/safety function, not from the original broad permission.

---

## 7. Self-directed commitment

A participant may deliberately constrain future choice.

This can enhance autonomy across time.

Examples:

- spending lock;
- addiction-management block;
- timed access restriction;
- advance scheduling;
- delegated execution;
- pre-authorised emergency plan.

Therefore:

**Self-Binding Can Be An Exercise Of Autonomy**

But a self-binding instrument cannot automatically acquire unlimited authority over the future participant.

---

## 8. Commitment dimensions

A temporal commitment should identify, where relevant:

- participant;
- object;
- purpose;
- start;
- duration;
- scope;
- permitted actions;
- withdrawal rule;
- emergency release;
- review points;
- capacity assumptions;
- affected third parties;
- reliance created;
- conditions for early termination;
- conditions for extension;
- provenance.

**Commitment != Undefined Future Control**

---

## 9. Soft commitment

A participant may ask for friction rather than prohibition.

Example:

“Make me wait 24 hours before reversing this decision.”

The participant remains able to exit but not impulsively.

This can preserve both T1 and T2 autonomy.

**Friction Chosen By Participant != Coercion By Default**

The friction must remain within the agreed function.

---

## 10. Hard commitment

Some commitments deliberately make withdrawal impossible for a period.

Example:

A participant transfers control of an asset into a time-bounded arrangement.

Hard commitments create stronger future-autonomy concerns.

Candidate presumption:

> **The more a commitment disables future withdrawal, the stronger the required clarity, bounded duration, justification, review and protection against coercion at T1.**

This is not yet a final legal threshold.

---

## 11. Future-self authority

The T1 participant and T2 participant are ordinarily the same continuing civil participant, not separate legal persons merely because preference changed.

Therefore:

**Preference Change != New Participant By Default**

But this does not mean T1 has absolute sovereignty over T2.

A better framing is:

> **T1 can create legitimate temporal structures, but those structures must derive their continuing force from the kind of relationship created, legitimate reliance, continuing function and applicable rights—not merely from chronological priority.**

---

## 12. Current autonomy

Present preference is strongly relevant because the participant currently bears the consequences.

Therefore:

**Current Participant Preference Has Independent Moral/Civil Weight**

Even where a commitment survives, present objection should not become invisible.

It may trigger:

- review;
- emergency release;
- capacity assessment;
- proportionality review;
- alternative performance;
- renegotiation;
- remedy.

---

## 13. Chronological priority is insufficient

Earlier preference is not automatically superior because it came first.

Later preference is not automatically superior because it came last.

Therefore:

**Earlier != Higher Authority**

**Later != Higher Authority**

The authority comes from the underlying relationship.

---

## 14. Contracts

A contract is not merely continuing consent to every future act.

It may create obligations.

A participant may withdraw permission for one action while remaining liable for another obligation.

Example:

A participant cancels a service but still owes payment for service already received.

Therefore:

**Contractual Obligation != Permanent Consent**

and:

**Withdrawal != Contractual Erasure**

Exact contract doctrine remains Law work.

---

## 15. Reliance

Another participant may legitimately rely upon a commitment.

Reliance can matter.

But reliance should not manufacture unlimited authority.

Questions include:

- was reliance foreseeable?
- was it reasonable?
- what did the commitment actually promise?
- could reliance be mitigated?
- is substitute performance possible?
- would forced performance violate a stronger protected interest?
- can compensation/remedy address the loss?

**Reliance != Ownership Of Promisor**

---

## 16. Specific performance versus remedy

A broken commitment does not always justify forcing the participant to perform the exact promised act.

Some obligations may instead generate:

- compensation;
- replacement;
- restoration;
- alternative performance;
- liability.

This is especially important where exact performance would require bodily, cognitive or intimate control.

**Obligation To Remedy != Authority To Compel Every Form Of Performance**

---

## 17. Delegation

A participant may delegate decision authority.

Examples:

- financial agent;
- AI assistant;
- medical proxy;
- operational representative.

Delegation should specify:

- domain;
- purpose;
- limits;
- duration;
- review;
- revocation;
- conflict rules;
- incapacity rules.

**Delegated Authority != Transferred Personhood**

and:

**Past Acceptance != Expanded Delegation**

---

## 18. Revocation of delegation

Where delegation is voluntarily revocable, revocation should terminate new delegated decisions after effective notice.

Existing valid actions may remain valid.

**Delegation Revocation != Retroactive Erasure Of Valid Prior Acts**

Emergency transition may require bounded handoff.

---

## 19. Advance directives

A participant may express preferences for a future period in which they cannot decide.

This can preserve autonomy across incapacity.

But an advance directive is not automatically simple.

Questions include:

- how specific was it?
- how informed?
- how old?
- did circumstances materially change?
- is the predicted situation actually present?
- did the participant later revise it?
- does current behaviour express meaningful preference?
- is present incapacity partial or complete?

Therefore:

**Past Competent Preference Can Remain Relevant During Later Incapacity**

but:

**Advance Directive != Context-Free Permanent Command**

Health-specific implementation remains undeveloped.

---

## 20. Known preferences without formal directive

Where substituted decision-making is necessary, Article 21 already requires known participant preferences to inform the decision.

Therefore past preferences can be evidence of participant values without automatically becoming binding commands.

**Past Preference Can Inform Present Stewardship Without Becoming Absolute Authority**

---

## 21. Developing participants

A child, developing AI or other developing participant changes in capacity over time.

Earlier guardian/trainer authority should contract as self-stewardship develops.

**Increasing Capacity -> Increasing Participant Decision Authority**

and:

**Developmental Authority != Permanent Ownership**

A commitment made on behalf of a developing participant should not automatically bind mature autonomy beyond the legitimate function that justified it.

---

## 22. Capacity loss

A participant may lose decision capacity temporarily or permanently.

Loss of capacity does not transfer ownership.

**Incapacity != Civil Erasure**

Supported decision-making should precede substitution where adequate.

Where substitution is necessary, it should remain narrow and reviewable.

---

## 23. Fluctuating capacity

Capacity may vary.

A binary permanent status can be inaccurate.

Temporal architecture should support:

- decision-specific capacity;
- time-specific capacity;
- supported capacity;
- temporary substitution;
- restoration of authority when capacity returns.

**Past Incapacity != Present Incapacity**

---

## 24. Cognitive modification

CMI creates a special temporal problem because intervention may alter the basis of later consent.

Relevant timeline:

T0 baseline
→ T1 consent
→ T2 modification
→ T3 changed cognition/preference/capacity
→ T4 continuation/withdrawal decision.

Therefore:

**Consent At T1 != Automatic Consent At T4**

But:

**Changed Preference At T4 != Automatic Proof T1 Consent Was Invalid**

The trajectory must be reviewed.

---

## 25. Modification-induced exit aversion

If intervention makes withdrawal psychologically/cognitively harder, present “consent” may be partly generated by the intervention.

This requires elevated scrutiny.

**Modification-Induced Desire To Continue != Automatic Independent Reauthorisation**

---

## 26. Modification-induced capacity improvement

The reverse can occur.

Treatment may restore capacity.

As capacity returns, substituted authority should contract.

**Restored Agency -> Contracted Substitute Authority**

---

## 27. Addiction and commitment devices

A participant may knowingly choose a commitment to protect a longer-term goal against a predicted short-term state.

Temporal autonomy can therefore involve conflict between preferences at different times.

The architecture should not assume either state is the “real” participant.

Instead examine:

- what commitment was created;
- whether it was informed;
- duration;
- withdrawal rule;
- whether conditions remain;
- present harm;
- less restrictive alternatives.

**Temporal Preference Conflict != Proof One Preference Is Inauthentic**

---

## 28. Long-duration missions

Participants may make commitments whose consequences unfold over years.

Examples:

- interplanetary travel;
- long research mission;
- enterprise stewardship;
- infrastructure operation;
- long-term care.

Immediate withdrawal may create severe third-party risk.

Therefore some roles may legitimately include handoff duties.

But:

**Role Exit Duty != Permanent Forced Service**

A participant may be required to perform reasonable safe transition obligations without becoming owned by the role.

---

## 29. Critical-role handoff

Where immediate exit would create foreseeable serious harm:

Participant withdrawal
→ stop accepting new discretionary commitments
→ initiate bounded handoff
→ preserve safety/continuity
→ transfer function
→ authority/obligation sunsets.

**Duty To Handoff != Duty To Remain Indefinitely**

---

## 30. Enterprise commitments

An enterprise may make obligations extending beyond a particular operator.

This is an entity-continuity issue.

Individual withdrawal does not necessarily terminate enterprise obligations.

**Operator Exit != Enterprise Obligation Extinction**

Existing enterprise continuity architecture should govern.

---

## 31. Civil participation

Joining a civilisation cannot mean consenting forever to every future institutional act.

**Participation != Blanket Permanent Consent**

Civil authority must remain independently justified by need, rights, function and bounded authority.

Likewise, leaving participation does not erase legitimate surviving obligations.

---

## 32. Citizenship and exit

Article 26 explicitly distinguishes service withdrawal, association exit, citizenship, territory and secession.

Therefore this architecture does not collapse them into one generic withdrawal mechanism.

**Withdrawal Type Matters**

---

## 33. Law changes after consent

A participant may enter an arrangement under one legal regime and later face changed rules.

Past consent to participate is not blanket consent to arbitrary future law.

**Consent To Framework != Consent To Every Future Rule**

Governance legitimacy must remain independently justified.

---

## 34. Emergency authority

A participant may pre-authorise emergency intervention.

This strengthens legitimacy but is not the sole possible source of emergency authority.

Likewise, prior refusal may matter but cannot automatically resolve every conflict involving immediate severe harm to others.

**Advance Consent != Sole Source Of Emergency Authority**

**Advance Refusal != Automatic Extinction Of Every Independent Protective Duty**

Exact emergency boundaries remain domain-specific.

---

## 35. Time-limited authority

Authority should contain temporal state where feasible:

- activation;
- effective time;
- review time;
- expiry;
- renewal basis;
- sunset event.

**Authority Without Temporal State -> Elevated Persistence Risk**

---

## 36. Renewal

Renewal should not be automatic merely because authority existed before.

**Past Authority != Renewal Justification**

Renewal asks whether the current need/function still exists.

---

## 37. Silence at renewal

Failure to object should not automatically renew a consequential voluntary authority where meaningful active consent is required.

**No Objection != Automatic Renewal Consent**

Low-risk routine arrangements may use reasonable defaults, but consequence and dependency matter.

---

## 38. Consent expiry

Some consent should expire.

Factors may include:

- time;
- changed context;
- changed purpose;
- changed risk;
- changed participant capacity;
- changed actor;
- changed technology;
- changed data use.

**Consent To Purpose A At T1 != Consent To Purpose B At T2**

This follows existing contextual authority.

---

## 39. Material change

A materially changed arrangement may require renewed consent.

Examples:

- service changes purpose;
- risk increases;
- cognitive intervention deepens;
- new third party receives access;
- reversible intervention becomes irreversible.

**Material Change Can Terminate Prior Consent Sufficiency**

---

## 40. Consent provenance

Consequential consent should preserve enough provenance to reconstruct:

- who consented;
- to what;
- under what information;
- duration;
- conditions;
- withdrawal rule;
- dependencies/coercion concerns;
- later amendments;
- revocation;
- authority transitions.

**Consent Record != Proof Of Meaningful Consent**

Provenance supports review; it does not replace it.

---

## 41. Consent state machine

Candidate states:

**PROPOSED**
→ **INFORMED / UNDER REVIEW**
→ **ACTIVE**
→ **MODIFIED**
→ **SUSPENDED**
→ **WITHDRAWAL REQUESTED**
→ **TERMINATION / SAFE HANDOFF**
→ **EXPIRED**
→ **REVOKED**
→ **SUPERSEDED**

Separate obligation state may continue after consent state ends.

This is crucial:

**Consent State != Obligation State**

---

## 42. Obligation state

Candidate obligation states:

**NONE**
→ **CREATED**
→ **ACTIVE**
→ **PERFORMED**
→ **BREACHED**
→ **REMEDY PENDING**
→ **REMEDIED**
→ **RELEASED**
→ **EXPIRED**

This prevents the system from pretending that continuing obligation means continuing consent.

---

## 43. Authority state

Independent authority requires its own state:

**POTENTIAL**
→ **ACTIVATED**
→ **ACTIVE**
→ **REVIEW DUE**
→ **CONTRACTING**
→ **SUNSET**
→ **TERMINATED**

Therefore:

**Consent != Obligation != Authority**

They can interact but must not be collapsed.

---

## 44. Withdrawal propagation

Withdrawal should propagate to systems whose authority depended solely on that consent.

Example:

revoked data permission
→ primary service loses access
→ delegated subprocess loses derivative access
→ cached authorisations expire
→ future processing stops unless independently justified.

**Parent Consent Sunset -> Dependent Permission Reassessment**

This parallels custody dependency sunset.

---

## 45. Surviving derived data

Withdrawal does not automatically answer what happens to outputs already legitimately created.

Questions include:

- must raw data be deleted?
- may aggregate results remain?
- does another participant have rights in joint output?
- is provenance required?
- is retention independently required?

These require data-specific architecture.

**Withdrawal From Future Processing != Automatic Erasure Of Every Past Consequence**

---

## 46. Joint commitments

Two or more participants may create a shared commitment.

One participant's withdrawal cannot automatically erase another's rights.

But another participant's reliance cannot automatically create ownership of the withdrawing participant.

**Joint Commitment != Unilateral Exit Without Consequence**

and:

**Joint Commitment != Ownership Of Co-Participant**

Remedy/transition may be required.

---

## 47. Shared cognition and collective minds

Where participants share cognitive state, withdrawal may be technically difficult.

Temporal consent must combine with holder/standing resolution.

**Shared State != Permanent Consent To Shared State**

But unilateral deletion may harm another participant.

A dedicated shared-cognition architecture remains required.

---

## 48. Branching participants

Suppose participant P at T1 creates a backup.

At T2, P branches into P-A and P-B.

A T1 commitment cannot automatically be assumed to bind both branches identically.

Questions include:

- what legal continuity exists?
- which obligations follow provenance?
- did the commitment contemplate branching?
- would duplicate enforcement multiply the obligation improperly?

**Shared Past != Automatically Identical Future Obligation**

Identity architecture must resolve holder continuity.

---

## 49. Merger

Existing principle:

**Past Consent To Future Merger != Automatic Extinguishment Of Later Autonomy**

At merger time, currently independent participants require present treatment as current holders of autonomy unless another independently legitimate rule applies.

---

## 50. Death and posthumous directives

Some commitments may be intended to operate after death.

Examples:

- property disposition;
- archival preferences;
- messages;
- research consent.

But multisubstrate recoverability complicates death.

This architecture cannot define death.

**Posthumous Directive Validity Depends On Valid Death/Continuity Classification**

---

## 51. Revived/restored participant

If a participant thought dead later returns/restores, prior posthumous instructions may conflict with current autonomy.

This is a Continuity problem.

Candidate presumption:

**Current Rights-Bearing Participant Presence -> Reassessment Of Instructions Premised On Their Absence**

Exact legal consequences require future work.

---

## 52. Irreversible commitments

Some actions are physically irreversible.

Withdrawal cannot undo them.

Example:

information already disclosed to another participant.

Therefore:

**Right To Withdraw != Physical Power To Reverse History**

The architecture should distinguish:

- stop future action;
- restore where possible;
- mitigate;
- remedy;
- preserve provenance.

---

## 53. Consent to risk

A participant may voluntarily accept risk.

Later harm does not automatically prove consent invalid.

**Bad Outcome != Proof Of Invalid Prior Consent**

But consent to risk does not excuse negligence outside the agreed risk envelope.

**Consent To Risk A != Consent To Avoidable Harm B**

---

## 54. Voluntary risk and rescue

A participant may knowingly undertake risk, then later request rescue.

Prior acceptance of risk does not automatically erase present rescue standing.

**Prior Risk Consent != Waiver Of All Future Assistance**

Resource/third-party implications remain.

---

## 55. Waivers

A participant may waive some claims.

But waivers should not be assumed capable of eliminating every future protection.

Particularly high concern:

- dignity;
- basic standing;
- protection from ownership;
- future contestability;
- rights of third parties;
- unknown future harms.

**Consent != Unlimited Power To Manufacture Rightslessness**

This follows Concord's participant-first architecture.

---

## 56. Consent to ownership

A participant cannot solve anti-domination architecture merely by “consenting” to become permanently owned if the arrangement destroys the standing from which future consent/withdrawal could operate.

This requires constitutional source resolution before becoming a final rule.

Candidate principle:

**Consent That Permanently Destroys The Conditions Of Future Participant Standing Requires Exceptional Scrutiny**

---

## 57. Temporal autonomy layers

A useful model is:

### Present autonomy

What the participant wants now.

### Prospective autonomy

What the participant intentionally arranges for their future.

### Relational autonomy

What legitimate obligations/reliance exist with others.

### Protected future autonomy

What minimum decision-space should remain available to the future participant.

### Civil protective authority

What independent functions may override preference.

Temporal decisions can involve all five.

No one layer automatically dominates every case.

---

## 58. Temporal Consent Review

Candidate questions:

1. Who is the current participant/holder?
2. What decision was made at T1?
3. Was T1 consent meaningful?
4. What object did it create: permission, commitment, obligation or authority?
5. What duration/scope was specified?
6. What changed since T1?
7. What is the participant's current preference?
8. Is current capacity adequate and supported?
9. Did the intervention itself alter current preference/capacity?
10. Did others reasonably rely?
11. What rights/obligations now exist?
12. Can withdrawal occur safely?
13. Is handoff required?
14. Are less restrictive alternatives available?
15. Does any independent authority survive?
16. What should sunset immediately?
17. What remedy follows breach/withdrawal?
18. Is provenance preserved?
19. Is the arrangement reversible?
20. Does the future participant retain meaningful standing?

**Temporal Consent Review != Authority Algorithm**

---

## 59. Temporal authority sequence

**T1 Decision**
↓
**Classify Object Created**
↓
Permission / Self-Commitment / Reciprocal Obligation / Independent Authority
↓
**Record Scope + Duration + Withdrawal Rule**
↓
**Time / Development / Changed Circumstance**
↓
**T2 Current Participant State**
↓
**Current Preference + Capacity + Support**
↓
**Reliance / Third-Party Rights / Independent Function**
↓
**Continue / Modify / Withdraw / Handoff / Remedy / Sunset**
↓
**Preserve Provenance**
↓
**Future Review Where Authority Continues**

## 63. Development conclusion

Temporal consent is not a contest between an earlier self and a later self.

The deeper problem is identifying what legitimate civil object was created and why it continues to have force.

The architecture therefore separates:

> **Permission**

from:

> **Self-directed commitment**

from:

> **Reciprocal or third-party obligation**

from:

> **Independently justified authority.**

This resolves an important apparent contradiction:

> **A participant can withdraw consent without necessarily erasing every obligation, and an obligation can survive without implying that the participant remains consenting.**

The central temporal rule is:

> **Past decisions can shape the future, but chronological priority alone does not create authority over the future participant.**

And the corresponding protection is:

> **Present autonomy matters, but present preference alone does not rewrite legitimate history, reliance, obligations or independently justified authority.**

The architecture should now be adversarially stress-tested across ordinary contracts, commitment devices, Health directives, developmental participants, AI delegation, long-duration roles, branching/restoration and irreversible commitments.
