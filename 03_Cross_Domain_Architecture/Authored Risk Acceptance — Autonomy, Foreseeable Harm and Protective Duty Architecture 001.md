**Project:** The Concord Framework
**Date:** 4 October 2026
**Status:** CROSS-DOMAIN ARCHITECTURE / PROVISIONAL / NON-CANONICAL
**Domains:** Health / Law / Employment / Commerce / Education / Research / Civil Participation

## 1. Purpose

Many legitimate activities contain foreseeable risk of serious injury, permanent disability or death.

Examples include:
- boxing and other combat sports;
- motorsport;
- climbing and mountaineering;
- diving;
- aviation;
- dangerous industrial work;
- emergency response;
- construction;
- mining;
- maritime work;
- hazardous research;
- exploration;
- voluntary rescue;
- other physically consequential activity.

The existence of foreseeable severe harm does not by itself establish authority to prohibit the activity or override the participant.

> **Foreseeable Harm != Intended Harm**

and:

> **Known Risk Of Death != Intention To Die**

## 2. Boxing as the boundary example

In boxing, physical harm is not merely imaginable; some degree of physical impact is intrinsic to the activity.

Severe injury or death is not ordinarily the intended final outcome, but is a known possible consequence.

A participant may rationally understand that risk and still choose to participate.

If the mere possibility of severe injury or death generated civil authority to override autonomous choice, boxing and many other accepted activities would become difficult to distinguish from prohibited self-harm solely by outcome severity.

Therefore:

> **Risk Acceptance Must Be Evaluated Through Authorship, Not Outcome Severity Alone**

## 3. Authored risk acceptance

A participant can author a risky choice where they can sufficiently:
- identify the activity;
- understand material foreseeable hazards;
- understand the plausible severity of consequences;
- understand material uncertainty;
- connect participation with those consequences;
- understand meaningful alternatives;
- compare the risk with their own purposes and values;
- act sufficiently voluntarily;
- communicate or otherwise establish the choice.

This is an application of decision navigational competence.

> **Risk Tolerance != Incapacity**

Different participants may rationally accept different levels of risk.

## 4. Objective, risk and consequence

Three concepts must remain separate:

**Objective** — what the participant is trying to achieve.

**Accepted risk** — an unwanted consequence knowingly tolerated as a possibility.

**Result** — what actually happens.

Thus:

**Objective != Accepted Risk != Result**

A boxer may intend to compete, accept the risk of brain injury, and suffer an injury they did not intend.

An industrial worker may intend to earn income or perform useful work, accept a bounded occupational risk, and nevertheless be injured.

> **Foreseeability != Desire**

## 5. Autonomy does not erase protective duty

Recognising authored risk does not release organisers, employers, equipment providers or institutions from their own duties.

Participant consent to a risk does not authorise another actor to:
- conceal material hazards;
- negligently increase risk;
- violate agreed safety conditions;
- use defective equipment;
- exceed the agreed rules;
- exploit dependency;
- substitute unnecessary danger for reasonably available safety;
- misrepresent evidence;
- coerce participation.

> **Consent To Risk != Consent To Negligence**

> **Acceptance Of Inherent Risk != Acceptance Of Avoidable Risk**

## 6. Employment

Employment creates a particularly important power asymmetry.

A worker may knowingly accept hazardous work.

But economic dependence can affect voluntariness, and an employer controls many conditions the worker does not.

Therefore:

> **Employment Consent != Employer Sovereignty Over Worker Safety**

The employer remains responsible for hazards within its control and for truthful disclosure, competent systems, appropriate equipment, training and reasonably achievable risk reduction.

## 7. Residual risk

Even well-designed systems may contain residual risk.

Once reasonably avoidable hazards have been addressed, a competent participant may choose whether the remaining risk is acceptable to them.

> **Residual Risk Can Be Authored**

A zero-risk requirement would itself prohibit many valuable activities and remove meaningful participant choice.

## 8. Risk reduction versus activity prohibition

Where risk can be reduced without destroying the legitimate function, risk reduction is generally preferable to prohibition.

For boxing this can include:
- rules;
- weight classes where justified;
- medical screening;
- equipment;
- referee intervention;
- concussion protocols;
- recovery periods;
- emergency medical provision;
- truthful risk information.

These protections do not imply that participation itself lacks autonomy.

> **Safety Architecture Should Reduce Unnecessary Risk Without Automatically Erasing Authored Risk**

## 9. State change during activity

Initial consent does not permanently authorise every later state.

If a participant becomes unconscious, confused, severely injured or otherwise loses relevant decision authorship, the authority context changes.

> **Authored Entry Into Risk != Permanent Consent Through Incapacity**

Emergency Health or other protective authority may activate according to its own predicates.

## 10. Withdrawal

Where the activity permits withdrawal, continuing consent must remain meaningful.

An employer, sporting body or organiser cannot rely solely on initial consent after the participant validly withdraws.

> **Consent To Begin != Irrevocable Consent To Continue**

Some actions may have unavoidable transition delays, but these do not create general authority to force continuation.

## 11. Participants protecting others

Some risky roles involve duties toward others:
- firefighter;
- rescue worker;
- ship crew;
- pilot;
- emergency responder;
- certain safety-critical employment.

A participant may voluntarily accept bounded role obligations.

But:

> **Accepted Role Obligation != Unlimited Duty To Die**

The exact duty depends on contract, function, current capability, risk, alternatives and legitimate authority.

## 12. Risk to third parties

Self-regarding risk differs from risk imposed on others.

A participant's autonomy can author their own exposure but does not automatically authorise equivalent exposure for another participant.

> **Authority To Accept Risk For Self != Authority To Impose Risk On Others**

This creates a separate public-protection and duty-of-care question.

## 13. Competitive and cultural pressure

Apparent voluntary risk can be distorted by:
- employment dependency;
- financial desperation;
- threats;
- abusive coaching;
- peer pressure;
- institutional culture;
- misinformation;
- hidden injury;
- performance incentives.

These factors should be evaluated proportionately where they materially affect authorship.

But:

> **Presence Of Social Influence != Automatic Absence Of Autonomy**

Human choices normally occur within social context.

The question is whether influence materially defeats authorship.

## 14. Repeated exposure

A participant may repeatedly accept risk.

Repeated consent does not remove institutional responsibility to learn from accumulating evidence.

If evidence shows a hazard is materially greater than represented, KCS/STRA-style review should propagate the changed knowledge.

> **Prior Consent Based On Old Risk Knowledge != Consent Based On New Risk Knowledge**

Updated material risk should be communicated and, where necessary, consent/qualification/safety architecture reviewed.

## 15. Known versus unknown risk

No participant can be informed of every unknown hazard.

Authorship requires proportionate access to material known or reasonably knowable information, not omniscience.

> **Informed Choice != Perfect Prediction**

Concealing known material risk, however, corrupts the choice.

## 16. Relationship to self-harm

Risky activity and intentional self-harm are not identical.

The distinction can include:
- participant objective;
- relationship between action and harmful consequence;
- probability;
- accepted versus intended outcome;
- contextual function.

But the constitutional lesson is shared:

> **Severity Of Possible Harm Does Not By Itself Create Authority**

Even where the harmful outcome is intended, the separate self-harm architecture shows that full decision authorship matters.

Where harm is merely a known risk of another legitimate objective, the case for automatic paternalistic authority is weaker still.

## 17. Protective intervention during risky activity

Protective intervention can still be legitimate where:
- agreed rules authorise it;
- participant loses relevant authorship;
- emergency predicates activate;
- third-party protection is required;
- an organiser must perform a previously accepted safety function;
- another independent legal predicate exists.

Example: a boxing referee may stop a fight under the agreed sporting rules even if the boxer wishes to continue.

That authority arises from the bounded participation context and rules, not from a general state ownership of the boxer.

> **Contextual Safety Authority != General Sovereignty Over Risk**

## 18. Employment refusal and retaliation

A participant should not be treated as having consented to materially changed or undisclosed risk merely because they accepted employment.

Where risk changes materially:

**Material Risk Change
-> Updated Information
-> Safety Review
-> Role/Competence Review Where Relevant
-> Renewed Participant Choice Where Practicable**

Retaliation for declining a materially changed risk may itself undermine voluntariness and require Employment/Law treatment.

## 19. Candidate decision model

For consequential voluntary risk, evaluate:

**<Participant, Activity, Objective, Material Hazards, Consequence Severity, Probability/Uncertainty, Participant Understanding, Alternatives, Voluntariness, Risk Controls, Third-Party Exposure, Withdrawal Route, State-Change Triggers>**

The model should not collapse to a numerical acceptable-risk score.

Different activities and participants require contextual evaluation.

## 21. General principle

The broader principle is:

> **A Legitimate System Must Be Capable Of Respecting An Authored Risk Or Outcome It Would Not Itself Choose.**

This does not require passivity.

The system may:
- improve information;
- reduce unnecessary risk;
- create safer alternatives;
- enforce legitimate duties on risk-creators;
- intervene when agreed contextual rules permit;
- protect third parties;
- respond when decision authorship materially changes.

But it must distinguish those functions from paternalistic ownership of the participant's risk tolerance.

## 22. Result

The self-harm work generalises beyond self-harm.

Concord can simultaneously hold that:
1. severe injury and death are undesirable;
2. unnecessary risk should be reduced;
3. organisers and employers retain safety duties;
4. participants can knowingly author residual risk;
5. severe possible consequences do not themselves create authority to prohibit autonomous participation;
6. state changes can create new protective contexts;
7. self-directed risk does not authorise imposition of equivalent risk on others.

This provides a common architecture for sport, employment, exploration, research and other consequential voluntary activity without reducing autonomy to permission to choose only safe outcomes.
