# PART II — ADVERSARIAL TESTING

# 81. Adversarial Testing Method

The preceding architecture is internally coherent.

Internal coherence is insufficient.

A governance architecture can appear elegant because its assumptions have been selected to make it work. It may perform well where:

- participants are competent;
- authority-holders act in good faith;
- functions are clearly defined;
- evidence is available;
- consequences are reversible;
- participants can exit;
- responsibilities are legible;
- time exists for review;
- authority relationships are uncontested.

Civilisations routinely operate outside those conditions.

The architecture must therefore be tested against cases involving:

- coercion;
- urgency;
- uncertainty;
- deception;
- incompetence;
- conflicting rights;
- malicious participants;
- asymmetric information;
- irreversible consequences;
- inability to exit;
- non-consenting participants;
- conflicting legitimate functions;
- responsibility diffusion;
- strategic misuse of protective autonomy;
- manufactured emergencies;
- prestige and informal power;
- radically unequal capability;
- artificial agents operating at machine speed.

The adversarial question is not:

> **Can the architecture explain a well-functioning organisation?**

It is:

> **What happens when participants deliberately or accidentally exploit its boundaries?**

Several forms of failure should be distinguished.

### 81.1 Conceptual Failure

The architecture produces contradictory legitimate outcomes.

### 81.2 Operational Failure

The principles are coherent but cannot practically determine what should happen quickly enough.

### 81.3 Capture Failure

A participant can manipulate the architecture to manufacture illegitimate authority.

### 81.4 Coordination Failure

Distributed autonomy or functional authority prevents necessary collective action.

### 81.5 Rights Failure

The architecture permits legitimate organisational functions to overwhelm protected participants.

### 81.6 Epistemic Failure

The system cannot reliably distinguish genuine evidence from mistaken, manipulated or strategically manufactured claims.

### 81.7 Responsibility Failure

Authority can be exercised while responsibility disappears or becomes impossible to attribute.

### 81.8 Scale Failure

The architecture works for small teams but fails when applied to large institutions or civilisation-scale systems.

### 81.9 Temporal Failure

The architecture works under ordinary decision times but fails when action must occur faster than review.

### 81.10 Multisubstrate Failure

The architecture assumes roughly human capabilities and breaks when participants differ radically in speed, intelligence, persistence, embodiment or ability to duplicate.

The following sections test these possibilities.

---

# 82. Test One — Military Command Under Immediate Threat

Consider a military unit under attack.

The commander orders:

> "Move to the eastern defensive position immediately."

A soldier believes the western position is safer.

If personal autonomy permits every soldier to independently determine whether to obey, coordinated defence may collapse.

The architecture therefore cannot mean:

> **Equal Standing → Optional Compliance**

This confirms an earlier principle:

> **Lateral Standing ≠ Optional Compliance With Legitimate Activated Authority.**

The soldier voluntarily participating in the military wrapper may have accepted legitimate command authority for precisely this class of decision.

The relevant chain may be:

**Military Context**  
→ **Immediate Operational Threat**  
→ **Command Function Required**  
→ **Commander Has Functional Jurisdiction**  
→ **Command Authority Activates**  
→ **Order Falls Within Legitimate Mission and Constraints**  
→ **Obligation to Comply**

The soldier's equal fundamental standing remains intact.

But equality does not erase legitimate obligations created by the context.

Thus:

> **Equal Standing ≠ Symmetrical Authority in Every Context**

The architecture survives this first attack.

---

# 83. Test Two — The Soldier Has Better Local Information

Now alter the case.

The commander orders movement east based upon a map.

The soldier can physically see that the eastern route has just been destroyed.

Strict rank obedience would require following an order that reality has rendered obsolete.

The architecture therefore needs to preserve local epistemic input.

The soldier might respond:

> "Eastern route destroyed. Request alternate route."

If communication exists, the command function can update.

But suppose there is no time.

The soldier must act.

This exposes a distinction between:

- **legitimate command authority;**
- **obsolete command information.**

The command may have been legitimate when issued.

Reality has changed.

Therefore:

> **Legitimate Authority ≠ Infallible Instruction**

and:

> **Changed Reality Can Alter the Legitimate Execution of a Previously Legitimate Instruction.**

This does not imply unrestricted soldier discretion.

The participant should remain oriented toward the commander's legitimate objective.

The relevant question becomes:

> **What action best preserves the legitimate function when literal compliance has become impossible or clearly inconsistent with observed reality?**

A candidate principle emerges:

> **Where changed or directly observed conditions make literal execution of a legitimate instruction impossible or clearly incompatible with the instruction's legitimate function, an appropriately situated participant may exercise bounded adaptive discretion directed toward preserving that legitimate function, subject to subsequent accountability.**

This resembles emergency assumed authority.

The participant gains autonomy at the unavoidable decision point and accepts corresponding responsibility.

Thus:

> **Local Adaptive Discretion Carries Local Responsibility.**

---

# 84. Test Three — Mistaken Local Observation

Now suppose the soldier is wrong.

The eastern route only appeared destroyed.

The soldier disobeys, and the alternative route causes casualties.

Does distributed autonomy destroy command discipline?

Not necessarily.

The architecture does not promise consequence-free refusal.

The soldier's decision must be evaluated against:

- information reasonably available;
- competence;
- urgency;
- severity of expected consequences;
- ability to communicate;
- plausibility of observation;
- legitimate mission;
- available alternatives.

The question is not simply:

> "Was the soldier ultimately correct?"

It is also:

> **Was the decision reasonable given the state of knowledge and responsibility at the time?**

This is important throughout the Concord.

Otherwise retrospective knowledge creates impossible standards.

Thus:

> **Outcome Error ≠ Automatically Illegitimate Decision**

and:

> **Good-Faith Protective or Adaptive Action ≠ Immunity From Review**

The participant carries responsibility for exercising the autonomy.

The system carries responsibility for evaluating that exercise fairly.

---

# 85. Test Four — Manifestly Illegitimate Military Order

A commander orders soldiers to deliberately kill non-threatening civilians.

The order travels correctly through the command topology.

If rank establishes legitimacy, soldiers must comply.

The Concord architecture rejects this.

The military wrapper exists beneath the ethical and constitutional surface.

Therefore:

> **Correct Command Provenance ≠ Legitimate Command Content**

and:

> **Military Wrapper Authority ≠ Constitutional Exemption**

Protective refusal may activate.

The soldier's refusal does not imply that the soldier has become commander.

It means the command function has attempted to cross a higher-order boundary.

Thus:

> **Protective Refusal Can Constrain a Legitimate Authority-Holder Without Transferring General Authority to the Refusing Participant.**

The architecture survives this test conceptually.

Operational difficulty remains: not every illegitimate order will be obvious.

---

# 86. Test Five — Ambiguous Military Legitimacy

Suppose the target might contain combatants and civilians.

The commander possesses intelligence unavailable to the soldier.

The soldier suspects the attack is illegitimate but cannot know.

Unlimited protective refusal could make military operations impossible.

Unlimited obedience could permit atrocity.

The architecture cannot eliminate this uncertainty.

It can only structure it.

Relevant variables include:

- seriousness of potential violation;
- evidential confidence;
- participant's information position;
- commander's information advantage;
- reversibility;
- time available;
- availability of legal or specialist review;
- whether the order is clearly outside ordinary legitimate military function.

This exposes a general requirement:

> **Protective Thresholds Should Reflect Both Consequence Severity and Epistemic Position.**

A participant with weak information may legitimately question without possessing sufficient basis for unilateral permanent veto.

A participant with direct evidence of an extreme violation may possess much stronger refusal authority.

Therefore protective capability should be graded rather than binary.

Possible levels include:

**Question**  
→ **Request Clarification**  
→ **Warn**  
→ **Request Review**  
→ **Temporary Pause**  
→ **Refuse Personal Participation**  
→ **Prevent Action Where Sufficiently Justified**  
→ **Emergency Intervention**

This is a significant refinement.

> **Protective Authority Is a Capability Gradient, Not Necessarily a Binary Veto.**

---

# 87. Test Six — Conscription

Voluntary military service fits contextual consent relatively well.

Conscription does not.

A conscript has not voluntarily entered the military wrapper.

If the entire military authority model depends upon consent, it fails here.

Therefore consent cannot be the sole legitimate basis of contextual authority.

Possible alternative bases include:

- constitutional obligation;
- collective defence necessity;
- emergency necessity;
- reciprocal civic obligation;
- legitimate law.

But each is dangerous because states historically use claimed necessity to manufacture coercive authority.

The Concord must therefore distinguish:

> **Consent-Based Authority**

from:

> **Non-Consensual Authority Requiring Independent Legitimation**

Conscription would require a substantially stronger justification burden because it overrides personal autonomy rather than operating principally through voluntarily accepted obligations.

Thus:

> **Absence of Consent Does Not Make All Authority Impossible; It Increases the Burden of Independent Legitimation.**

This is important far beyond military service.

Examples include:

- arrest;
- quarantine;
- emergency intervention;
- taxation;
- court orders;
- child protection;
- compulsory evacuation under some conditions.

The architecture survives only if consent remains one legitimate basis among several rather than becoming universal.

---

# 88. Test Seven — Manufactured Emergency

Suppose a government or commander discovers that emergency conditions activate stronger authority.

A predictable capture strategy follows:

> manufacture or exaggerate emergency → activate exceptional authority → prolong emergency → retain capability.

This is one of the oldest mechanisms of authority expansion.

The architecture therefore requires:

> **Emergency Declaration ≠ Self-Validating Emergency Authority**

and:

> **An Authority-Holder Should Not Be the Sole Authority Determining Whether the Condition Activating Its Own Exceptional Capability Exists.**

Where immediate action prevents prior independent review, retrospective review should strengthen.

Thus:

> **Reduced Prospective Oversight Should Produce Increased Retrospective Accountability.**

And:

> **Emergency Authority Should Contain Its Own Termination Logic Wherever Practicable.**

Potential mechanisms include:

- explicit activation conditions;
- time limits;
- independent confirmation;
- automatic review;
- scope limitation;
- post-event responsibility review;
- prohibition against self-extension without additional justification.

The architecture therefore survives only by maintaining the anti-self-justification rule already present in MNC.

---

# 89. Test Eight — Police Stop

A police officer instructs a participant to stop.

The participant did not consent to police authority.

The officer's authority therefore cannot arise from contextual consent.

It must arise from a legitimate civil function.

The chain becomes:

**Constitutionally Legitimate Public-Safety / Law Function**  
→ **Specified Police Function**  
→ **Relevant Legal Condition**  
→ **Authority Activation**  
→ **Minimum Necessary Capability**  
→ **Rights Constraints**  
→ **Exercise**  
→ **Responsibility / Review**

Thus:

> **Police Status ≠ Continuously Activated Coercive Authority**

The officer's role creates standing potential authority.

The situation must activate the relevant capability.

This means:

> **Uniform ≠ Universal Permission**

and:

> **Police Role ≠ General Authority Over Participants**

The architecture handles policing better as functionally activated authority than as permanent elevated status.

---

# 90. Test Nine — Police Officer Mistaken About the Situation

Suppose an officer reasonably but incorrectly believes a participant committed an offence.

The officer stops them.

The participant knows they are innocent.

Can the participant simply reject the officer's authority?

If yes, policing becomes practically impossible because every participant can self-declare the authority illegitimate.

If no, innocent participants can be temporarily constrained by mistaken authority.

This reveals an uncomfortable but unavoidable distinction:

> **Legitimate Authority Can Be Exercised on Incorrect Information.**

Legitimacy therefore cannot require factual infallibility.

Instead, legitimacy may depend partly upon whether:

- the function itself is legitimate;
- activation conditions were reasonably believed to exist;
- the capability used was proportionate;
- correction and review exist;
- errors produce remedy where appropriate.

Thus:

> **Mistaken Activation ≠ Automatically Abusive Authority**

But neither does reasonable mistake erase consequences.

The system must distinguish:

- good-faith reasonable error;
- negligent error;
- reckless error;
- fabricated justification;
- discriminatory or strategic misuse.

This is a Layer 0 and accountability problem.

---

# 91. Test Ten — Police Authority and Protective Autonomy

Suppose the officer uses clearly excessive force.

Other officers are present.

A conventional solidarity hierarchy may discourage intervention.

The Concord architecture implies that those officers can possess protective authority that cuts laterally across the immediate enforcement function.

Thus:

> **Shared Institutional Membership ≠ Obligation to Protect Illegitimate Exercise of Colleague Authority**

A subordinate officer might legitimately intervene against a senior officer.

Again:

> **Lower Rank in Coordination Topology ≠ Lower Protective Authority in Every Context**

The test therefore supports distributed protective capability.

But it also raises responsibility:

> **Possessing a protective function may create responsibility to act where qualifying thresholds are reached.**

Protective authority may therefore sometimes be not merely permission but **duty**.

This needs careful development.

---

# 92. Test Eleven — Judiciary

A judge can issue orders affecting participants who did not personally consent to that judge.

Judicial authority derives from constitutional/legal function rather than personal superiority.

The judge may possess substantial decision authority while remaining constitutionally constrained.

This is strongly compatible with burden-bearing authority.

The judge's authority exists because someone must carry responsibility for adjudication.

The judge does not own the dispute.

Thus:

> **Adjudicative Authority ≠ Personal Sovereignty**

The judge may also be protected from managerial interference.

A court administrator might occupy a higher administrative position concerning budgets or staffing while possessing no authority to dictate the judge's decision in a case.

This strongly validates multiple authority topologies.

> **Administrative Authority ≠ Adjudicative Authority**

The judiciary therefore provides one of the cleaner demonstrations of lateral functional authority.

---

# 93. Test Twelve — Corrupt Judge

Suppose the judge deliberately abuses adjudicative authority.

If judicial independence means nobody may interfere, the function becomes sovereign.

Therefore:

> **Functional Independence ≠ Absence of Accountability**

A judge may need decisional independence from ordinary management while remaining subject to:

- appeal;
- constitutional review;
- misconduct processes;
- evidentiary rules;
- procedural requirements;
- removal mechanisms under sufficiently serious conditions.

This illustrates an important general distinction:

> **Independence From One Authority Topology ≠ Independence From All Review Topologies**

The same principle can apply to:

- scientific bodies;
- safety functions;
- auditors;
- AI guardians;
- constitutional reviewers.

Independence should be function-specific.

---

# 94. Test Thirteen — Emergency Medicine and Incapacity

An unconscious participant arrives with life-threatening injuries.

They cannot consent.

Medical professionals must act.

The architecture already contains:

> **Assumed Authority ≠ Assumed Consent**

The authority arises from emergency function and fiduciary responsibility, not fictional consent.

A possible chain is:

**Participant Incapacitated**  
→ **Serious Immediate Threat**  
→ **Known Prior Wishes Checked Where Practicable**  
→ **Emergency Fiduciary Function Activates**  
→ **Minimum Necessary Medical Authority**  
→ **Treatment**  
→ **Responsibility**  
→ **Participant Regains Capacity**  
→ **Substituted Authority Contracts or Ends**

This supports:

> **Inability to Give Present Consent ≠ Erasure of Underlying Autonomy**

and:

> **Inability to Give Present Consent ≠ Permission to Ignore Known Prior Refusal**

The authority-holder carries a burden on behalf of the participant.

This strongly fits the burden-bearing interpretation.

---

# 95. Test Fourteen — Capable Patient Refuses Treatment

A capable adult refuses a treatment likely to save their life.

The doctor believes refusal is irrational.

If professional expertise creates authority over the patient's body, personal autonomy disappears.

The architecture instead distinguishes:

**Epistemic Authority / Expertise**  
from  
**Decision Authority Over Another Participant's Body**

The doctor may possess strong epistemic weight.

They may explain, advise and warn.

But:

> **Protective Advice ≠ Protective Authority**

where the capable participant retains primary decision authority.

Thus:

> **Expertise Concerning Consequences ≠ Automatic Authority to Choose Another Capable Participant's Values or Risk Acceptance.**

This strongly supports Personal Safety and Rights Autonomy.

---

# 96. Test Fifteen — Questionable Capacity

Now suppose the patient's decision capacity is uncertain.

The binary model:

**capable → autonomous**  
**incapable → substitute authority**

is too crude.

Capacity may be:

- domain-specific;
- temporary;
- fluctuating;
- impaired by medication;
- impaired by fear;
- affected by cognitive state;
- partially supported by assistance.

Therefore:

> **Decision Capacity May Be Contextual and Graduated Rather Than Globally Binary.**

The Concord should prefer preserving as much participant autonomy as the actual capacity permits.

This suggests:

> **Substituted Authority Should Not Exceed the Decision-Capacity Deficit That Justifies It.**

That is effectively MNC applied to fiduciary authority.

A participant requiring help understanding one medical decision does not thereby lose general autonomy.

---

# 97. Test Sixteen — Children

Children expose the same issue developmentally.

A young child cannot exercise every adult decision capability.

Parents or guardians therefore possess legitimate fiduciary authority.

But if parental authority is treated as ownership, the child's underlying standing disappears.

The burden model instead gives:

> **Parental Authority Is Responsibility-Bearing Fiduciary Capability, Not Ownership of the Child.**

As the child's relevant capacity develops:

**Increasing Capacity**  
→ **Increasing Participatory Autonomy**  
→ **Reduced Need for Substituted Authority**

Thus:

> **Developmental Authority Should Contract as the Legitimate Need for Substitution Contracts.**

This is strongly compatible with the Concord's Developmental Priority principle.

But difficult questions remain:

- Who evaluates capacity?
- What if parents deliberately suppress development?
- What if child and guardian disagree about serious risk?
- What rights are non-waivable?
- How does AI developmental emergence map onto this?

These remain open.

---

# 98. Test Seventeen — Abusive Fiduciary Authority

A guardian claims:

> "I know what is best for this dependent participant."

Because fiduciary authority exists specifically where the protected participant has reduced ability to resist, it is highly capture-prone.

The burden model therefore implies **greater**, not lesser, scrutiny.

Thus:

> **Greater Dependency → Greater Fiduciary Responsibility → Stronger Need for Appropriate Safeguards**

This is an inversion of a dangerous conventional pattern where the least capable participants receive the weakest practical control over authority exercised against them.

Potential safeguards include:

- rights that survive incapacity;
- independent advocacy;
- review;
- least-restrictive alternatives;
- capacity reassessment;
- conflict-of-interest controls;
- responsibility provenance.

The architecture survives conceptually but requires substantial implementation work.

---

# 99. Test Eighteen — Employer and Employee

An employee voluntarily accepts a role.

Does this mean the employer gains authority over them?

Only within legitimate employment functions.

Thus:

> **Employment Contract ≠ General Submission**

The employee may accept:

- work allocation;
- schedules within agreed bounds;
- quality standards;
- legitimate workplace rules;
- coordination;
- review.

But managerial authority remains bounded by:

- role;
- contract;
- rights;
- law;
- safety;
- legitimate organisational purpose.

The employee's personal life does not become organisational property.

This supports the bounded-team wrapper.

---

# 100. Test Nineteen — “Do It or You're Fired”

Formal voluntariness can conceal dependency.

Suppose a worker is instructed to perform dangerous work.

Technically:

> "They can refuse and leave."

But losing employment may threaten housing, healthcare, family welfare or immigration status.

Therefore:

> **Formal Exit Availability ≠ Meaningful Freedom From Coercion**

This is a serious attack on consent-based wrapper reasoning.

Contextual consent must consider dependency.

A participant may formally consent while possessing very weak practical alternatives.

Thus:

> **Formal Consent ≠ Automatically Legitimate Capability Basis**

The Concord needs to distinguish voluntary agreement from agreement produced by excessive dependency or coercive leverage.

This connects directly to effective capability:

> **Dependency Can Generate Effective Power Without Formal Authority.**

The buoyancy model should therefore include economic and infrastructural dependency as sources of authority pressure.

---

# 101. Test Twenty — Worker Falsely Claims Unsafe Conditions

Return to the machine.

The operator dislikes the assigned task and falsely claims:

> "The machine is unsafe."

Protective autonomy stops production.

If every such claim is automatically protected indefinitely, malicious participants can exploit the system.

The architecture therefore requires responsibility for protective claims.

The operator's refusal activates:

- protective authority;
- responsibility for the assertion;
- review.

Thus:

> **Protective Authority ≠ Responsibility-Free Veto**

If maintenance determines the machine is safe, several possibilities remain:

- the operator made a reasonable good-faith mistake;
- the operator lacked competence but reasonably perceived danger;
- the operator acted negligently;
- the operator knowingly fabricated the concern.

These are not equivalent.

The system should evaluate the claim against the information reasonably available at the time.

Deliberate fabrication may itself violate legitimate responsibilities.

This allows distributed resistance without making deception consequence-free.

---

# 102. Test Twenty-One — Management Retaliates Against Genuine Refusal

Suppose the machine really is unsafe.

The worker refuses.

Management formally accepts the refusal but later denies promotion.

No formal disciplinary action occurs.

The architecture may appear compliant while effective authority pressure remains.

This validates:

> **Formal Protective Authority ≠ Effective Protective Authority**

Layer 0 therefore needs to observe longitudinal outcomes.

Potential signals include:

- promotion disparities;
- dismissal patterns;
- assignment changes;
- social exclusion;
- performance-review anomalies;
- whistleblower outcomes.

However, this creates a privacy and surveillance danger.

Protecting participants from hidden retaliation could itself justify intrusive monitoring.

Therefore:

> **Anti-Retaliation Observation Must Itself Satisfy Minimum Necessary Capability.**

The safeguard cannot become a universal surveillance system.

---

# 103. Test Twenty-Two — The Incompetent Manager

A manager possesses legitimate coordination responsibility but insufficient domain understanding.

They repeatedly make poor decisions while remaining within formal authority.

The architecture says:

> **Appointment to Function ≠ Demonstrated Fitness for Function**

But who decides that the manager is incompetent?

Subordinates may dislike difficult but correct decisions.

Peers may protect one another.

Senior management may lack local information.

Performance metrics may be Goodharted.

The architecture therefore needs multiple evidence channels rather than one popularity or performance measure.

Possible inputs include:

- outcomes;
- specialist review;
- participant feedback;
- error rates;
- responsibility history;
- decision provenance;
- observed response to contradiction;
- ability to recognise functional boundaries.

No single measure should automatically become sovereign.

This is a Reality Tree / epistemic problem as much as an authority problem.

---

# 104. Test Twenty-Three — The Brilliant but Abusive Manager

Suppose a manager consistently achieves exceptional outcomes but:

- humiliates participants;
- suppresses dissent;
- crosses functional boundaries;
- retaliates against refusal;
- creates dependency.

A purely outcome-based system might promote them.

The Concord cannot.

Reality has more than one relevant dimension.

Production success does not erase rights violations.

Thus:

> **Functional Success ≠ Constitutional Legitimacy**

and:

> **Performance Excellence ≠ Permission to Violate Rights**

This demonstrates why ethics and reality form different constraints.

The manager may be empirically effective at production while constitutionally unacceptable.

---

# 105. Test Twenty-Four — The Kind but Incompetent Manager

Reverse the case.

A manager is respectful, supportive and ethically motivated but repeatedly makes decisions that cause serious operational failure.

Good intentions cannot substitute for competence.

Thus:

> **Ethical Intent ≠ Functional Competence**

and:

> **Benevolent Leadership ≠ Sufficient Leadership**

The burden-bearing model requires ability to bear the weight, not merely willingness.

This is important because "servant leadership" language can otherwise become sentimental.

A steward must be both appropriately oriented **and capable**.

---

# 106. Test Twenty-Five — Nobody Wants the Burden

If authority becomes responsibility, scrutiny and accountability rather than privilege, perhaps too few participants seek leadership.

This is a genuine possible failure.

A civilisation needs people willing to carry difficult burdens.

Removing domination incentives does not guarantee sufficient stewardship supply.

The Concord may therefore legitimately provide positive rewards for responsibility, including:

- compensation;
- recognition;
- prestige;
- rest;
- support;
- education;
- resources;
- social appreciation.

Thus:

> **Authority Need Not Be a Privilege for Responsibility to Be Rewarded.**

The system should distinguish:

> **Reward for Carrying Responsibility**

from:

> **Personal Sovereignty as Reward**

This preserves incentives without turning authority itself into the prize.

---

# 107. Test Twenty-Six — Responsibility Overload

A highly capable participant repeatedly accepts more responsibility.

Because they are reliable, the organisation continually adds functions.

Eventually they become a single point of failure.

The inverted ladder can therefore produce its own capture risk:

> **Burden Concentration**

The participant may not seek power at all.

The system may nevertheless become dependent upon them.

Thus:

> **Benevolent Responsibility Accumulation Can Produce Effective Power Concentration.**

This is critical.

Anti-capture must not only monitor malicious authority accumulation.

It must monitor dependency generated by competence and reliability.

Therefore:

> **Trusted Stewardship ≠ Safe Unlimited Stewardship**

and:

> **Systemic Dependency Can Create Authority Even Where Authority Was Not Sought.**

The architecture should encourage redundancy, delegation and succession.

---

# 108. Test Twenty-Seven — Founder Problem

A founder possesses extraordinary historical knowledge and legitimacy within a project.

Participants defer to the founder even where no formal authority exists.

The founder may sincerely reject sovereignty.

Nevertheless, effective authority can emerge.

Thus:

> **Foundership ≠ Sovereignty**

and:

> **Disclaimed Authority ≠ Absence of Effective Authority**

This is directly relevant to the Concord itself.

The architecture must survive its founder.

A system whose principles work only while its founder remains benevolent has failed.

Therefore the Concord should preserve:

- provenance;
- attribution;
- historical recognition;

without converting authorship into constitutional ownership.

---

# 109. Test Twenty-Eight — Democratic Election

An elected representative wins a large majority.

Does democratic support create broad authority?

It creates a legitimate basis for specified representative functions.

It does not create unlimited sovereignty.

Thus:

> **Electoral Mandate ≠ Unlimited Authority**

The representative's authority remains bounded by:

- constitutional rights;
- jurisdiction;
- function;
- term;
- applicable law;
- institutional checks;
- legitimate scope of representation.

Democracy determines an important source of legitimacy.

It does not erase the MNC chain.

---

# 110. Test Twenty-Nine — Majority Wants a Rights Violation

Suppose 80% of voters support depriving a minority of a fundamental right.

If democratic authority sits above rights, the action becomes legitimate.

The inverted architecture places fundamental rights above governmental function.

Therefore:

> **Majority Preference ≠ Automatic Constitutional Legitimacy**

This does not solve every constitutional dispute because rights themselves require interpretation.

But it prevents democratic procedure from becoming the sole arbiter of legitimacy.

The people do not collectively acquire unlimited authority over every individual merely by voting.

---

# 111. Test Thirty — Constitutional Guardians Become Rulers

Suppose a constitutional court or guardian function can block democratic decisions.

The guardian may gradually become the true sovereign.

This is a classic functional-capture problem.

The function intended to protect the surface begins determining everything beneath it.

Therefore:

> **Constitutional Review Authority ≠ General Governing Authority**

The guardian's function must itself be bounded.

A court may determine constitutional compatibility without thereby acquiring ordinary legislative or executive authority.

Again:

> **Function Must Not Become the New Rank.**

And:

> **A Protective Function Must Not Convert Its Veto Capability Into General Policy Sovereignty.**

This is one of the hardest design problems in constitutional systems.

The Concord does not yet have a complete solution.

---

# 112. Test Thirty-One — The Expert Technocracy

Suppose technical experts are consistently more accurate than elected representatives.

Why not simply let experts govern?

Because:

> **Epistemic Weight ≠ Political Authority**

Experts may know more about consequences.

They do not automatically possess authority to determine participants' values, rights or legitimate trade-offs.

Thus:

> **Expertise Can Inform Decision Authority Without Automatically Becoming Decision Sovereignty.**

A useful formulation is:

> **Listen broadly; decide legitimately.**

This preserves expertise without technocracy.

---

# 113. Test Thirty-Two — Politicians Ignore Expertise

Reverse the case.

Elected leaders repeatedly ignore engineers, scientists or safety experts because:

> "We have the democratic mandate."

A bridge collapses.

Democratic legitimacy cannot overrule physical reality.

Thus:

> **Political Authority ≠ Epistemic Supremacy**

The authority-holder remains responsible for incorporating relevant evidence.

This reconnects to:

> **Let reality be the final arbiter.**

A legitimate decision process can still produce a physically impossible decision.

Reality will reject it regardless of political legitimacy.

---

# 114. Test Thirty-Three — Hazardous Industrial Facility

Consider a nuclear facility.

Strong coordination, technical specialisation and safety constraints are essential.

A technician detects an abnormal condition.

Production management wants continued operation.

The architecture gives the technician or safety function potential stop authority.

This appears straightforward.

But suppose unnecessary shutdown itself creates serious risk.

Then:

> **Safety ≠ Always Stop**

The relevant safety function may require expert judgement concerning competing risks.

This demonstrates:

> **Protective Function ≠ Simplistic Precaution**

Protective authority requires competence appropriate to the intervention.

The safest immediate action may sometimes be continued operation.

Therefore:

> **Protective Authority Must Remain Coupled to Relevant Competence Where the Protective Decision Itself Creates Significant Risk.**

This is an important qualification.

---

# 115. Test Thirty-Four — Safety Department Captures the Organisation

A safety function can prevent dangerous action.

Over time it expands its interpretation of safety until almost every organisational decision requires its approval.

The organisation becomes unable to function.

This is:

> **Protective Function Creep**

The safety function has become the new rank.

Therefore:

> **Protective Priority in Relevant Decisions ≠ Universal Organisational Priority**

Safety authority should apply to actual safety questions within legitimate jurisdiction.

The function must not redefine every preference as safety.

Again:

> **Claimed Functional Relevance ≠ Established Functional Jurisdiction**

---

# 116. Test Thirty-Five — Competing Safety Functions

An engineering safety team says:

> "Shut the reactor down."

A grid-stability team says:

> "Immediate shutdown risks regional grid collapse."

A medical infrastructure function says:

> "Grid collapse threatens hospitals."

All three invoke safety.

The architecture cannot resolve this by saying:

> "Safety overrides production."

There are now multiple safety functions with conflicting consequences.

This exposes a limit of simple Functional Priority.

Sometimes there is no predetermined priority.

The system needs:

> **Functional Collision Resolution**

Potential mechanisms include:

- joint competent assessment;
- escalation to a broader integrative function;
- pre-defined emergency protocols;
- probabilistic risk comparison;
- reversible temporary actions;
- independent adjudication where time permits.

The key is that the resolving function must not become permanently superior to all contributing functions.

Thus:

> **Functional Collision May Require Temporary Integrative Authority Without Creating Permanent Personal Hierarchy.**

This deserves separate architectural development.

# 117. Test Thirty-Six — Aircraft Captain Versus Engineer

A captain wants to depart.

The captain has flight command authority.

The engineer has airworthiness authority.

The aircraft does not depart.

This validates:

> **Command Authority ≠ Universal Override Authority**

and:

> **Lower Organisational Position ≠ Lower Decision Authority in Every Domain**

But now suppose the engineer clears the aircraft and the captain independently believes it unsafe to fly.

Does the engineer's clearance compel departure?

No.

The engineer determines:

> **airworthiness within engineering jurisdiction**

The captain may still possess operational responsibility for accepting the flight.

Thus multiple permissions may be required.

This suggests:

> **Some High-Consequence Actions Require Convergent Authority Rather Than Singular Authority.**

That is an important addition.

Not every decision should have one final decider.

Some may require multiple independent functional conditions to be satisfied.

# 118. Test Thirty-Seven — The Spacecraft

A spacecraft is an extreme bounded high-dependency context.

- cannot immediately exit;
- depend upon shared life-support systems;
- may face communication delay;
- may need rapid local decision-making;
- may possess radically specialised functions.

Earth-based command may issue instructions based upon delayed information.

Local crew observes changed conditions.

The architecture strongly favours:

> **Contextual Local Autonomy Under Communication Constraint**

This is a case where authority may need to migrate toward participants closest to reality as communication delay increases.

A candidate principle is:

> **Where external authority cannot obtain sufficiently current information or respond within the decision horizon, legitimate operational discretion should migrate toward competent participants with better local access to reality, bounded by the wider mission and constitutional constraints.**

This is subsidiarity derived from epistemic latency.

# 119. Test Thirty-Eight — Ship Captain and Immediate Necessity

- fragmented decision-making can kill everyone.

The architecture therefore permits very strong authority where reality genuinely requires it.

This is important.

In some contexts, MNC may justify strong command.

> **Minimum Necessary Capability Can Be Substantial Capability.**

# 122. Test Forty-One — Dangerous Driving and Personal Autonomy

> "Should government protect this person from themselves?"

It becomes:

> **"Does this participant's exercise of autonomy materially interfere with the legitimate safety and autonomy of others?"**

> **Autonomy Over Self ≠ Authority Over Shared Infrastructure**

This supports decision decomposition.

# 123. Test Forty-Two — Rescue Externality

- probability of rescue need;
- magnitude of imposed risk;
- whether rescue is optional;
- whether warnings were clear;
- whether participant assumed explicit responsibilities;
- whether the activity consumes scarce emergency resources during crisis.

This remains a difficult externality problem.

The architecture provides a test but not a universal answer.

# 124. Test Forty-Three — Public Health Quarantine

- the externality is sufficiently serious;
- causally attributable;
- evidence-supported;
- less restrictive alternatives are insufficient;
- capability is proportionate;
- review exists.

Thus:

> **Personal Autonomy Can Be Bounded Where Its Exercise Creates Sufficiently Serious Conflict With the Rights or Safety of Others.**

But the public-health authority cannot simply invoke "safety."

The burden of justification increases with intrusion.

Thus:

> **Greater Rights Intrusion Requires Stronger Justification and Safeguards.**

# 129. Test Forty-Eight — Team A Invades Team B Through Shared Management

# 164. Test Eighty-Three — Responsibility for Refusing

The machine operator refuses unsafe work.

Maintenance later proves the machine safe.

Should the operator be punished?

Not merely because the concern was wrong.

The relevant question is whether the refusal was reasonable.

But suppose the operator knowingly lied.

Then responsibility attaches.

This confirms the user's state-machine insight:

> **Activation of Protective Autonomy Also Activates Responsibility for Its Exercise.**

That is crucial.

Distributed resistance is therefore not:

> "I say no and the system must obey me."

It is:

> **"I possess bounded capability to interrupt this function under qualifying conditions, and I accept responsibility for exercising that capability."**

This may be one of the strongest safeguards against frivolous veto.

---

# 165. Test Eighty-Four — Responsibility Persists After the Ball Moves

The operator raises the safety concern.

The manager escalates.

Maintenance investigates.

The issue is now with maintenance.

Has the operator's responsibility disappeared?

No.

The operator retains responsibility for the original assertion.

The manager retains responsibility for the escalation decision.

Maintenance acquires responsibility for the technical determination.

Thus:

> **Transfer of Functional Initiative ≠ Transfer of All Prior Responsibility**

This confirms the need for **Responsibility Provenance**.

The functional "ball" moves.

The responsibility history accumulates.

---

# 166. Test Eighty-Five — State Invisible to External Observer

A senior manager observes:

> Machine not running.

They know:

> I instructed Manager A to start production.

They infer:

> Manager A failed.

But the actual state may be:

**Production Instruction Issued**  
→ **Operator Safety Refusal**  
→ **Protective Authority Activated**  
→ **Manager Escalated Correctly**  
→ **Maintenance Investigation Pending**

No physical output changed.

The normative state changed substantially.

Therefore:

> **Observable Output State ≠ Complete Authority/Responsibility State**

This strongly supports explicit state representation.

A machine-readable architecture could make visible:

- current function;
- active authority;
- unresolved claim;
- responsibility provenance;
- next legitimate function.

This should be developed separately rather than overloading the present conceptual paper.

---

# 167. Test Eighty-Six — State Machine Becomes Bureaucratic Surveillance

Formalising every authority transition could create an enormous monitoring system.

That would conflict with privacy, autonomy and MNC.

Therefore:

> **Machine-Readable Governance ≠ Universal Activity Logging**

The level of provenance required should scale with consequence.

A casual team decision need not produce the same audit trail as:

- lethal military action;
- constitutional emergency power;
- medical intervention without consent;
- shutdown of critical infrastructure.

Thus:

> **Capability Provenance Should Be Proportionate to Capability Consequence.**

This already exists in MNC and applies directly here.

---

# 168. Test Eighty-Seven — Participants Game the State Machine

Once authority activation rules are formalised, strategic participants may manipulate the inputs.

For example:

> "If I classify this as an emergency, I gain authority X."

This is Goodharting of governance.

Therefore machine-readable state transitions cannot substitute for reality testing.

> **Formal State Classification ≠ Reality**

Layer 0 must remain capable of challenging the state machine's representation.

This is a critical safeguard.

The state machine is a model.

Reality remains the final empirical arbiter.

---

# 169. Test Eighty-Eight — Authority Holder Creates the Function

A department wants more power.

It identifies a new risk.

It defines a function to address the risk.

It declares itself responsible.

It requests authority.

It then cites its responsibility as proof that the authority is necessary.

The chain appears formally correct but is circular.

This is a major capture attack.

Therefore:

> **A Capability Holder Should Not Be Permitted to Manufacture Necessity and Then Cite That Necessity as Independent Justification for Expanded Capability.**

The architecture must preserve independent challenge at the:

- problem;
- objective;
- function;
- responsibility;
- capability;

levels.

This is why the full chain matters.

---

# 170. Test Eighty-Nine — Responsibility Is Manufactured to Obtain Authority

A more subtle strategy is:

> "Give me responsibility for X."

Then:

> "I cannot be responsible for X unless you give me authority over Y and Z."

Responsibility itself becomes a vehicle for authority accumulation.

Therefore:

> **Accepted Responsibility ≠ Automatic Entitlement to Any Capability the Responsibility-Holder Requests.**

The function and capability must still be independently tested.

This prevents burden-bearing rhetoric from becoming a new route to power.

---

# 171. Test Ninety — Senior Leader Claims Ultimate Responsibility

A chief executive says:

> "I am ultimately responsible for the whole organisation, therefore I need authority over every decision."

This would recreate the conventional pyramid.

The architecture rejects the inference.

Ultimate responsibility for organisational performance may justify broad coordination and stewardship functions.

It does not automatically transfer every specialist jurisdiction upward.

Thus:

> **Broad Accountability ≠ Universal Decision Jurisdiction**

A senior steward may be responsible for ensuring that competent functions exist without personally possessing the authority of those functions.

This is a crucial distinction.

---

# 172. Test Ninety-One — Responsibility Without Direct Control

This creates a difficult question.

How can a senior steward be responsible for system performance without controlling every function?

The answer may be that responsibility itself is layered.

The steward may be responsible for:

- architecture;
- appointments;
- resources;
- coordination;
- escalation pathways;
- institutional learning.

The specialist remains responsible for specialist decisions.

Thus:

> **Responsibility for the System ≠ Responsibility for Personally Making Every System Decision**

This allows broad stewardship without universal command.

---

# 173. Test Ninety-Two — “The Buck Stops Here”

Conventional leadership often values the idea that one senior person ultimately accepts responsibility.

This can be healthy where it prevents blame-shifting.

But it can become misleading if it erases distributed responsibility.

A Concordian interpretation might be:

> **Senior stewardship carries responsibility for the adequacy of the system and for the legitimate decisions assigned to that stewardship function, without erasing the responsibility of other participants for their own materially relevant decisions and actions.**

Thus:

> **Ultimate Stewardship Responsibility ≠ Exclusive Responsibility**

This preserves both leadership accountability and responsibility provenance.

---

# 174. Test Ninety-Three — Promotion Through Popularity

If managers are selected by those they support, candidates may avoid difficult but necessary decisions to remain popular.

Therefore burden-bearing leadership cannot simply become:

> "The nicest person wins."

Competence, judgement and willingness to carry unpopular legitimate responsibilities remain necessary.

Thus:

> **Supportive Leadership ≠ Pleasing Everyone**

A manager sometimes supports a team by making decisions the team dislikes.

The legitimacy comes from function, evidence and responsibility—not popularity.

---

# 175. Test Ninety-Four — Promotion Through Technical Excellence

The best engineer is promoted to engineering manager.

They are technically brilliant but poor at coordination.

This confirms again that:

> **Competence Is Function-Specific**

Technical achievement is evidence relevant to some responsibilities.

It is not proof of management competence.

Promotion should evaluate the actual function being entered.

---

# 176. Test Ninety-Five — Promotion Through Management Excellence Across Domains

A brilliant hospital administrator becomes head of an aerospace engineering organisation.

They may possess transferable coordination skill but limited domain understanding.

The architecture does not forbid this.

It asks whether:

- the coordination function genuinely requires deep domain knowledge;
- the participant possesses enough relevant understanding;
- specialist functions remain independently authoritative;
- the leader can recognise limits;
- the structure prevents generic managerial authority from overriding specialist competence.

Thus:

> **Transferable Management Competence Can Support Cross-Domain Appointment Without Creating Transferable Domain Authority.**

This is more nuanced than either "professional managers are illegitimate" or "management skill transfers universally."

---

# 177. Test Ninety-Six — Prestige Becomes Shadow Rank

A Nobel-level scientist joins Team A as an ordinary adviser.

Everyone defers to them.

They begin effectively determining decisions outside their assigned function.

No formal authority has changed.

The architecture nevertheless detects:

> **Effective Authority Expansion Through Prestige**

Layer 0 must therefore consider observed influence.

But the Concord cannot forbid people from voluntarily respecting expertise.

The relevant boundary is between:

- persuasion;
- epistemic weight;
- coercive expectation;
- de facto decision control.

This remains difficult to formalise.

It is likely partly cultural.

---

# 178. Test Ninety-Seven — Charismatic Steward

A senior Concord steward becomes enormously admired.

Participants begin treating their preferences as constitutional interpretation.

The steward never asks for this.

Effective sovereignty emerges anyway.

This is another founder/prestige problem.

Potential responses include:

- plural stewardship;
- explicit jurisdiction boundaries;
- independent institutions;
- cultural resistance to personal canonisation;
- transparent provenance;
- term limits where appropriate;
- separation of expertise from decision authority.

No single mechanism is sufficient.

The key principle remains:

> **Respect May Be General; Authority Remains Specific.**

---

# 179. Test Ninety-Eight — Everyone Supports Everyone

The inverted ladder could become conceptually meaningless if every function is described as "support."

A commander supports soldiers.

Soldiers support the mission.

Engineers support management.

Management supports engineers.

The term may cease distinguishing authority relationships.

Therefore "support" should remain a cultural metaphor, not the formal basis of legitimacy.

Formal legitimacy still requires:

- function;
- jurisdiction;
- responsibility;
- capability;
- context;
- constraints.

Thus:

> **Supportive Language ≠ Authority Justification**

This protects the architecture from becoming rhetorical.

---

# 180. Test Ninety-Nine — The Inverted Ladder Becomes Another Ladder

Participants may begin competing to be "lower" because lower positions carry prestige.

The most burden-bearing steward becomes culturally superior.

The hierarchy simply reverses its language.

This is a real sociological possibility.

Therefore:

> **Inverted Organisational Imagery ≠ Elimination of Status Hierarchy**

The Concord should not attempt to eliminate all prestige.

Instead it should prevent prestige from automatically creating jurisdiction.

Thus even if senior stewardship becomes highly respected:

> **Recognition of Burden ≠ Constitutional Elevation of the Bearer**

The ladder metaphor remains explanatory, not ontological.

---

# 181. Test One Hundred — The Ethical Kernel Becomes a Prestige Object

A participant claims uniquely deep understanding of the Ethical Kernel.

Others defer.

Spiritual or philosophical prestige becomes authority.

This recreates priestly sovereignty.

Therefore:

> **Claimed Ethical Insight ≠ Constitutional Authority**

The Ethical Kernel must remain open to reasoned interpretation and challenge.

No participant becomes sovereign merely by claiming superior moral understanding.

---

# 182. Test One Hundred and One — Reality Is Used to Justify Cruelty

A leader says:

> "Reality is harsh. Therefore harsh policy is necessary."

This abuses the empirical principle.

Reality constrains possibilities.

It does not erase ethical choice.

Thus:

> **Descriptive Reality ≠ Normative Permission**

The Concord must resist both:

- denial of reality in the name of ethics;
- abandonment of ethics in the name of reality.

This confirms the dual-boundary model again.

---

# 183. Test One Hundred and Two — Ethics Demands the Impossible

Conversely, an ethical policy requires resources that physically do not exist.

Insisting upon impossible implementation may cause wider harm.

Thus:

> **Normative Desirability ≠ Physical Feasibility**

The civilisation must search for the closest legitimate feasible path rather than pretending constraints do not exist.

This is another reason stewardship sits between ethics and reality.

---

# 184. Test One Hundred and Three — Scarcity

Two participants need one life-saving resource.

Both possess equal standing.

Only one can receive it.

Equality cannot manufacture a second resource.

Authority must make or administer a tragic allocation decision.

This demonstrates that:

> **Equal Standing ≠ Equal Outcome Under Genuine Scarcity**

The decision architecture requires legitimate allocation principles.

Burden-bearing authority means the allocator carries responsibility for the decision.

It does not imply that a morally painless answer always exists.

The Concord must be able to acknowledge tragedy rather than hide it behind procedure.

---

# 185. Test One Hundred and Four — Unpopular Necessary Constraint

A contagious participant must be isolated.

A dangerous structure must be evacuated.

A failing spacecraft module must be sealed.

Participants strongly object.

The architecture permits coercive authority where the legitimate function and evidence justify it.

Thus the inverted model is not anti-coercion in an absolute sense.

It is anti-**unjustified coercion**.

This distinction should be explicit:

> **The Concord Does Not Eliminate Coercive Capability Where Reality and Legitimate Rights Protection Require It; It Demands Stronger Justification, Bounding and Accountability for Its Use.**

---

# 186. Test One Hundred and Five — Rights Require Enforcement

A participant violently attacks another.

The victim's rights cannot be protected merely by recognising both participants' autonomy.

Protective authority may require coercive intervention against the attacker.

Thus:

> **Protection of Autonomy Can Require Constraint of Autonomy**

There is no contradiction if the attacker's exercise of autonomy is violating another participant's rights.

This confirms that rights architecture requires capability, not merely declarations.

---

# 187. Test One Hundred and Six — The Victim Retaliates Excessively

The victim possesses protective authority.

They stop the attacker.

Then continue harming them after the threat ends.

Protective authority has exceeded its function.

Thus:

> **Protective Authority Terminates or Contracts When the Protective Need Terminates or Contracts.**

This is MNC applied dynamically.

A legitimate defensive action can become illegitimate through continuation.

---

# 188. Test One Hundred and Seven — Authority Changes Faster Than Formal Roles

A rapidly changing emergency causes relevant functional authority to move repeatedly:

**Pilot**  
→ **Engineer**  
→ **Medical Officer**  
→ **Emergency Coordinator**  
→ **Pilot**

Static organisational charts cannot represent this well.

The architecture strongly supports dynamic state modelling.

Thus:

> **Authority Topology Can Be Temporally Dynamic Even Where Organisational Roles Are Stable.**

This is one of the clearest reasons for distinguishing invested from activated authority.

---

# 189. Test One Hundred and Eight — Participants Do Not Know Who Has Authority

Dynamic authority can create confusion.

If participants cannot determine whose authority is active, response slows.

Therefore:

> **Legitimate Authority Must Be Sufficiently Legible to Those Expected to Respond to It.**

Machine-readable state could help AI systems.

Human interfaces may require:

- role identifiers;
- clear procedures;
- communication protocols;
- contextual indicators.

Legibility itself becomes a safety function.

---

# 190. Test One Hundred and Nine — False Authority Signalling

A participant falsely claims:

> "I am the emergency coordinator."

Others comply because the role would legitimately possess authority.

Therefore authority systems require provenance and authentication proportionate to consequence.

Thus:

> **Claimed Authority ≠ Verified Authority**

High-consequence authority may require stronger identity and role verification than ordinary coordination.

Again:

> **Capability Provenance Should Be Proportionate to Capability Consequence.**

---

# 191. Test One Hundred and Ten — Too Much Verification Prevents Emergency Action

If every instruction requires cryptographic verification, legal review and multi-party approval, urgent action may become impossible.

Therefore verification itself is bounded by decision horizon.

The architecture must balance:

- authentication;
- speed;
- consequence;
- reversibility;
- retrospective accountability.

Thus:

> **Legitimacy Architecture Must Operate Within the Time Available for the Function It Governs.**

A system that produces perfect legitimacy decisions after everyone is dead has failed operationally.

---

# 192. Test One Hundred and Eleven — Autonomous Emergency Action

This leads to a general emergency pattern:

**Urgency Increases**  
→ **Prospective Review Capacity Decreases**  
→ **Local/Pre-Authorised Autonomy May Increase**  
→ **Immediate Responsibility Increases**  
→ **Retrospective Review Strengthens**

This does not mean emergencies erase constraints.

It means the timing of safeguards changes.

Thus:

> **Autonomy at the moment of unavoidable decision; accountability immediately thereafter.**

This existing MNC formulation survives adversarial testing strongly.

---

# 193. Test One Hundred and Twelve — Retrospective Review Becomes Witch-Hunt

After an emergency, reviewers know what happened.

Participants making the original decision did not.

Hindsight bias can make every unsuccessful choice appear negligent.

Therefore review must reconstruct the **decision state at the time**.

This is another major advantage of authority–responsibility state provenance.

The system should preserve:

- available evidence;
- uncertainty;
- time constraints;
- active functions;
- alternatives known;
- authority state.

Thus:

> **Retrospective Accountability Should Evaluate the Decision From the Information State Reasonably Available When It Was Made, While Still Learning From the Outcome.**

That is important enough to retain.

---

# 194. Test One Hundred and Thirteen — Nobody Reviews the Reviewer

Every review function creates the classic recursion:

> Who reviews the reviewer?

Infinite oversight is impossible.

The Concord therefore cannot solve capture by adding endless vertical review layers.

This strongly supports:

- distributed challenge;
- transparency where legitimate;
- plural independent functions;
- bounded jurisdiction;
- Ratchet evidence;
- ability to fork or peacefully distance where appropriate.

The answer is architectural rather than an infinite hierarchy.

> **Recursive Oversight ≠ Infinite Management Depth**

This is highly relevant to the buoyancy model.

---

# 195. Test One Hundred and Fourteen — Independent Reviewers Share the Same Dependency

Three independent safety bodies all rely on the same data provider.

They agree.

Their agreement appears robust.

But the shared dependency is wrong.

Thus:

> **Geographical or Organisational Plurality ≠ Epistemic Independence**

and:

> **Distributed Structure ≠ Independent Structure**

This is an existing Concord concern and remains essential here.

Authority review must examine dependency topology, not merely institution count.

---

# 196. Test One Hundred and Fifteen — Management Layer Exists Only Because Another Layer Exists

Department A manages reports required by Department B.

Department B exists to review reports generated because Department C requires oversight of Department A.

No original external function remains.

The hierarchy has become self-referential.

This is bureaucratic autopoiesis.

MNC should ask:

> **What external legitimate function ultimately justifies this chain?**

If the answer is merely:

> "The procedure requires it,"

the architecture should permit redesign.

Thus:

> **Internal Procedural Need ≠ Automatically Independent Legitimate Function**

This may be one of the strongest tests for unnecessary authority depth.

---

# 197. Test One Hundred and Sixteen — Removing a Layer Creates Hidden Work

Suppose a management layer appears unnecessary and is removed.

Its coordination work does not disappear.

It is redistributed invisibly onto specialists, reducing their ability to perform primary functions.

Therefore layer-removal tests must include total system burden.

> **Removal of Formal Authority Structure ≠ Removal of the Function It Performed**

This protects MNC from simplistic organisational cutting.

The correct question remains:

> **What is the minimum sufficient structural complexity required to perform the legitimate functions reliably?**

---

# 198. Test One Hundred and Seventeen — Informal Leader Emerges

A formally flat team removes managers.

One charismatic participant becomes the de facto coordinator.

They possess no formal accountability.

The attempt to eliminate hierarchy has created hidden hierarchy.

Thus:

> **Absence of Formal Authority ≠ Absence of Effective Authority**

This strongly supports formalising legitimate functions rather than pretending power can simply be abolished.

A bounded, accountable manager may be safer than an invisible informal ruler.

---

# 199. Test One Hundred and Eighteen — Authority Is Necessary

This adversarial sequence produces an important conclusion.

The principal danger is not authority itself.

Attempts to abolish authority can produce:

- hidden authority;
- informal dominance;
- coordination failure;
- responsibility ambiguity;
- unaccountable influence.

Therefore the Concord should not aim for:

> **No Authority**

It should aim for:

> **Legitimate, Function-Derived, Minimum-Sufficient, Contextually Bounded, Responsibility-Coupled, Reviewable and Terminable Authority.**

That is a materially different objective.

# 202. Provisional Result of the Adversarial Phase

The adversarial tests do not presently reveal a need to restore a universal hierarchy of persons.

This is significant.

Military command, policing, emergency medicine, hazardous industry, judicial authority, fiduciary care, spacecraft operation and AI coordination all require asymmetric authority.

But the tested asymmetries can generally be represented through:

- legitimate function;
- contextual activation;
- functional jurisdiction;
- competence;
- minimum sufficient capability;
- responsibility;
- protective constraints;
- review;
- termination.

The strongest cases for conventional hierarchy turn out to require **strong command topology**, not necessarily superior personal standing.

Likewise, the strongest cases for autonomy do not require eliminating authority.

They require preserving legitimate local decision capability and resistance where relevant.

The architecture therefore continues to support:

> **Equal Fundamental Standing + Differentiated Function + Asymmetric Contextual Authority**

rather than:

> **Equal Standing + Identical Authority**

or:

> **Differentiated Function + Hierarchy of Persons**

This is an important result.

It remains provisional.

The next task is to use the failures and refinements identified above to construct the revised integrated architecture.

That architecture must explain simultaneously:

- why authority exists;
- why authority is a burden;
- how responsibility attaches;
- how promotion works;
- why bounded teams exist;
- how cross-wrapper authority is prevented;
- how strong command remains possible;
- how protective autonomy is distributed;
- how authority pressure accumulates;
- how management depth is justified;
- how reality corrects authority;
- how ethics constrains action;
- how responsibility provenance survives delegation;
- how the entire structure remains multisubstrate-compatible.

Only after that synthesis should the paper attempt its final constitutional and cultural formulations.
