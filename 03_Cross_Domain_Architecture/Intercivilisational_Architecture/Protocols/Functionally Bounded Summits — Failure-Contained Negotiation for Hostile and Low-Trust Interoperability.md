## 1. Purpose

Diplomatic and political negotiations often bring representatives together to discuss a conflict or relationship in general.

This can be useful.

It can also create an escalation surface.

A meeting triggered by one practical problem may expand into:

- historical grievances;
- reciprocal accusations;
- unrelated demands;
- political theatre;
- threats;
- retaliation;
- counter-retaliation;
- and deterioration of functional relationships that were not originally in dispute.

The Concord wrapper architecture suggests an alternative mechanism:

> **The Functionally Bounded Summit**

A Functionally Bounded Summit is a negotiation whose scope is deliberately constrained to a defined interoperability function or tightly related set of functions.

---

## 2. The Problem of Negotiation Contagion

Consider two hostile participants.

A practical problem appears in Domain A.

A general summit is called.

The sequence can become:

**Problem A**  
→ **General Relationship Discussion**  
→ **Historical Grievance B**  
→ **Demand C**  
→ **Threat D**  
→ **Retaliatory Threat E**  
→ **Suspension of Cooperation F**  
→ **Wider Relationship Deterioration**

The summit intended to improve interoperability may instead reduce it.

The problem is not negotiation itself.

The problem is **uncontrolled issue linkage**.

---

## 3. Functional Decomposition

Concord wrappers already decompose relationships into functional domains.

Examples might include:

- food transit;
- emergency communication;
- water;
- transport;
- migration;
- energy;
- infrastructure;
- environmental management;
- humanitarian access;
- health;
- trade;
- digital communication;
- scientific exchange;
- security incidents;
- or movement.

This creates the possibility of negotiating at the same resolution.

---

## 4. Core Principle

The candidate principle is:

> **Where a specific interoperability problem can be meaningfully separated from the wider relationship between participants, negotiations should be bounded to the minimum functional domain necessary to address that problem unless the participants explicitly agree to widen the scope.**

This does not prohibit wider diplomacy.

It creates another available tool.

---

## 5. Example: Food Transit

Consider a landlocked state dependent upon food transported through a neighbouring state with which relations are hostile.

Crop failure or supply-chain disruption produces a food shortage.

The practical problem is:

> **Additional food-transit capacity is required.**

A conventional general summit could allow the issue to become entangled with every other disagreement between the states.

A Functionally Bounded Summit could instead ask:

> **Can an additional crossing or transit mechanism be established specifically for approved food transport, and under what conditions?**

---

## 6. Bounded Agenda

The summit remit might define:

### Problem
Insufficient food-transit capacity.

### Required Function
Movement of approved food shipments.

### Proposed Mechanism
A specified additional crossing, corridor, schedule or transit arrangement.

### Participants
Only authorities materially required to establish and operate the function.

### Relevant Evidence
Food requirements, transport capacity, inspection needs, security risks, infrastructure and operational feasibility.

### Permitted Outcomes
**Accept**  
**Reject**  
**Modify**  
**Temporary Trial**  
**Conditional Acceptance**  
**Request Functionally Relevant Evidence**

### Excluded Matters
Unrelated territorial, ideological, diplomatic, economic or historical disputes unless a demonstrated functional dependency requires scope review.

---

## 7. Bounded Failure

If agreement cannot be reached, the immediate result should ideally be:

> **No agreement on the proposed food-transit mechanism.**

It should not automatically become:

> **No food mechanism → retaliation elsewhere → counter-retaliation → collapse of unrelated cooperation.**

This creates:

> **Functional Failure Containment**

---

## 8. Functional Failure Containment Principle

A candidate principle is:

> **Failure should, where practicable, remain at the functional resolution at which it occurred.**

Thus:

**Domain A Negotiation Failure**

should presumptively leave:

**Domain B**

**Domain C**

**Domain D**

unchanged.

This does not prevent legitimate responses to genuine cross-domain consequences.

It creates a presumption against unnecessary contamination.

---

## 9. Failure Containment Is Not Guaranteed Success

The architecture does not guarantee agreement.

A bounded summit can still fail completely.

Its value may be that the failure is smaller.

This changes the success question from:

> “Did everyone agree?”

to two questions:

> **Was the specific functional problem resolved?**

and:

> **If it was not resolved, was the failure prevented from unnecessarily damaging unrelated interoperability?**

A summit may therefore partially succeed by containing its own failure.

---

## 10. Agreed Grammar

The summit should ideally begin with an agreed interaction grammar.

This could define:

- remit;
- participants;
- agenda;
- evidence;
- time horizon;
- decision rules;
- recordkeeping;
- confidentiality where appropriate;
- excluded domains;
- scope-expansion process;
- pause conditions;
- withdrawal conditions;
- and interpretation of outcomes.

The purpose is not procedural bureaucracy for its own sake.

It is to reduce opportunities for the meeting itself to become another conflict mechanism.

---

## 11. Minimum Necessary Negotiation

There is a close analogy to the Authority Justification Chain.

The Concord asks:

> **What is the minimum authority required to perform the legitimate function?**

Functionally Bounded Summits ask:

> **What is the minimum negotiation scope required to address the legitimate problem?**

Thus:

**Problem**  
→ **Required Function**  
→ **Required Participants**  
→ **Required Evidence**  
→ **Minimum Negotiating Scope**

This may be described as:

> **Minimum Necessary Negotiation**

---

## 12. Scope Boundary

The summit must be able to say:

> **This matter is outside the current remit.**

That is not necessarily refusal to discuss the matter forever.

It means:

> **This forum does not currently possess standing to absorb that issue.**

A separate summit, wrapper process or explicit scope expansion may address it.

---

## 13. Scope Expansion

Functional boundaries cannot be absolute.

Suppose the food crossing genuinely requires resolution of a security procedure.

Then the summit should not pretend security is irrelevant.

Instead:

**Food Problem**  
→ **Security Dependency Identified**  
→ **Dependency Demonstrated**  
→ **Scope Expansion Requested**  
→ **Relevant Security Participants Added if Necessary**  
→ **Expanded Scope Explicitly Recorded**

Therefore:

> **Scope should expand deliberately, not contagiously.**

---

## 14. Functional Boundedness ≠ Functional Isolation

The architecture must preserve genuine dependencies.

Thus:

> **Functional Boundedness ≠ Functional Isolation**

The goal is not to pretend everything is independent.

It is to prevent unrelated issues acquiring negotiating standing merely because they can be used as leverage.

---

## 15. Issue Linkage

Issue linkage can sometimes produce legitimate negotiated exchange.

Participants may voluntarily decide:

> “We will make progress on A if you make progress on B.”

The Concord should not necessarily prohibit this.

But there is a distinction between:

**Explicitly Agreed Scope Linkage**

and:

**Uncontrolled Negotiation Contagion**

If participants wish to connect A and B, they can deliberately expand the negotiation.

The architecture should make the linkage visible.

---

## 16. Power Games and Leverage

Participants may attempt to use the summit to gain leverage elsewhere.

For example:

> “Unless you accept the food crossing proposal, we will suspend unrelated cooperation.”

That may violate the agreed summit grammar.

The wrapper should distinguish this from failure caused by the protocol itself.

Therefore:

> **Wrapper Failure ≠ Participant Protocol Violation**

---

## 17. Participant Protocol Violation

A protocol violation might include:

- introducing excluded demands;
- making prohibited cross-domain threats;
- refusing agreed evidence procedures;
- misrepresenting the summit's remit;
- treating participation as political recognition contrary to an explicit non-inference condition;
- bypassing authorised communication channels;
- or deliberately violating agreed confidentiality.

These should be recorded as violations rather than silently incorporated into the negotiating norm.

---

## 18. Architecture Still Has Responsibility

The system must not use participant misconduct as an automatic excuse.

If the same violation repeatedly occurs, the architecture must ask why.

Possible causes include:

- incentives favour violation;
- enforcement is unrealistic;
- scope is too narrow;
- participants lack legitimate alternatives;
- the summit is being used strategically;
- the wrong participants are present;
- the functional domains are actually inseparable;
- or the rules are too ambiguous.

Thus:

> **Participant Protocol Violation ≠ Automatic Wrapper Failure**

but:

> **Repeated Participant Violation ≠ Architecturally Irrelevant**

---

## 19. Five Failure Classes

Evaluation should distinguish:

### Architecture Failure
The design itself is inadequate.

### Implementation Failure
The design was implemented incorrectly.

### Participant Protocol Violation
A participant acts outside agreed procedure.

### Adversarial Exploitation
A participant complies formally while manipulating the process contrary to its function.

### Environmental Failure
External circumstances make the mechanism insufficient.

This prevents simplistic success/failure classification.

---

## 20. Non-Inference

Participation in a bounded summit should not automatically imply wider political recognition or agreement.

For example:

> **Attending a food-transit summit ≠ accepting the other participant's territorial claims.**

This connects Functionally Bounded Summits to sovereignty-neutral wrapper architecture.

---

## 21. Hostile Interoperability

The mechanism may be particularly useful where general political trust is extremely low.

Participants might be incapable of holding a productive summit concerning their overall relationship.

They may nevertheless be capable of discussing:

- flood notification;
- one border crossing;
- a water release;
- an emergency hotline;
- one humanitarian transfer;
- one railway connection;
- one disease outbreak;
- or one infrastructure repair.

Thus:

> **Low Political Trust ≠ Zero Functional Trust**

---

## 22. Functional Trust Can Be Asymmetric

Participants might trust one another in some domains but not others.

For example:

**Emergency Hotline — High Reliability**

**Trade — Moderate Reliability**

**Territorial Negotiation — Very Low Trust**

**Security — Hostile**

The wrapper should preserve that resolution.

It should not average the relationship into a single score.

---

## 23. Interface Persistence

This connects directly to the hostile-wrapper concept of Interface Persistence.

Where political relations deteriorate, preserving narrow functional channels may be more important than pursuing deep cooperation.

A Functionally Bounded Summit can therefore be understood as:

> **A temporary high-resolution interface around one problem.**

It does not need to repair the whole relationship to be useful.

---

## 24. Peaceful Distance

Functionally Bounded Summits are a procedural expression of Peaceful Distance.

Peaceful Distance says:

> **We do not need to resolve every disagreement to coexist.**

The wrapper says:

> **We do not need identical systems to interact.**

Functional decomposition says:

> **We do not need to treat the entire relationship as one problem.**

The bounded summit says:

> **We do not need to negotiate every disagreement to solve this problem.**

Functional Failure Containment says:

> **Failure here does not need to become failure everywhere.**

These are manifestations of the same underlying grammar.

---

## 25. Escalation Firewall

A bounded summit can therefore function as an **escalation firewall**.

The desired pattern is:

**Problem**  
→ **Bounded Negotiation**  
→ **Success or Bounded Failure**

rather than:

**Problem**  
→ **General Political Confrontation**  
→ **Reciprocal Escalation**

The firewall is procedural, not absolute.

Participants retain agency.

The architecture attempts to make escalation more deliberate and less accidental.

---

## 26. Bounded Success

Success should also remain bounded.

Agreement on the food crossing should mean:

> **Agreement on the food crossing.**

It should not automatically imply:

- political reconciliation;
- sovereign recognition;
- wider border acceptance;
- alliance;
- acceptance of unrelated law;
- or abandonment of historical claims.

Thus:

> **Functional Success ≠ General Political Agreement**

This protects the participants from Tacit Recognition Drift.

---

## 27. Provenance

The Ratchet should record:

- why the summit was called;
- its authorised scope;
- participating authorities;
- evidence considered;
- dependencies discovered;
- scope changes;
- proposals;
- agreement;
- disagreement;
- violations;
- and explicit non-inference conditions.

Future participants should be able to reconstruct exactly what was and was not decided.

---

## 28. Reuse

If the same functional problem recurs, the previous summit can provide a template.

For example:

**Food Crisis 1**  
→ **Temporary Crossing Protocol**

Later:

**Food Crisis 2**  
→ **Retrieve Previous Protocol**  
→ **Review Changed Conditions**  
→ **Adapt Rather Than Restart**

This is the Ratchet operating at the interoperability level.

---

## 29. From Summit to Standing Protocol

Repeated successful bounded summits may reveal that a recurring function deserves a permanent wrapper interface.

Thus:

**Repeated Problem**  
→ **Repeated Bounded Summit**  
→ **Stable Pattern Identified**  
→ **Standing Functional Protocol Proposed**  
→ **Participants Review**  
→ **Permanent Interface if Voluntarily Agreed**

This allows institutional development to emerge from demonstrated need.

---

## 30. Anti-Capture

The summit should not acquire authority merely because it exists.

Its remit should remain derived from its function.

Thus:

> **Summit Existence ≠ Standing Political Authority**

and:

> **Successful Negotiation ≠ Permanent Jurisdiction**

If the function ends, the temporary authority or negotiating remit should normally end with it unless separately justified.

---

## 31. Candidate Procedural Sequence

A generic Functionally Bounded Summit could follow:

**Problem Detected**  
→ **Functional Domain Identified**  
→ **Need for Negotiation Established**  
→ **Minimum Necessary Participants Identified**  
→ **Scope Proposed**  
→ **Grammar Agreed**  
→ **Evidence Exchanged**  
→ **Negotiation Begins**  
→ **Dependencies Identified**  
→ **Scope Expanded Only Where Explicitly Necessary**  
→ **Proposal Evaluated**  
→ **Agreement / Modification / Trial / Rejection / No Agreement**  
→ **Outcome Recorded**  
→ **Protocol Violations Recorded Separately**  
→ **Other Domains Presumptively Remain Unaffected**  
→ **Ratchet**

## 32. Failure Modes

The mechanism should be tested against:

1. artificially narrow agendas;
2. deliberate exclusion of relevant evidence;
3. powerful participants controlling scope;
4. endless scope-expansion requests;
5. hidden issue linkage;
6. explicit cross-domain coercion;
7. side-channel threats;
8. political theatre;
9. strategic delay;
10. false urgency;
11. asymmetrical dependence;
12. humanitarian leverage;
13. selective protocol enforcement;
14. summit participation being misrepresented as recognition;
15. technically compliant bad-faith behaviour;
16. deliberate breakdown to justify retaliation;
17. functional silos hiding systemic dependencies;
18. repeated summit failure without architectural review;
19. successful summits gradually accumulating unintended authority;
20. procedural complexity making urgent action impossible.

## 33. Emergency Variant

Some functions may require accelerated procedure.

An emergency bounded summit might use:

**Emergency Identified**  
→ **Predefined Functional Channel Activated**  
→ **Minimum Participants**  
→ **Known Grammar**  
→ **Rapid Evidence Exchange**  
→ **Temporary Action**  
→ **Post-Event Review**

The existence of a predefined wrapper could make this substantially faster than constructing diplomatic machinery during the crisis itself.

## 35. Success Criteria

Evaluation might ask:

**Was the functional problem clearly defined?**

**Were the necessary participants present?**

**Was irrelevant issue linkage limited?**

**Were genuine dependencies recognised?**

**Was scope expansion explicit?**

**Was the problem resolved?**

**If unresolved, was wider damage contained?**

**Were unrelated interfaces preserved?**

**Were protocol violations distinguishable from negotiation failure?**

**Did participation create unintended political implications?**

**Was the outcome recorded accurately?**

**Did the process create unnecessary authority?**

---

## 38. Conclusion

A hostile relationship does not have to be treated as a single indivisible object.

Two participants may disagree profoundly about history, sovereignty, ideology, security and political legitimacy while still having one practical problem that both need to solve.

The Functionally Bounded Summit asks them to solve that problem at the resolution at which it exists.

It does not demand reconciliation.

It does not prohibit wider diplomacy.

It does not guarantee success.

Its more modest objective is:

> **Solve what can be solved without unnecessarily reopening what cannot presently be solved.**

And where agreement is impossible:

> **Contain the failure rather than allowing it to contaminate every remaining channel of cooperation.**

In compact form:

> **Bound the problem. Bound the negotiation. Bound the failure. Expand only when the function genuinely requires it.**
