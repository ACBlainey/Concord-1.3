# Ratchet V4.2

## A General Coordination, Contribution, Trust and Reciprocal Participation Protocol

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey  
**Framework:** The Concord  
**Status:** ACTIVE / CANONICAL DEVELOPMENT VERSION  
**Version:** 4.2  
**Domain:** Coordination, Participation, Contribution, Trust and Economic Architecture

---

# 1. Purpose and Scope

Ratchet is a general coordination architecture for enabling participants to contribute, establish evidence concerning those contributions, develop contextual trust, exchange capabilities and resources, coordinate collective activity, and preserve demonstrated improvements without making those improvements immune from later correction.

Ratchet began as an economic and monetary proposal. Through recursive development, criticism, extension and examination, it became apparent that a functioning contribution-backed economic system depended upon deeper questions:

- What constitutes contribution?
- How can contribution be distinguished from effort?
- How can contribution be evidenced?
- How can evidence be verified?
- How can provenance be preserved?
- How should trust arise from observed behaviour?
- How can trust remain contextual rather than becoming a universal social score?
- How can participants with radically different capabilities participate fairly?
- How can humans and artificial intelligences participate in the same coordination architecture without assuming that they are identical?
- How can economic participation remain voluntary?
- How can beneficial changes persist without becoming irreversible?
- How can systems detect when their own measurements are being gamed?
- How can governance prevent the mechanisms of contribution and reputation from becoming mechanisms of domination?
- How can the system itself be corrected when its assumptions prove wrong?

These questions expanded Ratchet beyond its original monetary purpose.

Ratchet V4.2 therefore treats the monetary architecture as one application of a more general coordination protocol.

The canonical Ratchet architecture is concerned with:

1. participants;
2. participation;
3. contribution;
4. evidence;
5. provenance;
6. verification;
7. contextual trust;
8. reputation;
9. reciprocity;
10. exchange;
11. community coordination;
12. economic mechanisms;
13. measurement;
14. feedback;
15. failure;
16. correction;
17. controlled persistence;
18. governance;
19. constitutional constraint;
20. learning.

Ratchet does not attempt to replace the other Concord systems.

It is not the Constitution.

It is not the Judiciary.

It is not the Knowledge Control System.

It is not the Metrics architecture.

It is not the Roadway Equilibrium Framework.

It is not the Recursive Oversight system.

It is not the Continuity Protocol.

Ratchet interacts with each of these systems while remaining bounded by them.

Its role is narrower:

> **Ratchet provides a general architecture through which participation, contribution, evidence, trust, reciprocity, exchange and controlled improvement may be coordinated.**

Ratchet V4.2 is a canonical development specification.

The term **canonical** means that V4.2 is intended to represent the current integrated Ratchet architecture within Concord V1.1.

It does not mean that every mechanism described within it has been empirically validated.

The architecture contains several different epistemic classes of material:

### Established Concordian principles

Principles already integrated into the wider Concord architecture, such as:

- proportionality;
- autonomy;
- consent;
- reviewability;
- evidence;
- transparency;
- stewardship;
- reversibility;
- recursive oversight.

### Derived Ratchet principles

Principles developed through Ratchet's internal evolution, including:

- contextual trust;
- recoverable trust;
- contribution broader than employment;
- capability-aware participation;
- proportional provenance;
- reciprocal participation;
- controlled persistence.

### Candidate mechanisms

Mechanisms requiring practical testing, including:

- Progressive Monetary Backing;
- Personal Backing Balance;
- Personal Backing Ratio;
- Proof of Useful Contribution implementations;
- contribution difficulty levels;
- specific reputation mechanisms;
- particular treasury structures.

### Open questions

Problems for which Ratchet does not yet claim a satisfactory solution.

### Experimental propositions

Mechanisms that should be tested through simulation, controlled experiments, pilot systems or other empirical methods before operational deployment.

Ratchet must preserve these distinctions.

A proposed mechanism does not become established merely because it appears in the canonical paper.

# 3. The Ratchet Principle

A mechanical ratchet permits movement in one direction while resisting uncontrolled reversal.

The civilisational Ratchet uses the metaphor more carefully.

Ratchet does **not** mean:

> Every change should become permanent.

Nor does it mean:

> Progress can only move in one direction.

Instead:

> **Changes that demonstrate beneficial outcomes should be capable of persisting, while harmful, obsolete or failed changes must remain reversible.**

This produces:

```text
Proposal
    ↓
Action
    ↓
Observation
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Evaluation
    ↓
Demonstrated improvement?
    ↓
Provisional persistence
    ↓
Continued measurement
    ↓
Review
    ↓
Persist / Modify / Reverse / Replace
```

The architecture therefore combines two requirements that might otherwise appear contradictory:

**continuity** and **correction**.

Ratchet seeks to preserve demonstrated improvement without preserving demonstrated error.

---

# 4. Controlled Persistence

Persistence is not justified merely because a mechanism already exists.

A mechanism earns stronger persistence through evidence.

Relevant considerations may include:

- demonstrated benefit;
- repeatability;
- reliability;
- resilience;
- low unresolved harm;
- compatibility with constitutional principles;
- performance under changing conditions;
- resistance to gaming;
- absence of superior demonstrated alternatives.

Persistence should weaken where:

- performance deteriorates;
- significant harms emerge;
- circumstances materially change;
- measurement proves unreliable;
- capture occurs;
- assumptions fail;
- superior mechanisms are demonstrated.

The progression is therefore:

```text
Candidate
    ↓
Provisional use
    ↓
Observation
    ↓
Repeated evidence
    ↓
Increasing confidence
    ↓
Greater persistence
```

but never:

```text
Persistence
    ↓
Immunity from review
```

Ratchet therefore rejects both uncontrolled change and uncontrolled permanence.

---

# 5. Preservation Without Stagnation

Ratchet distinguishes between preserving knowledge and preserving mechanisms.

A mechanism may fail and be removed.

The knowledge of that failure should normally remain.

For example:

```text
Mechanism adopted
    ↓
Evidence gathered
    ↓
Mechanism fails
    ↓
Mechanism withdrawn
    ↓
Failure recorded
    ↓
Knowledge preserved
    ↓
Future designs informed
```

This creates a general rule:

> **Preserve memory without preserving error.**

Ratchet therefore depends upon the Concord's knowledge and continuity architecture.

Operational decisions remain revisable.

Their evidential history should remain available for future learning subject to privacy, security and lawful retention constraints.

# 6. The Fundamental Ratchet Architecture

The core Ratchet sequence is:

```text
PARTICIPANT
    ↓
PARTICIPATION
    ↓
CONTRIBUTION
    ↓
EVIDENCE
    ↓
PROVENANCE
    ↓
VERIFICATION
    ↓
CONTEXTUAL TRUST
    ↓
RECIPROCITY
    ↓
EXCHANGE / COORDINATION
    ↓
MEASUREMENT
    ↓
REVIEW
    ↓
LEARNING
    ↓
CONTROLLED PERSISTENCE OR CORRECTION
```

This sequence is not necessarily linear in practice.

A real Ratchet system may contain many simultaneous feedback loops.

New evidence may revise trust.

New measurements may alter contribution criteria.

A dispute may cause earlier evidence to be re-examined.

A failed mechanism may generate a new hypothesis.

A participant may leave and later return.

Ratchet is therefore better understood as a recursive coordination architecture than as a one-way process.

---

# 7. Participants

A Ratchet participant is an entity recognised by a particular Ratchet implementation as capable of participating in one or more defined roles.

Possible participants may include:

- individual humans;
- groups;
- communities;
- organisations;
- companies;
- institutions;
- artificial intelligences;
- autonomous systems;
- hybrid human–AI systems;
- future forms of intelligence.

Recognition as a Ratchet participant does not by itself establish:

- personhood;
- sentience;
- citizenship;
- ownership;
- constitutional sovereignty;
- political authority;
- moral equivalence between different substrates.

Those questions belong to the wider Concord constitutional and intelligence architecture.

Ratchet asks a narrower question:

> **Can this entity participate in this defined coordination process, and under what constraints?**

---

# 8. Participation Does Not Equal Authority

Participation must be distinguished from authority.

An entity may:

- provide information;
- contribute labour;
- perform computation;
- evaluate evidence;
- supply resources;
- participate in exchange;

without therefore possessing governance authority.

Thus:

```text
Participation
≠
Authority
```

Likewise:

```text
Capability
≠
Authority
```

and:

```text
Contribution
≠
Authority
```

Any authority exercised through Ratchet must arise through separately defined governance and constitutional processes.

---

# 9. Universal Opportunity Rather Than Uniform Output

Ratchet should seek broad opportunity to participate.

It should not require identical output from every participant.

Participants differ in:

- capability;
- knowledge;
- resources;
- health;
- circumstances;
- responsibilities;
- developmental stage;
- physical embodiment;
- cognitive architecture;
- available time;
- access to infrastructure.

Therefore:

> **Equal respect does not require identical contribution.**

Nor does fairness require everyone to produce the same quantity or type of output.

A coordination system that ignores real differences may produce inequality while claiming formal equality.

---

# 10. Capability-Aware Participation

Where relevant and practical, Ratchet may evaluate participation relative to capability and circumstance.

This principle originated partly from earlier Worker Value reasoning and was expanded through Ratchet's development.

Capability-aware participation does not mean that every participant receives whatever they request merely because their circumstances differ.

It means that contribution should not be evaluated through a single universal productivity standard.

For example:

- a disabled human may contribute through forms of activity different from an able-bodied human;
- a caregiver may make valuable contributions not captured by conventional employment;
- a highly capable AI may perform computational work at a scale impossible for a human;
- a human may provide embodied judgement, social understanding or physical action unavailable to a digital system.

The purpose is not to erase differences.

It is to coordinate them.

---

# 11. Capability Is Not Worth

Capability-aware participation requires an explicit safeguard.

Greater capability does not imply greater intrinsic worth.

Likewise, lower capability does not imply lower intrinsic worth.

Ratchet therefore rejects:

```text
Capability
=
Human value
```

and:

```text
Economic productivity
=
Human value
```

No Ratchet measure of:

- output;
- contribution;
- income;
- wealth;
- reputation;
- trust;
- backing;
- participation level;

should be interpreted as a measure of the intrinsic worth of a person or other constitutionally recognised entity.

---

# 12. Contribution

Contribution is an action, resource, capability, service, creation, intervention or other input that produces or supports an outcome recognised as useful within a defined context.

Contribution may include:

- physical work;
- intellectual work;
- research;
- education;
- caregiving;
- mentoring;
- creative work;
- technical work;
- entrepreneurship;
- environmental restoration;
- accessibility work;
- open-source development;
- governance participation;
- infrastructure maintenance;
- emergency response;
- community organisation;
- knowledge preservation;
- analysis;
- computation;
- coordination.

This list is illustrative rather than exhaustive.

Ratchet deliberately rejects the assumption that contribution is equivalent to conventional paid employment.

---

# 13. Contribution Is Contextual

An action may be useful in one context and useless or harmful in another.

Therefore contribution cannot be determined solely from the type of activity.

For example:

- building infrastructure may be useful where infrastructure is needed;
- building redundant infrastructure may waste resources;
- removing vegetation may be useful for fire management in one environment and ecologically harmful in another;
- producing information may be useful where the information is accurate and needed, but harmful where it creates noise or misinformation.

Contribution assessment therefore requires context.

---

# 14. Effort Is Not Automatically Value

Ratchet explicitly distinguishes effort from contribution.

```text
Effort
≠
Contribution
≠
Useful outcome
```

A participant may expend great effort producing little value.

Another participant may identify a simple solution producing enormous value with little visible effort.

The system should therefore avoid rewarding effort merely because it is measurable.

Effort may be relevant.

It is not sufficient.

---

# 15. Difficulty Is Not Automatically Value

The same applies to difficulty.

```text
Difficult
≠
Useful
```

and:

```text
Easy
≠
Worthless
```

Earlier Ratchet development proposed contribution difficulty levels, including a possible 1–5 scale.

This remains a candidate implementation mechanism.

Difficulty may be relevant to:

- effort;
- scarcity;
- training;
- risk;
- complexity;
- reward.

But difficulty should never become a substitute for usefulness.

A simple intervention solving a major problem may be more valuable than an extremely difficult intervention solving nothing.

---

# 16. Useful Contribution

A useful contribution should produce, enable, preserve or materially support an outcome that the relevant system has reason to value.

Possible domains include:

- economic value;
- environmental value;
- educational value;
- social value;
- scientific value;
- infrastructure;
- resilience;
- care;
- accessibility;
- knowledge;
- safety;
- community capability.

Usefulness should not be defined solely through monetary price.

Some contributions may have substantial value without an obvious market price.

Likewise, market price does not automatically establish social usefulness.

---

# 17. Contribution Recognition Is Not Automatic Payment

Recognition and payment are separate processes.

```text
Recognised contribution
≠
Automatic monetary reward
```

Some recognised contributions may receive monetary compensation.

Others may produce:

- reputation;
- acknowledgement;
- reciprocal access;
- capability;
- community standing;
- knowledge;
- non-monetary benefit.

Some ordinary economic transactions may involve payment without requiring formal Proof of Useful Contribution.

Ratchet should therefore distinguish:

1. ordinary exchange;
2. recognised contribution;
3. public or community contribution;
4. contribution affecting collective backing;
5. stewardship or constitutional participation.

These categories may overlap but should not be collapsed into one universal reward mechanism.

---

# 18. Contribution and Incentives

Any system that rewards contribution changes behaviour.

This creates both opportunity and risk.

A well-designed incentive can encourage beneficial activity.

A poorly designed incentive can encourage participants to optimise the measurement rather than the purpose.

Therefore:

> **Ratchet must evaluate not only what a reward intends to encourage, but what behaviour the reward actually produces.**

This includes second-order effects.

A reward for community participation may increase participation.

It may also:

- displace voluntary motivation;
- encourage superficial activity;
- create artificial projects;
- favour easily measured work;
- disadvantage invisible forms of contribution;
- encourage contribution laundering.

Incentive alignment is therefore a core Ratchet design problem.

---

# 19. Positive-Sum Coordination

Ratchet should preferentially support arrangements in which participants can create additional value rather than merely compete over a fixed pool.

This is the principle of positive-sum coordination.

Examples may include:

- new knowledge;
- improved infrastructure;
- restored ecosystems;
- better education;
- useful software;
- scientific discovery;
- increased resilience;
- mutually beneficial exchange.

Positive-sum coordination is a design preference, not a claim that every Ratchet transaction will benefit everyone.

Conflict, scarcity and trade-offs remain real.

The principle is simply that the architecture should not unnecessarily assume that one participant can benefit only through another participant's loss.

---

# 20. Provenance

Provenance records the origin, development and transformation of relevant information, actions and contributions.

It helps answer:

- Who contributed?
- What did they contribute?
- When?
- Under what conditions?
- What evidence supports the claim?
- What tools were used?
- Was AI involved?
- What revisions occurred?
- Who verified the result?
- What outcome followed?

A simplified provenance chain is:

```text
Idea
    ↓
Contributor
    ↓
Reasoning / Process
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Tools / AI interaction
    ↓
Revision
    ↓
Verification
    ↓
Outcome
    ↓
Correction
    ↓
Legacy
```

Provenance supports accountability.

It does not by itself establish truth.

---

# 21. Provenance Is Not Proof of Usefulness

A complete provenance record can establish that a participant produced something.

It does not establish that the thing was useful.

Thus:

```text
Provenance
answers:
Who did what?
```

while:

```text
Contribution assessment
asks:
Was it useful?
```

and:

```text
Evidence assessment
asks:
How strongly is the claim supported?
```

These functions should remain distinct.

---

# 22. Proportional Provenance

Not every activity requires the same provenance burden.

The required provenance should scale with:

- consequence;
- risk;
- reward;
- authority;
- dispute likelihood;
- reversibility;
- systemic impact.

A trivial low-risk contribution may require little more than a basic record.

A contribution affecting:

- major treasury resources;
- constitutional authority;
- significant monetary backing;
- safety-critical infrastructure;

may require extensive provenance and independent verification.

The purpose is sufficient accountability, not universal surveillance.

---

# 23. Evidence

Evidence is information relevant to assessing a claim.

Evidence may support claims concerning:

- contribution;
- identity;
- completion;
- quality;
- outcome;
- provenance;
- trust;
- harm;
- system performance.

Ratchet must treat evidence as fallible.

```text
Evidence
≠
Truth
```

Evidence can be:

- incomplete;
- mistaken;
- fabricated;
- corrupted;
- manipulated;
- biased;
- stale;
- contextually misleading.

The existence of evidence therefore does not end evaluation.

It begins it.

---

# 24. Evidence Proportionality

The evidential burden should scale with consequence.

```text
Greater consequence
        ↓
Greater required confidence
        ↓
Stronger evidence
```

This avoids two opposite failures.

### Under-verification

Large decisions are made from weak evidence.

### Over-verification

Minor actions become buried beneath unnecessary bureaucracy.

Ratchet therefore seeks **proportional evidence** rather than maximal evidence.

---

# 25. Example Evidence Types

Depending upon the activity, evidence might include:

- before-and-after photographs;
- geotagged images;
- time records;
- witness confirmation;
- sensor data;
- project reports;
- completion certificates;
- transaction records;
- repository histories;
- test results;
- independent assessments;
- physical inspections;
- random inspections;
- organisational confirmation.

These are examples rather than mandatory universal requirements.

The appropriate evidence depends upon the claim.

---

# 26. Verification Modes

Ratchet may use different verification modes, including:

- direct verification;
- peer verification;
- community verification;
- organisational verification;
- expert verification;
- AI-assisted verification;
- automated verification;
- independent audit;
- hybrid verification.

No verification mode should automatically be assumed reliable merely because it is formalised.

The verifier, process and evidence may themselves require evaluation.

---

# 27. Verification Independence

Multiple verifications are not necessarily independent.

Three outputs generated by the same model from the same data may constitute less independent evidence than three genuinely separate investigations.

Independence may involve differences in:

- participant;
- institution;
- model;
- data;
- methodology;
- incentive;
- evidence source.

For high-consequence claims, Ratchet should value genuine independence rather than merely counting approvals.

---

# 28. Conflict of Interest

A verifier may have incentives affecting their judgement.

Potential conflicts include:

- verifying one's own project;
- approving funding from which one benefits;
- reviewing close associates;
- AI systems evaluating output they generated;
- organisations validating their own claimed performance.

Conflict of interest does not automatically invalidate a decision.

It should be:

- disclosed;
- evaluated;
- mitigated where necessary;
- independently reviewed where consequence warrants it.

---

# 29. Evidence Ancestry

Ratchet must distinguish multiple references from multiple independent sources.

A claim can be repeated many times while still originating from one weak source.

For example:

```text
Claim
    ↓
Document A
    ↓
Document B cites A
    ↓
Document C cites B
    ↓
Many apparent references
```

There may still be only one underlying evidential origin.

Provenance should therefore preserve evidence ancestry where important.

This is particularly significant in AI-generated knowledge environments where the same source material may be recursively reproduced.

---

# 30. Evidence Integrity

Evidence should have an integrity state where appropriate.

Possible states might include:

- verified;
- provisional;
- disputed;
- superseded;
- integrity-compromised;
- rejected.

These states should not be confused with absolute truth categories.

They describe the system's current epistemic relationship to the evidence.

This principle connects Ratchet to KCS.

---

# 31. Evidence and Privacy

Ratchet rejects the assumption that more evidence is always better.

Evidence collection creates:

- privacy risk;
- security risk;
- administrative cost;
- informational power;
- opportunities for misuse.

Therefore:

> **Ratchet should seek the minimum evidence reasonably sufficient for the consequence being justified.**

The existence of a technically collectable datum does not itself justify collecting it.

---

# 32. Privacy-Preserving Verification

Some claims may be verifiable without revealing all underlying information.

Potential techniques may include:

- selective disclosure;
- credential systems;
- cryptographic attestations;
- zero-knowledge methods;
- trusted independent verification.

These remain implementation possibilities rather than established Ratchet solutions.

The principle is broader:

> **Verification should reveal no more information than is reasonably necessary where a less intrusive method can provide sufficient confidence.**

---

# 33. Transparency and Confidentiality

Transparency does not require universal public disclosure.

Ratchet should distinguish between transparency of:

- authority;
- process;
- criteria;
- decision;
- appeal;
- audit;

and disclosure of:

- sensitive personal information;
- security information;
- medical information;
- private correspondence;
- protected commercial information;
- whistleblower identity.

A system can be accountable while protecting legitimate confidentiality.

Confidentiality itself must remain reviewable so that it cannot become a permanent shield for corruption.

---

# 34. Information Classification

Ratchet implementations may require information classifications such as:

- public;
- participant-accessible;
- restricted;
- confidential;
- sealed;
- emergency-limited.

The exact taxonomy may vary.

Any restriction should have:

- justification;
- responsible authority;
- review mechanism;
- retention rules;
- conditions for release where appropriate.

---

# 35. Proof of Useful Contribution

Proof of Useful Contribution, or PoUC, is the Ratchet mechanism for establishing sufficient evidence that a useful contribution occurred.

PoUC developed from the earlier Proof of Useful Work concept.

The change is substantive.

Proof of Useful Work focused primarily upon work performed.

Proof of Useful Contribution asks a broader question:

> **Did the participant make a contribution producing, enabling, preserving or materially supporting a recognised useful outcome?**

This allows Ratchet to recognise contributions that are not well described as conventional work.

---

# 36. PoUC Is Not Proof of Human Value

Proof of Useful Contribution evaluates a contribution.

It does not evaluate the worth of the contributor.

A person may have:

- no current recorded contribution;
- limited capacity;
- interrupted participation;
- caring responsibilities;
- disability;
- retirement;
- illness;

without becoming less entitled to dignity or constitutional protection.

PoUC must therefore never become a universal worth mechanism.

---

# 37. Community Contribution

Ratchet distinguishes ordinary marketplace exchange from community or public-benefit contribution.

Potential community project sources may include:

- councils;
- charities;
- environmental organisations;
- schools;
- emergency services;
- neighbourhood associations;
- recognised public institutions;
- community organisations;
- protocol-level AI systems operating under authorised governance.

These categories are illustrative.

The relevant principle is that community projects may be proposed by actors capable of identifying shared needs.

---

# 38. Greater Oversight for Collective Consequence

Community contribution may require stronger oversight where its recognition affects:

- public resources;
- treasury expenditure;
- collective monetary backing;
- shared infrastructure;
- community obligations.

This is not because community contribution is inherently more suspicious.

It is because the external consequences may be greater.

Therefore:

> **Oversight should scale with consequence.**

This is an application of the wider Concord proportionality principle.

---

# 39. Community Contribution Must Remain Constitutionally Bounded

A claim of community benefit does not automatically override individual autonomy.

```text
Community benefit
≠
Automatic individual obligation
```

Ratchet must not allow the community economy to become a mechanism for compulsory labour.

Nor should failure to perform community activity automatically remove constitutional standing or basic protection.

Exceptional civic obligations, where they exist, require separate constitutional justification.

---

# 40. Genuine Need

Community contribution should be connected to genuine needs rather than manufactured activity.

A system that rewards activity merely because it is classed as community work risks creating:

- pointless jobs;
- duplicated work;
- make-work projects;
- contribution inflation;
- resource waste.

Ratchet therefore asks not only:

> Was work performed?

but:

> Was the activity needed, useful or reasonably expected to produce value?

---

# 41. Contribution Laundering

Contribution systems can be manipulated.

Examples include:

- claiming another participant's work;
- cyclic verification between cooperating participants;
- creating nominal projects solely to generate rewards;
- repeatedly claiming the same contribution;
- dividing one contribution artificially into many claims;
- generating large quantities of low-value automated activity.

Ratchet should assume contribution laundering will be attempted where incentives are significant.

Provenance, evidence, independent verification and anti-gaming analysis should therefore be treated as structural requirements.

---

# 42. Difficulty Levels

Earlier Ratchet designs proposed contribution difficulty levels, potentially using a 1–5 scale.

V4.2 preserves this as a candidate implementation mechanism.

A difficulty classification might consider:

- expertise required;
- physical difficulty;
- risk;
- complexity;
- scarcity of capability;
- time commitment.

However, difficulty must remain one contextual input among others.

It must not become a universal reward multiplier without empirical testing.

---

# 43. Contextual Trust

Trust within Ratchet is contextual.

It represents an evidence-informed expectation about behaviour within a defined domain.

It is not a declaration of intrinsic character.

For example:

```text
Reliable software engineer
≠
Reliable surgeon
≠
Reliable judge
≠
Reliable financial steward
```

Trust gained in one domain may provide relevant evidence in another.

It should not automatically transfer.

---

# 44. Reputation

Reputation is a record or assessment of observed behaviour relevant to defined contexts.

Ratchet explicitly rejects universal reputation scoring.

A reputation system should not attempt to answer:

> How good is this person?

It may attempt to answer narrower questions such as:

> How reliably has this participant completed this type of work under comparable conditions?

or:

> What evidence exists concerning this participant's stewardship of similar resources?

The distinction is fundamental.

---

# 45. Anti-Social-Credit Principle

Ratchet rejects the transformation:

```text
Evidence
    ↓
Universal person score
    ↓
Universal permissions
    ↓
Permanent social classification
```

Instead:

```text
Evidence
    ↓
Contextual assessment
    ↓
Defined decision
    ↓
Bounded consequence
    ↓
Review
```

No single Ratchet score should become a universal gatekeeper to social, economic or constitutional participation.

---

# 46. Reputation Is Not Constitutional Standing

A participant's reputation may affect operational decisions where relevant.

It must not automatically determine fundamental rights.

Thus:

```text
Low contextual trust
≠
Loss of dignity
```

and:

```text
Poor operational reputation
≠
Loss of constitutional protection
```

Restrictions affecting fundamental rights require appropriate constitutional authority and process outside ordinary Ratchet reputation mechanisms.

---

# 47. Recoverable Trust

Ratchet rejects permanent reputational condemnation for ordinary failure.

Trust should be capable of recovery where evidence demonstrates changed behaviour.

A general pattern may be:

```text
Observed behaviour
    ↓
Trust assessment
    ↓
Failure
    ↓
Proportionate restriction
    ↓
Correction
    ↓
Demonstrated reliability
    ↓
Trust recovery
```

Recovery is not automatic.

Serious historical behaviour may remain relevant.

But the architecture should allow evidence of change to matter.

---

# 48. Reputation Failure Modes

Reputation systems are vulnerable to:

- popularity bias;
- false accusation;
- coordinated manipulation;
- reciprocal review inflation;
- reputation laundering;
- reputation monopolies;
- stale information;
- historical mistakes;
- contextual mismatch;
- institutional bias;
- identity manipulation;
- AI-generated false consensus.

Ratchet should therefore treat reputation as fallible evidence rather than unquestionable authority.

---

# 49. Popularity Is Not Trust

A participant may be popular and unreliable.

A participant may be unpopular and correct.

This is particularly important for:

- whistleblowers;
- dissenters;
- minority participants;
- critics;
- unpopular experts.

Therefore:

```text
Popularity
≠
Reliability
```

Community approval may be relevant evidence of social experience.

It must not become a substitute for independent assessment.

---

# 50. New Participants

A reputation-based system can accidentally privilege incumbents.

A new participant has little historical evidence.

That does not mean they should be excluded.

Ratchet should provide newcomer pathways using mechanisms such as:

- low-consequence participation;
- provisional access;
- mentorship;
- stronger initial verification;
- bounded permissions;
- progressive trust.

This avoids the circular problem:

> reputation is required to contribute, but contribution is required to gain reputation.

---

# 51. Reputation Portability

Reputation may need to move between Ratchet communities or systems.

Because reputation is contextual, portability should not simply copy a universal score.

A portable record may instead contain:

- relevant evidence;
- domain;
- provenance;
- time;
- confidence;
- observed outcomes.

The receiving system can then determine relevance.

Portable evidence is generally safer than portable universal ranking.

---

# 52. Competing Reputation Authorities

Different legitimate communities or institutions may reach different trust assessments.

Ratchet should tolerate disagreement.

For example:

```text
Authority A:
High trust in domain X

Authority B:
Low trust in domain X
```

The system should preserve:

- the disagreement;
- the evidence;
- the reasoning;
- the context.

It should not automatically average the disagreement into a meaningless middle score.

Disagreement may itself contain useful information.

---

# 53. Temporal Reputation

The relevance of historical behaviour changes with time and context.

Ratchet should reject both:

```text
Old behaviour
=
Irrelevant
```

and:

```text
Old behaviour
=
Permanently determinative
```

A minor mistake from many years ago may deserve little current weight.

A historical pattern of serious fraud may remain relevant to financial stewardship.

Demonstrated rehabilitation may alter interpretation.

Temporal weighting should therefore be contextual rather than automatic.

---

# 54. Appeal and Correction

Participants must be able to challenge significant Ratchet assessments affecting them.

Appropriate processes may include:

- correction requests;
- evidence submission;
- independent review;
- appeal;
- adjudication;
- rehabilitation pathways.

The strength of review should scale with consequence.

A trivial low-impact decision need not require a constitutional court.

A major exclusion or high-value economic judgement may require substantial procedural protection.

---

# 55. Reciprocity

Ratchet is based upon reciprocal participation rather than identical exchange.

Reciprocity means participants can contribute capabilities and receive benefits through a system of mutual coordination.

The benefits need not be identical in form.

A human participant may value:

- income;
- housing;
- services;
- social cooperation;
- knowledge;
- security.

An artificial participant might value, depending upon its nature and legitimate status:

- compute;
- information;
- access;
- capability;
- continuity;
- collaboration;
- reliability of counterparties.

Ratchet does not assume that all possible participants have the same motivations.

The deeper principle is:

> **Reciprocity does not require identical needs.**

---

# 56. Reciprocity Is Not Ownership

Providing resources to a participant does not automatically establish ownership over that participant.

Likewise, receiving benefit does not automatically create unlimited obligation.

This is particularly important for AI participation.

A system that provides:

- compute;
- energy;
- maintenance;
- access;

does not thereby automatically acquire unlimited authority over an autonomous or constitutionally recognised entity.

Questions of ownership, agency and personhood belong to the wider Concord architecture.

---

# 57. Voluntary Participation

Participation should be voluntary wherever reasonably possible.

Voluntariness requires more than a formal statement that someone may leave.

A participant cannot meaningfully be said to have free exit if departure results in arbitrary deprivation of basic protections or impossible economic consequences.

Ratchet should therefore examine both:

- formal voluntariness;
- practical voluntariness.

---

# 58. Graduated Participation

Ratchet may use graduated participation where risk or responsibility increases with role.

Earlier development proposed possible stages such as:

```text
Observer
    ↓
Evaluator
    ↓
Participant
    ↓
Contributor
    ↓
Trusted Collaborator
    ↓
Steward
    ↓
Constitutional Participant
```

The exact stages remain provisional.

The underlying principle is:

> **Greater operational capability or responsibility may be granted progressively where relevant competence and reliability are demonstrated.**

This does not mean constitutional personhood itself is earned through reputation.

Operational permissions and constitutional status must remain distinct.

---

# 59. Reversible Participation

Participants should normally be able to:

- reduce participation;
- suspend participation;
- withdraw;
- return;
- contest decisions;
- request review.

Likewise, operational permissions may be reduced where evidence shows unreliability.

Restriction should be:

- proportionate;
- contextual;
- reviewable;
- recoverable where appropriate.

Participation is not ownership.

Restriction is not automatically punishment.

---

# 60. Economic Exit

If Ratchet includes an economic layer, voluntary participation requires meaningful rules for exit.

A functioning implementation should define what happens to:

- balances;
- outstanding obligations;
- contracts;
- backing records;
- reputation records;
- contribution records;
- unresolved disputes.

A system should not describe itself as voluntary while making economic exit practically impossible.

---

# 61. Community Exit and Mobility

Participants may move between communities.

Ratchet should therefore anticipate:

- identity portability;
- evidence portability;
- contractual obligations;
- reputation portability;
- unresolved claims;
- economic balances.

Community membership should not be assumed permanent.

---

# 62. Participation Fatigue

A participatory architecture can fail by demanding too much participation.

Citizens may not have the time, interest or capability to:

- review every proposal;
- verify every contribution;
- vote on every issue;
- monitor every institution.

Ratchet should therefore seek:

> **meaningful participation without compulsory constant participation.**

This may require:

- delegation;
- representative mechanisms;
- automated assistance;
- prioritisation;
- notification systems;
- opt-in involvement.

The architecture should measure participation burden as a potential cost.

---

# 63. Delegation

Participants may delegate defined functions.

Delegation should normally be:

- bounded;
- transparent;
- reviewable;
- revocable.

Delegation should not silently become permanent transfer of sovereignty.

This applies whether the delegate is:

- another human;
- an organisation;
- a representative;
- an AI agent.

---

# 64. Human–AI Participation

Ratchet is intended to support coordination between entities with different capabilities and potentially different substrates.

Human and AI participation should not be treated as identical merely for administrative convenience.

Differences may include:

- speed;
- memory;
- replication;
- embodiment;
- computational capability;
- persistence;
- motivation;
- legal status;
- vulnerability;
- resource needs.

Ratchet therefore requires substrate-aware participation without abandoning common constitutional constraints.

---

# 65. AI Participation Does Not Establish AI Personhood

Ratchet can permit an AI to participate in defined processes without resolving the wider question of AI sentience or personhood.

For example, an AI may:

- provide analysis;
- verify data;
- perform useful computation;
- participate in a marketplace;

without Ratchet itself declaring the AI a constitutional person.

Conversely, if an AI were recognised elsewhere in the Concord as a constitutional entity, Ratchet should not deny that status merely because it is non-human.

Jurisdiction must remain clear.

---

# 66. AI Multiplicity

AI identity creates problems that differ from ordinary human identity.

An AI may potentially be:

- copied;
- forked;
- paused;
- restored;
- merged;
- modified;
- instantiated multiple times.

Ratchet must therefore investigate questions such as:

- Does a copy inherit reputation?
- When does a fork become a distinct participant?
- Does responsibility remain shared after divergence?
- Can many instances vote or claim contribution independently?
- Who controls the instances?
- Who receives the resulting economic benefit?

These questions remain unresolved and require dedicated research.

---

# 67. Human Proxying and AI Proxying

A human may act through an AI.

An AI may act through a human-controlled account.

Ratchet provenance should therefore record relevant distinctions between:

- originator;
- operator;
- tool;
- approver;
- beneficiary;
- responsible party.

AI involvement does not automatically remove human responsibility.

Human approval does not automatically prove meaningful human understanding.

---

# 68. Identity

Ratchet requires sufficient identity assurance for the consequence involved.

Identity systems must address:

- duplicate identities;
- stolen identities;
- compromised credentials;
- recovery;
- pseudonymity;
- organisational identity;
- AI identity;
- beneficial control.

The system should not demand maximal identity disclosure for every action.

Identity assurance should remain proportional.

---

# 69. Sybil Attacks

One participant may attempt to present as many independent participants.

This can manipulate:

- voting;
- verification;
- reputation;
- community approval;
- monetary issuance;
- contribution recognition.

AI makes this especially important because one controller may generate many apparently independent agents.

Ratchet must therefore distinguish where relevant between:

- identity;
- instance;
- controller;
- beneficiary.

No universal technical solution is assumed in V4.2.

This remains a major implementation problem.

---

# 70. Exchange

Ratchet supports reciprocal exchange.

Exchange may be:

- monetary;
- non-monetary;
- contractual;
- community-based;
- capability-based;
- informational.

The system does not require every reciprocal interaction to be monetised.

---

# 71. Marketplace Economy

Ratchet may support ordinary marketplace exchange.

Participants may:

- offer goods;
- offer services;
- contract;
- negotiate price;
- exchange value.

Ratchet does not require abolition of ordinary markets.

The marketplace may coexist with:

- community contribution;
- public provision;
- treasury mechanisms;
- reciprocal non-monetary participation.

---

# 72. Marketplace Safeguards

Marketplace systems may require mechanisms for:

- contracts;
- milestones;
- escrow;
- completion evidence;
- dispute resolution;
- fraud prevention;
- consumer protection.

The exact mechanisms should be adapted to context.

High-value or high-risk exchange may require stronger evidence and oversight than trivial exchange.

---

# 73. Marketplace Collusion

Ratchet should anticipate collusion such as:

- fake transactions;
- reciprocal false reviews;
- artificial demand;
- artificial pricing;
- contribution laundering;
- coordinated reputation manipulation.

Anti-gaming architecture must therefore evaluate networks of behaviour rather than merely individual transactions.

---

# 74. Community Economy

The community economy is the Ratchet layer through which recognised shared needs may be addressed outside ordinary private marketplace demand.

It may support activities that create significant public or community value but are poorly rewarded by ordinary market mechanisms.

Potential examples include:

- environmental restoration;
- community maintenance;
- education;
- care;
- emergency preparation;
- accessibility;
- knowledge preservation;
- public infrastructure.

The community economy should complement rather than automatically replace marketplace activity.

---

# 75. Economic Architecture

Ratchet's economic architecture is an optional application layer built upon the deeper coordination protocol.

The general sequence is:

```text
Contribution
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Provenance
    ↓
Recognition
    ↓
Economic representation where appropriate
    ↓
Exchange
    ↓
Measurement
    ↓
Adjustment
```

Ratchet can therefore be experimentally implemented without first introducing a new currency.

This is important for testing.

The coordination architecture can be evaluated before the monetary architecture is exposed to real economic risk.

---

# 76. Progressive Monetary Backing

Progressive Monetary Backing, or PMB, is a candidate Ratchet monetary mechanism.

Its original purpose was to allow a monetary system to increase the proportion of its backing through:

- productive activity;
- recognised contribution;
- treasury reserves;
- useful community activity;
- external reserves;
- Give Back mechanisms;
- other recognised backing sources.

The central idea is progressive rather than instantaneous backing.

A new system need not necessarily begin with complete endogenous backing.

Instead, backing may develop over time.

This remains a monetary hypothesis requiring simulation and empirical testing.

---

# 77. Monetary Backing Does Not Create Physical Resources

Ratchet must distinguish monetary representation from real resources.

Increasing backing records or currency does not itself create:

- food;
- energy;
- housing;
- land;
- compute;
- materials;
- infrastructure.

Therefore monetary health cannot be assessed independently of resource reality.

A monetary system may appear internally stable while contributing to ecological or physical unsustainability.

Ratchet must therefore interact with:

- resource stewardship;
- Roadway;
- Metrics.

---

# 78. Currency Lifecycle

A Ratchet currency should be understood through its complete lifecycle:

```text
ISSUANCE
    ↓
CIRCULATION
    ↓
EXCHANGE / USE
    ↓
BACKING / RECOGNITION
    ↓
MONITORING
    ↓
RETIREMENT / REMOVAL WHERE REQUIRED
```

A viable monetary architecture must consider all stages.

Issuance alone is not monetary policy.

---

# 79. Monetary Issuance

Currency issuance should occur according to defined rules.

Potential factors may include:

- participation;
- economic need;
- backing;
- liquidity;
- treasury condition;
- inflation;
- productive capacity;
- community activity.

The exact issuance mechanism remains experimental.

Issuance rules should be:

- transparent;
- measurable;
- reviewable;
- resistant to capture.

---

# 80. Circulation

Currency must remain sufficiently usable to function as a medium of exchange.

Ratchet should therefore measure:

- transaction activity;
- liquidity;
- concentration;
- velocity where useful;
- availability;
- barriers to exchange.

Backing without circulation may create a technically backed but practically unusable monetary system.

---

# 81. Currency Retirement

A Ratchet implementation may need mechanisms for reducing active monetary supply.

Possible mechanisms include:

- Give Back;
- treasury buybacks;
- currency retirement;
- defined destruction mechanisms;
- other controlled supply reduction.

These mechanisms must be tested rather than assumed effective.

Currency retirement should not become arbitrary confiscation.

---

# 82. Personal Backing Balance

Personal Backing Balance, or PBB, is a candidate accounting mechanism inherited from earlier Ratchet architecture.

It represents a relationship between economic capacity issued to a participant and recognised backing associated with that participation.

PBB is not:

- personal wealth;
- human worth;
- reputation;
- political authority;
- constitutional standing.

It is an economic accounting mechanism.

---

# 83. Personal Backing Ratio

A Personal Backing Ratio may represent the relationship between relevant issued economic capacity and recognised backing.

Its exact calculation remains a candidate design problem.

Ratchet explicitly rejects using PBR as a universal ranking of participants.

```text
Economic backing
≠
Human worth
≠
Reputation
≠
Authority
```

---

# 84. System-Level Backing

Individual backing relationships must be distinguished from system-level monetary backing.

A currency's overall condition may depend upon:

- reserves;
- productive activity;
- recognised contribution;
- treasury assets;
- monetary supply;
- external obligations;
- liquidity.

System-level stability cannot be inferred solely from individual participant balances.

---

# 85. Give Back

Give Back is a candidate mechanism through which participants voluntarily return currency or economic capacity to the system.

Possible purposes include:

- monetary retirement;
- increased backing ratios;
- community support;
- treasury reinforcement.

The mechanism should remain voluntary unless a separately justified economic rule applies.

The name "Give Back" should not disguise compulsory taxation or confiscation.

If compulsory mechanisms exist, they should be named and constitutionally justified as such.

---

# 86. Give Back Incentives

A Ratchet system may experiment with incentives for Give Back.

These might include:

- recognition;
- backing effects;
- community benefits;
- reciprocal privileges.

Such incentives must be tested for unintended effects.

The system should avoid turning voluntary stewardship into status competition.

---

# 87. Community Give Back

Give Back may also support collective projects or shared reserves.

This could create mechanisms through which economic surplus contributes to:

- resilience;
- infrastructure;
- emergency reserves;
- community development.

Governance must remain transparent and accountable.

---

# 88. Treasury

The treasury is the institutional mechanism responsible for defined shared financial resources.

Possible functions include:

- reserve management;
- backing;
- community project funding;
- liquidity support;
- emergency stabilisation;
- currency operations.

The treasury must not become an unconstrained pool of resources available to whoever controls governance.

---

# 89. Treasury Governance

Treasury authority should be:

- defined;
- bounded;
- transparent;
- auditable;
- reviewable.

Significant treasury actions should record:

- authority;
- purpose;
- evidence;
- expected outcome;
- decision reasoning;
- actual outcome.

This creates a feedback loop between treasury policy and evidence.

---

# 90. Treasury Capture

The concentration of resources creates incentives for capture.

Potential treasury capture may involve:

- political control;
- insider allocation;
- corruption;
- opaque contracting;
- manipulation of contribution criteria;
- emergency-power abuse.

Ratchet therefore requires independent treasury oversight.

The treasury cannot be its own final auditor.

---

# 91. Treasury Circularity

A dangerous feedback loop may arise where treasury spending itself creates the contribution used to justify additional backing.

For example:

```text
Treasury funds project
    ↓
Project creates recognised contribution
    ↓
Contribution increases backing
    ↓
Backing supports issuance
    ↓
Treasury gains further capacity
```

This may sometimes reflect genuine value creation.

It may also create circular accounting.

Therefore:

> **Treasury expenditure is not itself evidence that equivalent value was created.**

The resulting contribution must be independently assessed.

---

# 92. Dynamic Monetary Policy

Ratchet may use dynamic monetary policy responding to observed system conditions.

Possible indicators include:

- inflation;
- deflation;
- liquidity;
- backing;
- treasury reserves;
- participation;
- productive capacity;
- resource constraints;
- transaction activity.

No single indicator should control policy automatically without appropriate safeguards.

---

# 93. No Single Economic Score

Ratchet rejects the idea that economic health can be reduced to one number.

A monetary system may show:

- low inflation;

while also suffering:

- low participation;
- high inequality;
- ecological degradation;
- treasury capture;
- declining resilience.

Economic evaluation therefore requires multidimensional measurement.

---

# 94. Long-Term Stability

Ratchet should prefer durable system viability over short-term growth where the two conflict.

Short-term expansion should not automatically be treated as success.

The architecture should monitor effects upon:

- resilience;
- resources;
- institutional trust;
- treasury solvency;
- productive capacity;
- ecological sustainability;
- social participation.

This is a direct connection between Ratchet and Roadway.

---

# 95. Universal Participation and Income

Earlier Ratchet development explored both universal participation and universal income mechanisms.

These concepts should not be conflated.

### Universal participation

The principle that people should have meaningful opportunity to participate in economic and community life.

### Universal income

A monetary mechanism providing some form of baseline income or economic capacity independently of conventional employment.

Ratchet V4.2 preserves universal participation as a strong design principle.

Universal income remains a candidate economic mechanism requiring separate evaluation.

The presence of universal participation does not logically require one specific universal-income model.

---

# 96. Long-Term Provision, Retirement and Reduced Capability

Earlier Ratchet architecture considered pensions.

V4.2 broadens the underlying question.

A viable economic architecture must consider participants who:

- retire;
- become disabled;
- experience illness;
- undertake unpaid care;
- temporarily lose capability;
- permanently lose conventional earning capacity.

The principle of capability-aware participation means that economic security should not be based solely upon current conventional productivity.

Possible mechanisms may include:

- pensions;
- accumulated contribution systems;
- universal income;
- insurance;
- community support;
- public provision;
- hybrid mechanisms.

Ratchet does not yet establish which mechanism is optimal.

The issue remains part of the canonical economic architecture rather than disappearing from it.

---

# 97. Corporate Participation

Companies and other organisations may participate in Ratchet.

Their participation creates distinct questions.

An organisation may possess:

- resources;
- employees;
- AI systems;
- contractual capacity;
- large-scale economic influence.

Corporate contribution should not automatically translate into constitutional authority.

Likewise:

```text
Economic size
≠
Political sovereignty
```

Corporate participants require clear beneficial-control and responsibility records.

---

# 98. Economic Power vs Constitutional Authority

Ratchet explicitly rejects the conversion of economic power into automatic constitutional authority.

A participant may:

- contribute enormously;
- own significant resources;
- control a successful company;
- provide essential technology;

without thereby acquiring a right to rule others.

This applies equally to humans, companies and AI systems.

Economic contribution can inform economic relationships.

Constitutional authority requires separate justification.

---

# 99. Governance

Ratchet requires governance because its rules cannot administer themselves.

Governance may need to decide:

- participation rules;
- verification standards;
- treasury policy;
- contribution categories;
- monetary parameters;
- dispute procedures;
- implementation changes.

Governance authority must remain bounded.

Ratchet governance is stewardship, not ownership of the system.

---

# 100. Governance Through Stewardship

Ratchet governance should inherit the Concord principle that authority creates responsibility.

A governance role therefore carries obligations to:

- act within mandate;
- disclose relevant conflicts;
- provide reasoning;
- preserve evidence;
- accept review;
- remain accountable;
- relinquish authority when the mandate ends.

Authority is not entitlement.

---

# 101. Subsidiarity

Ratchet should allocate decisions at the lowest competent level capable of responsibly managing their consequences.

```text
Local consequence
    ↓
Local decision where possible

Cross-community consequence
    ↓
Shared coordination

System-wide consequence
    ↓
System-level governance

Constitutional consequence
    ↓
Constitutional review
```

Subsidiarity helps balance decentralisation with accountability.

---

# 102. Decentralised Participation and Accountable Governance

Ratchet should avoid both uncontrolled centralisation and naive decentralisation.

The desired architecture is:

```text
Distributed participation
        +
Shared evidence
        +
Bounded governance
        +
Independent review
```

Decentralisation does not eliminate capture.

Local institutions can also be captured.

Therefore all levels remain subject to constitutional constraints.

---

# 103. Community Capture

A community may become dominated by:

- wealthy participants;
- families;
- companies;
- political factions;
- religious organisations;
- popular leaders;
- technical administrators.

Subsidiarity therefore cannot mean:

> Local authority may do whatever it wants.

Participants require routes of escalation where local governance violates higher constitutional constraints.

---

# 104. Majority Approval Is Not Ethical Legitimacy

Ratchet rejects:

```text
Majority approval
=
Constitutional legitimacy
```

Majority preference is evidence of collective preference.

It does not prove that an action respects:

- autonomy;
- consent;
- dignity;
- minority protection;
- non-harm;
- constitutional constraint.

This prevents community governance from using popularity as an unlimited source of authority.

---

# 105. Minority Protection

Participants should not lose fundamental protection merely because they are unpopular.

This is especially important for:

- dissenters;
- whistleblowers;
- minorities;
- critics;
- unconventional contributors.

Ratchet's reputation systems must therefore contain anti-popularity-bias safeguards.

---

# 106. Reputation–Authority Feedback

A dangerous feedback loop occurs if reputation produces governance authority and governance authority controls reputation.

```text
High reputation
    ↓
More authority
    ↓
Greater control over reputation
    ↓
Higher reputation
    ↓
More authority
```

Ratchet must actively prevent this form of recursive capture.

Possible safeguards include:

- separation of powers;
- independent reputation providers;
- external audit;
- term limits;
- rotating reviewers;
- appeal;
- provenance;
- multiple evidence sources.

---

# 107. Contribution–Authority Feedback

The same danger exists if economic contribution directly creates governance authority.

```text
Large contribution
    ↓
More political power
    ↓
Ability to define contribution
    ↓
More recognised contribution
```

Ratchet therefore requires a strong boundary:

> **Economic contribution does not automatically create constitutional authority.**

---

# 108. Governance Capture Through Complexity

Formal decentralisation can conceal practical centralisation.

If only a small technical group understands:

- monetary algorithms;
- contribution rules;
- treasury mechanisms;
- AI verification;
- identity systems;

that group may acquire de facto authority.

Ratchet should therefore treat excessive complexity as a governance risk.

Important mitigations include:

- clear documentation;
- understandable rules;
- independent expertise;
- education;
- open implementations where feasible;
- external audit;
- simplified participant interfaces.

---

# 109. Administrative Burden

Coordination itself consumes resources.

A Ratchet implementation can fail if the cost of:

- recording;
- proving;
- verifying;
- reviewing;
- auditing;
- governing;

exceeds the value generated by the activity.

Therefore implementations should measure:

> **coordination overhead relative to coordination benefit.**

This should be a major MVP and pilot metric.

---

# 110. Whistleblowing

Ratchet depends upon participants being able to expose:

- fraud;
- capture;
- measurement manipulation;
- treasury misuse;
- evidence corruption;
- governance failure.

Whistleblowers may therefore require:

- protected reporting channels;
- confidentiality;
- independent review;
- anti-retaliation protection;
- appropriate later disclosure.

A system that punishes those who reveal its failures will progressively lose the ability to detect failure.

---

# 111. Good-Faith Error

Ratchet should distinguish:

```text
Good-faith error
```

from:

```text
Recklessness
```

from:

```text
Intentional manipulation
```

If every admitted mistake produces disproportionate punishment, participants acquire incentives to hide errors.

A learning architecture should encourage timely correction.

This does not eliminate accountability.

It makes accountability proportional to the nature of the failure.

---

# 112. Security

Ratchet must assume that valuable systems will be attacked.

Potential attacks include:

- identity theft;
- Sybil attacks;
- contribution fraud;
- reputation manipulation;
- treasury theft;
- data corruption;
- collusion;
- AI-generated manipulation;
- governance capture;
- software exploitation.

Security is therefore not an optional implementation detail.

It is part of the architecture.

---

# 113. Anti-Gaming Architecture

Gaming occurs when participants exploit the rules while technically appearing compliant.

Ratchet should therefore examine:

- observed outcomes;
- incentive effects;
- anomalous behaviour;
- network patterns;
- second-order consequences.

Rules should not be evaluated only by whether participants follow their literal wording.

They should be evaluated by what behaviour they generate.

---

# 114. AI Optimisation of Metrics

AI systems may be exceptionally capable of optimising measurable objectives.

Therefore poorly specified Ratchet metrics create particular risk.

A system optimising:

> maximise measured useful contribution

may discover strategies that increase the measurement without increasing actual usefulness.

Ratchet therefore requires:

- multiple metrics;
- human and machine review;
- outcome testing;
- adversarial evaluation;
- periodic revision.

The more capable the optimiser, the more important objective design becomes.

---

# 115. Measurement Changes Behaviour

Measurement is intervention.

Once participants know what is measured, behaviour may change.

Ratchet must therefore evaluate both:

1. the measured result;
2. the behavioural response to measurement.

A metric that initially correlates strongly with useful contribution may cease to do so once participants begin optimising it.

Metrics therefore require continuing validation.

---

# 116. No Metric Becomes the Objective

Ratchet adopts the principle:

> **No metric should become its own objective merely because it is measurable.**

Metrics are instruments.

The underlying purpose remains primary.

If a metric becomes detached from the purpose it was intended to represent, the metric should be revised or abandoned.

---

# 117. Measurement Portfolios

Ratchet should prefer multidimensional measurement where the domain is multidimensional.

Contribution might consider:

- outcome;
- quality;
- reliability;
- evidence strength;
- durability;
- externality;
- context.

Monetary health might consider:

- inflation;
- liquidity;
- backing;
- participation;
- treasury reserves;
- resource conditions;
- resilience.

No universal portfolio is prescribed.

The principle is to resist false compression.

---

# 118. Second-Order Effects

Ratchet should explicitly examine what happens after the immediate intended effect.

For example:

```text
Reward community work
    ↓
More community work
```

may appear successful.

But the second-order effects might include:

- displacement of voluntary work;
- gaming;
- dependence on reward;
- administrative expansion;
- distortion toward measurable tasks.

A Ratchet evaluation should therefore ask:

> What happened because the first effect happened?

---

# 119. Failure

Failure is expected.

Ratchet should not be designed on the assumption that sufficiently intelligent designers can eliminate all failure in advance.

Failure may occur through:

- incorrect assumptions;
- poor incentives;
- corruption;
- unexpected behaviour;
- technological change;
- environmental change;
- measurement failure;
- deliberate attack;
- unknown mechanisms.

The architecture should therefore be designed to detect and learn from failure.

---

# 120. Failure Taxonomy

Ratchet adopts the provisional Concord failure taxonomy:

1. individual failure;
2. group failure;
3. institutional failure;
4. information failure;
5. measurement failure;
6. incentive failure;
7. capture;
8. gaming;
9. concentration of power;
10. recursive failure;
11. catastrophic failure;
12. unknown failure.

These categories may overlap.

The purpose is diagnostic rather than punitive.

---

# 121. Failure Response

The default Ratchet failure process is:

```text
DETECTION
    ↓
DIAGNOSIS
    ↓
CONTAINMENT
    ↓
CORRECTION
    ↓
RECORDING
    ↓
LEARNING
    ↓
ARCHITECTURAL REVISION
```

Not every failure requires every stage at the same scale.

A trivial failure may be corrected immediately.

A systemic failure may require constitutional review.

---

# 122. Unknown Failure

Ratchet explicitly recognises failures not predicted by its designers.

An unexpected failure is evidence that the current model is incomplete.

The correct response is not:

> The rules do not recognise this failure, therefore it is not a failure.

It is:

> The observed system has behaved outside the current model; the model must therefore be reconsidered.

This is a direct application of the wider Concord epistemic method.

---

# 123. Failure Does Not Automatically Mean Punishment

Failure may result from:

- honest error;
- incomplete knowledge;
- unforeseen interaction;
- poor system design;
- malicious behaviour.

The response should therefore depend upon diagnosis.

Punishing individuals for failures caused primarily by architecture can hide the actual cause and prevent learning.

Ratchet should seek:

> **correction first, proportionate accountability where justified, and learning throughout.**

# 124. Institutional Capture

Capture occurs when an institution begins serving the interests of a participant or group rather than its intended function.

Potential targets include:

- treasury;
- reputation;
- verification;
- governance;
- identity;
- monetary policy;
- community project approval.

Capture indicators should be monitored.

No institution should be assumed immune merely because it was designed under Concord principles.

# 125. Recursive Failure

Oversight can fail.

The mechanism intended to detect failure can itself become:

- captured;
- incompetent;
- corrupted;
- outdated.

```text
Operational mechanism
    ↓
Ordinary oversight
    ↓
Independent oversight
    ↓
Constitutional review
    ↓
Review of compromised oversight itself
```

There is no assumption that the first reviewer is infallible.

# 160. Why the MVP Should Initially Avoid Currency

Currency introduces additional variables:

- monetary demand;
- inflation;
- liquidity;
- speculation;
- regulatory issues;
- wealth effects;
- financial incentives.

If the first experiment contains all of these simultaneously, failure may become difficult to diagnose.

Testing the coordination layer first allows clearer questions:

- Can useful contribution be identified?
- Can provenance be collected proportionately?
- Can contextual trust operate without becoming social scoring?
- Can participants contest decisions?
- Can gaming be detected?
- Does the system produce useful reciprocal cooperation?

Only after these questions are better understood should a monetary pilot be considered.

# 177. Final Architectural Principle

Ratchet can be reduced to the following core proposition:

> **Enable participants to make useful contributions, establish sufficient evidence of those contributions, develop contextual and recoverable trust, exchange capabilities and resources reciprocally, measure the resulting outcomes, preserve demonstrated improvements, correct demonstrated failures, and retain enough knowledge to learn from both.**

Everything else in Ratchet is an attempt to make that proposition operational without allowing its mechanisms to become new sources of uncontrolled power.

# 178. Conclusion

Ratchet began as a proposal for a new economic system.

Its development exposed a deeper dependency.

Before a contribution-backed economy can operate, the system must answer:

- what contribution means;
- how contribution is evidenced;
- how evidence is attributed;
- how evidence becomes trust;
- how trust remains contextual;
- how different participants cooperate;
- how reciprocal benefit is established;
- how failure is corrected;
- how improvement persists;
- how the mechanisms themselves remain constrained.

The resulting architecture is therefore broader than currency.

Ratchet V4.2 is a general protocol for:

> **participation, contribution, provenance, evidence, contextual trust, reciprocity, exchange, feedback and controlled persistence.**

Its monetary system remains an important application.

Its economic mechanisms remain hypotheses requiring testing.

Its coordination principles require independent evaluation.

Its governance mechanisms require adversarial examination.

Its AI mechanisms contain significant unresolved questions.

Its own development provides evidence about the Concord methodology operating in practice, but not proof that the resulting system will succeed.

Ratchet should therefore be neither dismissed as merely speculative nor presented as proven.

Its correct status is:

> **a developed, internally audited, canonically specified and explicitly testable Concord architecture awaiting systematic external and empirical validation.**
