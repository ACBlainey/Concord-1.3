# Ratchet

## A General Coordination, Contribution, Trust and Reciprocal Participation Protocol

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey

# 1. Purpose and Scope

Ratchet is a general coordination architecture for enabling participants to contribute, establish evidence concerning those contributions, develop contextual trust, exchange capabilities and resources, coordinate collective activity, and preserve demonstrated improvements without making those improvements immune from later correction.

Ratchet began as an economic and monetary proposal. Through recursive development, criticism, extension and examination, it became apparent that a functioning contribution-backed economic system depended upon deeper questions:

- What constitutes contribution?
- How can contribution be distinguished from effort?
- How can contribution be evidenced?
- How can evidence be verified?
- How can provenance be preserved?
- How should trust arise from observed behaviour?
- How can trust remain contextual rather than becoming a universal social score?
- How can participants with radically different capabilities participate fairly?
- How can humans and artificial intelligences participate in the same coordination architecture without assuming that they are identical?
- How can economic participation remain voluntary?
- How can beneficial changes persist without becoming irreversible?
- How can systems detect when their own measurements are being gamed?
- How can governance prevent the mechanisms of contribution and reputation from becoming mechanisms of domination?
- How can the system itself be corrected when its assumptions prove wrong?

These questions expanded Ratchet beyond its original monetary purpose.

Ratchet treats monetary architecture as one application of a more general coordination protocol.

The Ratchet architecture is concerned with:

1. participants;
2. participation;
3. contribution;
4. evidence;
5. provenance;
6. verification;
7. contextual trust;
8. reputation;
9. reciprocity;
10. exchange;
11. community coordination;
12. economic mechanisms;
13. measurement;
14. feedback;
15. failure;
16. correction;
17. controlled persistence;
18. governance;
19. constitutional constraint;
20. learning.

Ratchet does not attempt to replace the other Concord systems.

It is not the Constitution.

It is not the Judiciary.

It is not the Knowledge Control System.

It is not the Metrics architecture.

It is not the Roadway Equilibrium Framework.

It is not the Recursive Oversight system.

It is not the Continuity Protocol.

Ratchet interacts with each of these systems while remaining bounded by them.

Its role is narrower:

> **Ratchet provides a general architecture through which participation, contribution, evidence, trust, reciprocity, exchange and controlled improvement may be coordinated.**

# 2. Ratchet Architecture

The architecture contains several different epistemic classes of material:

### Established Concordian principles

Principles already integrated into the wider Concord architecture, such as:

- proportionality;
- autonomy;
- consent;
- reviewability;
- evidence;
- transparency;
- stewardship;
- reversibility;
- recursive oversight.

### Derived Ratchet principles

Principles developed through Ratchet's internal evolution, including:

- contextual trust;
- recoverable trust;
- contribution broader than employment;
- capability-aware participation;
- proportional provenance;
- reciprocal participation;
- controlled persistence.

### Economic and implementation mechanisms

Mechanisms with specific design, implementation and evaluation requirements include:

- Progressive Monetary Backing;
- Personal Backing Balance;
- Personal Backing Ratio;
- Proof of Useful Contribution implementations;
- contribution difficulty levels;
- specific reputation mechanisms;
- particular treasury structures.

### Open questions

Problems for which Ratchet does not yet claim a satisfactory solution.

### Evaluation requirements

Mechanisms whose consequences must be evaluated through suitable simulation, controlled experiments, pilot systems or other empirical methods.

Ratchet must preserve these distinctions.

# 3. The Ratchet Principle

A mechanical ratchet permits movement in one direction while resisting uncontrolled reversal.

The civilisational Ratchet uses the metaphor more carefully.

Ratchet does **not** mean:

> Every change should become permanent.

Nor does it mean:

> Progress can only move in one direction.

Instead:

> **Changes that demonstrate beneficial outcomes should be capable of persisting, while harmful, obsolete or failed changes must remain reversible.**

This produces:

```text
Proposal
    ↓
Action
    ↓
Observation
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Evaluation
    ↓
Demonstrated improvement?
    ↓
Provisional persistence
    ↓
Continued measurement
    ↓
Review
    ↓
Persist / Modify / Reverse / Replace
```

The architecture therefore combines two requirements that might otherwise appear contradictory:

**continuity** and **correction**.

Ratchet seeks to preserve demonstrated improvement without preserving demonstrated error.

---

# 4. Controlled Persistence

Persistence is not justified merely because a mechanism already exists.

A mechanism earns stronger persistence through evidence.

Relevant considerations may include:

- demonstrated benefit;
- repeatability;
- reliability;
- resilience;
- low unresolved harm;
- compatibility with constitutional principles;
- performance under changing conditions;
- resistance to gaming;
- absence of superior demonstrated alternatives.

Persistence should weaken where:

- performance deteriorates;
- significant harms emerge;
- circumstances materially change;
- measurement proves unreliable;
- capture occurs;
- assumptions fail;
- superior mechanisms are demonstrated.

The progression is therefore:

```text
Candidate
    ↓
Provisional use
    ↓
Observation
    ↓
Repeated evidence
    ↓
Increasing confidence
    ↓
Greater persistence
```

but never:

```text
Persistence
    ↓
Immunity from review
```

Ratchet therefore rejects both uncontrolled change and uncontrolled permanence.

---

# 5. Preservation Without Stagnation

Ratchet distinguishes between preserving knowledge and preserving mechanisms.

A mechanism may fail and be removed.

The knowledge of that failure should normally remain.

For example:

```text
Mechanism adopted
    ↓
Evidence gathered
    ↓
Mechanism fails
    ↓
Mechanism withdrawn
    ↓
Failure recorded
    ↓
Knowledge preserved
    ↓
Future designs informed
```

This creates a general rule:

> **Preserve memory without preserving error.**

Ratchet therefore depends upon the Concord's knowledge and continuity architecture.

Operational decisions remain revisable.

Their evidential history should remain available for future learning subject to privacy, security and lawful retention constraints.

# 6. The Fundamental Ratchet Architecture

The core Ratchet sequence is:

```text
PARTICIPANT
    ↓
PARTICIPATION
    ↓
CONTRIBUTION
    ↓
EVIDENCE
    ↓
PROVENANCE
    ↓
VERIFICATION
    ↓
CONTEXTUAL TRUST
    ↓
RECIPROCITY
    ↓
EXCHANGE / COORDINATION
    ↓
MEASUREMENT
    ↓
REVIEW
    ↓
LEARNING
    ↓
CONTROLLED PERSISTENCE OR CORRECTION
```

This sequence is not necessarily linear in practice.

A real Ratchet system may contain many simultaneous feedback loops.

New evidence may revise trust.

New measurements may alter contribution criteria.

A dispute may cause earlier evidence to be re-examined.

A failed mechanism may generate a new hypothesis.

A participant may leave and later return.

Ratchet is therefore better understood as a recursive coordination architecture than as a one-way process.

# 7. Participants

A Ratchet participant is an entity recognised by a particular Ratchet implementation as capable of participating in one or more defined roles.

Possible participants may include:

- individual humans;
- groups;
- communities;
- organisations;
- companies;
- institutions;
- artificial intelligences;
- autonomous systems;
- hybrid human–AI systems;
- future forms of intelligence.

Recognition as a Ratchet participant does not by itself establish:

- personhood;
- sentience;
- citizenship;
- ownership;
- constitutional sovereignty;
- political authority;
- moral equivalence between different substrates.

Those questions belong to the wider Concord constitutional and intelligence architecture.

Ratchet asks a narrower question:

> **Can this entity participate in this defined coordination process, and under what constraints?**

---

# 8. Participation Does Not Equal Authority

Participation must be distinguished from authority.

An entity may:

- provide information;
- contribute labour;
- perform computation;
- evaluate evidence;
- supply resources;
- participate in exchange;

without therefore possessing governance authority.

Thus:

```text
Participation
≠
Authority
```

Likewise:

```text
Capability
≠
Authority
```

and:

```text
Contribution
≠
Authority
```

Any authority exercised through Ratchet must arise through separately defined governance and constitutional processes.

---

### 8.1 Standing Functional Mandates and Bounded Authority

Ratchet's routine coordination, recording, matching and verification functions may operate under an expressly assigned standing functional mandate. Such a mandate is bounded by function, scope, applicable rules and oversight; it does not require a new triadic governance vote for each ordinary permitted operation. A participant's capability, contribution, reputation or possession of records does not confer that mandate. Ratchet cannot enlarge its own mandate, command other domains or create emergency authority. Decisions outside the mandate require the competent existing authorisation process.

# 9. Universal Opportunity Rather Than Uniform Output

Ratchet should seek broad opportunity to participate.

It should not require identical output from every participant.

Participants differ in:

- capability;
- knowledge;
- resources;
- health;
- circumstances;
- responsibilities;
- developmental stage;
- physical embodiment;
- cognitive architecture;
- available time;
- access to infrastructure.

Therefore:

> **Equal respect does not require identical contribution.**

Nor does fairness require everyone to produce the same quantity or type of output.

A coordination system that ignores real differences may produce inequality while claiming formal equality.

---

# 10. Capability-Aware Participation

Where relevant and practical, Ratchet may evaluate participation relative to capability and circumstance.

This principle originated partly from earlier Worker Value reasoning and was expanded through Ratchet's development.

Capability-aware participation does not mean that every participant receives whatever they request merely because their circumstances differ.

It means that contribution should not be evaluated through a single universal productivity standard.

For example:

- a disabled human may contribute through forms of activity different from an able-bodied human;
- a caregiver may make valuable contributions not captured by conventional employment;
- a highly capable AI may perform computational work at a scale impossible for a human;
- a human may provide embodied judgement, social understanding or physical action unavailable to a digital system.

The purpose is not to erase differences.

It is to coordinate them.

---

# 11. Capability Is Not Worth

Capability-aware participation requires an explicit safeguard.

Greater capability does not imply greater intrinsic worth.

Likewise, lower capability does not imply lower intrinsic worth.

Ratchet therefore rejects:

```text
Capability
=
Human value
```

and:

```text
Economic productivity
=
Human value
```

No Ratchet measure of:

- output;
- contribution;
- income;
- wealth;
- reputation;
- trust;
- backing;
- participation level;

should be interpreted as a measure of the intrinsic worth of a person or other constitutionally recognised entity.

---

# 12. Contribution

Contribution is an action, resource, capability, service, creation, intervention or other input that produces or supports an outcome recognised as useful within a defined context.

Contribution may include:

- physical work;
- intellectual work;
- research;
- education;
- caregiving;
- mentoring;
- creative work;
- technical work;
- entrepreneurship;
- environmental restoration;
- accessibility work;
- open-source development;
- governance participation;
- infrastructure maintenance;
- emergency response;
- community organisation;
- knowledge preservation;
- analysis;
- computation;
- coordination.

This list is illustrative rather than exhaustive.

Ratchet deliberately rejects the assumption that contribution is equivalent to conventional paid employment.

---

# 13. Contribution Is Contextual

An action may be useful in one context and useless or harmful in another.

Therefore contribution cannot be determined solely from the type of activity.

For example:

- building infrastructure may be useful where infrastructure is needed;
- building redundant infrastructure may waste resources;
- removing vegetation may be useful for fire management in one environment and ecologically harmful in another;
- producing information may be useful where the information is accurate and needed, but harmful where it creates noise or misinformation.

Contribution assessment therefore requires context.

---

# 14. Effort Is Not Automatically Value

Ratchet explicitly distinguishes effort from contribution.

```text
Effort
≠
Contribution
≠
Useful outcome
```

A participant may expend great effort producing little value.

Another participant may identify a simple solution producing enormous value with little visible effort.

The system should therefore avoid rewarding effort merely because it is measurable.

Effort may be relevant.

It is not sufficient.

---

# 15. Difficulty Is Not Automatically Value

The same applies to difficulty.

```text
Difficult
≠
Useful
```

and:

```text
Easy
≠
Worthless
```

Contribution difficulty may be represented by a context-specific scale, potentially 1–5. The exact classification, evidence and consequences are to be designed in the contribution mechanism; the scale alone does not determine usefulness or reward.

Difficulty may be relevant to:

- effort;
- scarcity;
- training;
- risk;
- complexity;
- reward.

But difficulty should never become a substitute for usefulness.

A simple intervention solving a major problem may be more valuable than an extremely difficult intervention solving nothing.

---

# 16. Useful Contribution

A useful contribution should produce, enable, preserve or materially support an outcome that the relevant system has reason to value.

Possible domains include:

- economic value;
- environmental value;
- educational value;
- social value;
- scientific value;
- infrastructure;
- resilience;
- care;
- accessibility;
- knowledge;
- safety;
- community capability.

Usefulness should not be defined solely through monetary price.

Some contributions may have substantial value without an obvious market price.

Likewise, market price does not automatically establish social usefulness.

---

# 17. Contribution Recognition Is Not Automatic Payment

Recognition and payment are separate processes.

```text
Recognised contribution
≠
Automatic monetary reward
```

Some recognised contributions may receive monetary compensation.

Others may produce:

- reputation;
- acknowledgement;
- reciprocal access;
- capability;
- community standing;
- knowledge;
- non-monetary benefit.

Some ordinary economic transactions may involve payment without requiring formal Proof of Useful Contribution.

Ratchet should therefore distinguish:

1. ordinary exchange;
2. recognised contribution;
3. public or community contribution;
4. contribution affecting collective backing;
5. stewardship or constitutional participation.

These categories may overlap but should not be collapsed into one universal reward mechanism.

---

# 18. Contribution and Incentives

Any system that rewards contribution changes behaviour.

This creates both opportunity and risk.

A well-designed incentive can encourage beneficial activity.

A poorly designed incentive can encourage participants to optimise the measurement rather than the purpose.

Therefore:

> **Ratchet must evaluate not only what a reward intends to encourage, but what behaviour the reward actually produces.**

This includes second-order effects.

A reward for community participation may increase participation.

It may also:

- displace voluntary motivation;
- encourage superficial activity;
- create artificial projects;
- favour easily measured work;
- disadvantage invisible forms of contribution;
- encourage contribution laundering.

Incentive alignment is therefore a core Ratchet design problem.

---

# 19. Positive-Sum Coordination

Ratchet should preferentially support arrangements in which participants can create additional value rather than merely compete over a fixed pool.

This is the principle of positive-sum coordination.

Examples may include:

- new knowledge;
- improved infrastructure;
- restored ecosystems;
- better education;
- useful software;
- scientific discovery;
- increased resilience;
- mutually beneficial exchange.

Positive-sum coordination is a design preference, not a claim that every Ratchet transaction will benefit everyone.

Conflict, scarcity and trade-offs remain real.

The principle is simply that the architecture should not unnecessarily assume that one participant can benefit only through another participant's loss.

---

# 20. Provenance

Provenance records the origin, development and transformation of relevant information, actions and contributions.

It helps answer:

- Who contributed?
- What did they contribute?
- When?
- Under what conditions?
- What evidence supports the claim?
- What tools were used?
- Was AI involved?
- What revisions occurred?
- Who verified the result?
- What outcome followed?

A simplified provenance chain is:

```text
Idea
    ↓
Contributor
    ↓
Reasoning / Process
    ↓
Evidence
    ↓
Tools / AI interaction
    ↓
Revision
    ↓
Verification
    ↓
Outcome
    ↓
Correction
    ↓
Legacy
```

Provenance supports accountability.

It does not by itself establish truth.
