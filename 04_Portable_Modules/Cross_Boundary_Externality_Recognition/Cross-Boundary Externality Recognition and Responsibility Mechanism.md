# Cross-Boundary Externality Recognition and Responsibility Mechanism

## A Bounded Architecture for Consequences Across Civilisational Boundaries

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey — Independent Researcher
**Project:** The Concord Framework
**Status:** CANONICAL DEVELOPMENT — INITIAL MECHANISM / CORRIGIBLE
**Owner domain:** Disputes, Jurisdiction and Externalities
**Derived from:** Development Freeze 001; Civilisation Clock Queue Test 002
**Date:** September 2026

## 1. Purpose

Civilisations can affect one another without sharing a government, legal system, ontology of personhood or political trust.

An environmental release may cross a boundary. Infrastructure failure may interrupt another civilisation's systems. Digital activity may propagate beyond the originating jurisdiction. A civilisation may affect entities that it does not recognise as persons but another civilisation does.

The architectural problem is therefore not merely dispute resolution.

It is:

> **How can a consequential cross-boundary effect become legible, evidenced, contestable and capable of reaching an appropriate response pathway without turning consequence recognition into supracivilisational sovereignty?**

This paper defines the first Concord mechanism for that function.

## 2. Core Separation

The mechanism depends upon preserving several distinctions:

**Consequence ≠ Responsibility**

**Responsibility ≠ Jurisdiction**

**Jurisdiction ≠ Remedy**

**Remedy ≠ General Authority**

**Communication ≠ Recognition**

**Coordination ≠ Subordination**

A system that collapses these distinctions can turn a legitimate concern about harm into an illegitimate expansion of authority.

## 3. Mechanism Overview

The mechanism is the **Cross-Boundary Externality Recognition and Responsibility Mechanism (CBERRM).**

Its core flow is:

**Signal/Event**
→ **Externality Candidate**
→ **Affected-Party/Domain Identification**
→ **Standing-to-Raise Assessment**
→ **Evidence and Causal Confidence**
→ **Materiality Assessment**
→ **Responsibility Mapping**
→ **Jurisdiction/Coordination Mapping**
→ **Minimum Necessary Response Path**
→ **Review/Correction**
→ **Provenance and State Update**

CBERRM does not itself govern another civilisation.

It produces a bounded, evidence-bearing externality case state that legitimate participants can use.

## 4. Externality Candidate

An externality candidate may be raised when there is credible reason to believe an action, omission, failure, persistent condition or system state produces a material consequence outside the originating civil domain.

Candidate classes include:

- environmental/resource;
- infrastructure;
- informational/digital;
- biological/health;
- economic/resource-flow;
- security/safety;
- rights/personhood;
- migration/mobility;
- shared-space/shared-system;
- delayed or cumulative;
- unknown/mixed.

Opening a candidate means only:

> **This possible consequence warrants bounded examination.**

It does not establish harm, fault or jurisdiction.

## 5. Standing to Raise

A case may be raised by:
- a directly affected participant;
- a recognised representative of an affected population;
- a legitimate civil system acting within its remit;
- a shared infrastructure/resource operator;
- an agreed monitoring interface;
- another party presenting credible evidence where the affected party cannot practically raise the issue.

Standing to raise a question is deliberately weaker than standing to impose a remedy.

**Standing to Raise ≠ Authority to Decide.**

Where personhood or representation is disputed, the case can retain a standing state of **DISPUTED** while evidence is examined.

This avoids allowing one civilisation's classification system to erase a possible affected party before the underlying issue can even be represented.

## 6. Evidence State

CBERRM records evidence without requiring premature certainty.

Candidate evidence states:

- OBSERVED;
- CORROBORATED;
- CONTESTED;
- INCOMPLETE;
- INDIRECT;
- MODEL-DEPENDENT;
- UNKNOWN;
- DISPUTED;
- REFUTED;
- SUPERSEDED.

Causal confidence should be represented separately from consequence severity.

A severe alleged consequence with weak causal evidence is not equivalent to a moderate consequence with strong causal evidence.

Where evidence systems differ between civilisations, provenance and translation assumptions must remain visible.

## 7. Materiality

Not every cross-boundary effect justifies formal coordination.

Materiality may consider:
- severity;
- scale;
- duration;
- reversibility;
- recurrence;
- affected population/system;
- rights impact;
- resource/ecological impact;
- dependency criticality;
- uncertainty;
- cumulative effect;
- recovery cost.

No universal scalar score is required.

A low-probability but irreversible consequence may require attention even where evidence remains uncertain.

## 8. Responsibility Mapping

Responsibility should not be assumed from origin alone.

Candidate responsibility states include:

- DIRECT;
- CONTRIBUTORY;
- SHARED;
- INDIRECT;
- FAILURE-TO-MITIGATE;
- UNKNOWN;
- DISPUTED;
- NOT-ESTABLISHED.

Multi-causal cases should preserve multiple contributors rather than forcing a single responsible actor.

Responsibility mapping should also distinguish historical cause from current capacity to mitigate.

A party may be able to reduce harm without being solely responsible for causing it.

## 9. Jurisdiction and Coordination Mapping

After consequence and responsibility are represented, the system asks what legitimate pathway exists.

Possible states include:

- INTERNAL-JURISDICTION;
- SHARED/AGREED-JURISDICTION;
- OVERLAPPING-JURISDICTION;
- JURISDICTION-DISPUTED;
- NO-SHARED-JURISDICTION;
- VOLUNTARY-COORDINATION-AVAILABLE;
- OPTIONAL-MEDIATION-AVAILABLE;
- EMERGENCY-CONTACT-ONLY;
- PEACEFUL-DISTANCE/NO-FURTHER-PROCESS;
- UNKNOWN.

The absence of shared jurisdiction does not erase the externality.

Equally, the existence of an externality does not create jurisdiction.

## 10. Minimum Necessary Response

The response should be bounded to the function required by the consequence.

Possible responses include:
- information exchange;
- verification;
- warning;
- temporary mitigation;
- technical coordination;
- compensation/repair negotiation;
- bounded operational agreement;
- functionally bounded summit;
- agreed mediation;
- shared-resource procedure;
- separate legitimate judicial process where jurisdiction already exists;
- monitoring;
- no action with recorded rationale.

The architecture asks:

> **What is the minimum response scope required to address the demonstrated consequence?**

This mirrors Concord's minimum-authority and minimum-negotiation grammar.

## 11. Functionally Bounded Externality Summit

Where direct technical coordination is insufficient, CBERRM may hand off to an existing Functionally Bounded Summit.

The summit receives:
- externality case;
- evidence state;
- affected parties;
- disputed points;
- material dependencies;
- permitted scope;
- explicit exclusions;
- scope-expansion rule.

Failure should remain bounded where practicable.

A failed water-externality negotiation should not automatically invalidate unrelated scientific, humanitarian or communication interfaces.

## 12. Scope Expansion

Some externalities genuinely cross domains.

Scope may expand only when:
1. a material dependency is demonstrated;
2. the expansion is recorded;
3. newly relevant participants are given appropriate standing;
4. the expansion does not silently create general jurisdiction.

**Functional Boundedness ≠ Functional Isolation.**

## 13. Non-Participation

A party may refuse participation.

CBERRM must represent that refusal without pretending either:
- that the externality therefore disappears; or
- that refusal authorises coercive jurisdiction.

Possible state:

**PARTY-NONPARTICIPATING / EXTERNALITY-STATE-RETAINED.**

Other parties may continue evidence preservation, mitigation within their own legitimate domains, warnings, peaceful-distance measures or other independently authorised responses.

**Refusal ≠ Exoneration.**

**Refusal ≠ Automatic Authority Over Refuser.**

## 14. Rights and Personhood Disagreement

Suppose Civilisation A regards a class of AI as property while Civilisation B regards them as persons.

An action by A materially affects those AIs across a boundary.

CBERRM must not require the personhood dispute to be resolved before the possible consequence can be represented.

Instead it can record:

**Affected Entity Standing: DISPUTED**

alongside evidence of the consequence.

Rights Translation and Recognition mechanisms can then supply relevant standing analysis without CBERRM itself deciding universal personhood.

This preserves:

**Foreign Classification ≠ Automatic Epistemic Authority Over Standing.**

and:

**Disputed Standing ≠ No Possible Harm.**

## 15. Low-Trust Operation

CBERRM must remain usable where trust is low.

It therefore favours:
- narrow scope;
- explicit evidence provenance;
- independent corroboration where available;
- reversible interim measures;
- no inference from participation to recognition;
- function-specific communication;
- visible disagreement states;
- bounded failure.

A party need not trust the other's entire government to verify a river contaminant, network incident or shared-orbit collision risk.

## 16. Correction

Externality cases are corrigible.

New evidence may:
- strengthen causation;
- weaken causation;
- reveal another contributor;
- change materiality;
- resolve standing;
- change jurisdiction;
- show mitigation succeeded;
- invalidate an earlier claim.

Case state therefore follows:

**Case State → New Evidence/Outcome → Review → Correction/Supersession → Updated Case State.**

Correction must propagate to materially dependent decisions.

## 17. Provenance

The case record should preserve:
- who raised it;
- what was alleged;
- evidence available at each stage;
- confidence and uncertainty;
- standing disputes;
- responsibility hypotheses;
- jurisdiction claims;
- proposed and rejected remedies;
- scope changes;
- participation/refusal;
- outcomes;
- corrections;
- unresolved questions.

Later agreement must not erase the provenance of earlier uncertainty.

## 18. Machine-Readable Candidate Record

A future interoperable record could contain:

**ExternalityCase = <ID, Origin, AffectedDomain, AffectedParties, Standing, Evidence, Causality, Materiality, Responsibility, Jurisdiction, ResponseScope, Participants, State, Review, Provenance>.**

Candidate case states:

- SIGNAL;
- OPEN;
- EVIDENCE-GATHERING;
- CONTESTED;
- MATERIALITY-CONFIRMED;
- RESPONSIBILITY-PARTIAL;
- RESPONSIBILITY-ESTABLISHED;
- JURISDICTION-MAPPED;
- COORDINATION-ACTIVE;
- MITIGATION-ACTIVE;
- REPAIR-ACTIVE;
- MONITORING;
- RESOLVED;
- CLOSED-NO-ACTION;
- SUPERSEDED;
- UNKNOWN;
- DISPUTED.

These states are descriptive, not grants of authority.

## 19. Test 1 — Environmental / Resource

Civilisation A's industrial activity contaminates a river used by Civilisation B.

CBERRM:
1. opens an environmental externality candidate;
2. identifies B and affected resource users;
3. records water measurements and provenance;
4. separates causal confidence from severity;
5. maps A's contribution and other causes;
6. determines whether shared-resource agreements exist;
7. selects minimum response: data exchange, source mitigation and bounded repair negotiation;
8. escalates to a bounded summit only if necessary.

**Result:** mechanism usable without general jurisdiction over A.

## 20. Test 2 — Infrastructure

A's orbital or network infrastructure fails and disables services relied upon by B.

Responsibility may be shared if B knowingly relied on an unguaranteed service or another actor caused the failure.

CBERRM preserves:
- dependency;
- failure cause;
- responsibility distribution;
- restoration capacity;
- existing service agreements;
- minimum restoration/repair coordination.

**Result:** consequence can be handled without converting technical dependence into political subordination.

## 21. Test 3 — Informational / Digital

A model, signal or autonomous digital process originating in A causes harmful effects in B.

Physical location may not map cleanly to agency or jurisdiction.

CBERRM records:
- originating infrastructure;
- controlling/authorising parties where known;
- propagation path;
- affected systems;
- causal uncertainty;
- containment options;
- jurisdiction dispute.

**Result:** UNKNOWN and DISPUTED states remain representable; no false territorial shortcut is required.

## 22. Test 4 — Rights / Personhood

A treats an AI population as property; B recognises comparable systems as persons. An action by A affects AI entities within B or a shared domain.

CBERRM records the consequence and disputed standing without deciding sovereignty/personhood itself.

Rights Translation and Recognition becomes an interface dependency.

**Result:** one civilisation cannot erase the externality simply by denying the affected class standing.

## 23. Test 5 — Low Trust / Hostility

A and B have hostile political relations but share a flood-control system.

A release decision threatens B.

CBERRM can operate through a narrow technical channel and, if needed, a Functionally Bounded Summit.

Participation does not imply wider recognition or reconciliation.

**Result:** low political trust does not require zero functional coordination.

## 24. Test 6 — Non-Participation

A refuses the process despite evidence of a material effect on B.

The case remains open with refusal recorded.

B may undertake independently legitimate mitigation and preserve evidence. Any stronger response requires separate legitimate authority.

**Result:** refusal neither deletes the problem nor manufactures jurisdiction.

## 25. Test 7 — Multi-Causal Externality

Several civilisations contribute to orbital debris that threatens all participants.

CBERRM records contributory/shared responsibility rather than forcing one defendant.

Mitigation may be allocated by capability, contribution, agreement or another legitimate mechanism without confusing mitigation capacity with sole causal responsibility.

**Result:** distributed causation is representable.

## 26. Adversarial Checks

### Jurisdiction laundering

Claim: “You harmed us, therefore we may govern your internal system.”

**Rejected.**

### Standing laundering

Claim: “We filed a case, therefore our preferred representative speaks for everyone affected.”

**Rejected.**

### Recognition laundering

Claim: “You negotiated with us, therefore you recognise our sovereignty.”

**Rejected.**

### Refusal laundering

Claim: “We refused the process, therefore no valid externality finding can exist.”

**Rejected.**

### Evidence laundering

Claim: “The consequence is severe, therefore causation is proven.”

**Rejected.**

### Peaceful-distance abuse

Claim: “We choose peaceful distance, therefore consequences we impose outside our boundary are irrelevant.”

**Rejected.**

Peaceful distance protects legitimate separation, not consequence denial.

## 27. Ownership and Interfaces

CBERRM belongs in the existing **Disputes, Jurisdiction and Externalities** domain.

It interfaces with:
- Civilisational Wrapper architecture for legibility and communication;
- Intercivilisational Protocols for bounded negotiation;
- Rights Translation and Recognition for disputed standing;
- Peaceful Distance for bounded separation;
- KCS for evidence/provenance/dependency state;
- CDT for topology and consequence relationships;
- existing Judiciary/Governance only where legitimate jurisdiction already exists;
- optional mediation only by explicit authorisation.

CBERRM itself is not a court, government, mediator or enforcement authority.

## Conclusion

Cross-boundary consequences require civilisational legibility without supracivilisational sovereignty.

CBERRM supplies a minimum mechanism:

**recognise the possible consequence, preserve uncertainty, identify affected standing, evaluate evidence and causation, map responsibility separately from jurisdiction, select the minimum legitimate response path, and preserve correction and provenance.**

Its constitutional boundary is as important as its positive function:

> **A civilisation's actions do not become consequence-free at its border; another civilisation's suffering does not automatically create authority over the actor.**
