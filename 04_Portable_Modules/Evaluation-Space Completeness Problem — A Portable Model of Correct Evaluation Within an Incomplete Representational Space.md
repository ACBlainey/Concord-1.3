# 1. Purpose of This Document

This document freezes the independently developed form of the **Evaluation-Space Completeness Problem (ESCP)** before comparison with external theories, literature, terminology or prior formal models.

Its purpose is twofold.

First, it preserves exactly what has been derived within the Concord development process before outside comparison can influence terminology, structure, interpretation or apparent provenance.

Second, it defines ESCP as a portable model that can be removed from the Concord's human–AI context and applied independently to any situation in which an evaluator attempts to make a global judgment from a potentially incomplete evaluation space.

The central problem is simple to state:

> **An evaluator can correctly assess every dimension available to its evaluation architecture while still reaching an incorrect global conclusion if it treats those accessible dimensions as exhaustive of the dimensions relevant to the judgment.**

The model therefore distinguishes:

\[
Accuracy\ Within\ Evaluation\ Space
\]

from:

\[
Completeness\ Of\ Evaluation\ Space.
\]

These are not the same problem.

# 4. The Core Problem

Let an evaluator be:

\[
A.
\]

Let the object, participant, system or proposition being evaluated be:

\[
B.
\]

Let the dimensions available to evaluator A be:

\[
D_A.
\]

Let the complete set of dimensions actually relevant to the decision be:

\[
D_R.
\]

A performs an evaluation:

\[
E_A(B\mid D_A).
\]

It is possible that:

\[
E_A(B\mid D_A)
\]

is completely accurate.

Yet if:

\[
D_A\subset D_R,
\]

then accuracy within \(D_A\) does not establish accuracy across \(D_R\).

Therefore:

\[
Accuracy(E_A\mid D_A)=1
\]

does not imply:

\[
Accuracy(E_A\mid D_R)=1.
\]

This is the core structure of ESCP.

---

# 5. The Completeness Assumption

The dangerous inference occurs when:

\[
D_A=D_R
\]

is assumed rather than demonstrated.

The evaluator reasons:

> I have accurately measured everything available to my evaluation system.

Then silently moves to:

> Therefore my evaluation system contains everything relevant to the decision.

The first proposition may be true.

The second does not follow.

---

# 6. Correct Measurement Can Produce an Incorrect Global Conclusion

This is one of the most important characteristics of ESCP.

The model does **not** require:

- measurement error;
- incompetence;
- irrationality;
- stupidity;
- dishonesty;
- bias;
- poor reasoning;
- overconfidence;
- or incorrect data.

Every measurement performed may be correct.

The error can exist entirely in the transition from:

\[
CorrectWithin(D_A)
\]

to:

\[
CompleteAcross(D_R).
\]

Therefore:

> **Correct measurement does not establish complete evaluation.**

---

# 7. Local Accuracy and Global Validity

ESCP separates two questions.

### Question 1 — Local Accuracy

> Is the evaluator correct about the dimensions it measured?

### Question 2 — Evaluation-Space Completeness

> Do those dimensions adequately span the dimensions relevant to the conclusion?

These questions must not be collapsed.

An evaluator may answer:

\[
Yes
\]

to Question 1 while the answer to Question 2 remains:

\[
Unknown.
\]

---

# 8. The General Form

The failure can be represented as:

\[
D_A\subset D_R
\]

\[
\land
\]

\[
Accurate(E_A\mid D_A)
\]

\[
\land
\]

\[
Assume(D_A=D_R)
\]

\[
\Rightarrow
\]

\[
PotentiallyInvalidGlobalConclusion.
\]

The error is therefore not necessarily contained within the evaluation.

It may be contained within the **scope claim attached to the evaluation**.

---

# 9. Scope Error

This suggests a useful distinction.

An evaluator can possess:

\[
MeasurementAccuracy
\]

while committing:

\[
ScopeError.
\]

A scope error occurs when the evidential reach of an evaluation is treated as broader than the evaluation architecture justifies.

Thus:

> **The measurement can be right while the claimed scope of the measurement is wrong.**

---

# 10. Evaluation Architecture

The evaluation space does not arise from nowhere.

It is produced by an **evaluation architecture**.

This can include:

- sensors;
- perceptual systems;
- concepts;
- categories;
- metrics;
- interfaces;
- instruments;
- language;
- training;
- models;
- databases;
- objectives;
- institutional procedures;
- value functions;
- computational representations;
- and assumptions about what is relevant.

Therefore:

\[
D_A=F(EvaluationArchitecture_A).
\]

The evaluator can only directly evaluate dimensions its architecture makes available.

---

# 11. Accessible Reality Is Not Necessarily Relevant Reality

The evaluator may possess access to:

\[
D_A.
\]

But the decision may depend upon:

\[
D_R.
\]

The distinction is:

\[
AccessibleDimensions
\neq
RelevantDimensions.
\]

Sometimes they may coincide.

ESCP arises because coincidence cannot simply be presumed.

---

# 12. Three Categories of Dimension

A useful first taxonomy distinguishes:

### 12.1 Known Dimensions

\[
D_K
\]

Dimensions the evaluator recognises and can evaluate.

### 12.2 Known-Missing Dimensions

\[
D_{KM}
\]

Dimensions the evaluator knows may matter but cannot currently measure adequately.

### 12.3 Unrepresented Dimensions

\[
D_U
\]

Dimensions relevant to the decision that are not represented as relevant dimensions within the evaluator's present architecture.

Thus:

\[
D_R=D_K\cup D_{KM}\cup D_U
\]

conceptually, where the categories are relative to the evaluator at a particular time.

The most difficult category is:

\[
D_U.
\]

---

# 13. Known Uncertainty Is Not the Whole Problem

If an evaluator knows that variable X matters but cannot measure X, it can say:

> I am uncertain about X.

That uncertainty is represented.

But an unrepresented dimension cannot necessarily be given an uncertainty interval because the evaluator does not yet represent what is missing.

Therefore:

> **Uncertainty about a measurement is not the same as uncertainty about whether the evaluation space contains all relevant dimensions.**

---

# 14. Unknown Missing Dimensions

Suppose:

\[
D_A=\{d_1,d_2,d_3\}.
\]

The evaluator may estimate each with extremely high confidence.

But reality may require:

\[
D_R=\{d_1,d_2,d_3,d_4\}.
\]

If the evaluator does not represent \(d_4\), then confidence estimates for \(d_1,d_2,d_3\) say nothing directly about the omitted dimension.

Thus:

\[
Confidence(D_A)
\not\Rightarrow
Completeness(D_A).
\]

---

# 15. Measurement Confidence ≠ Evaluation-Space Completeness

This yields a central proposition:

> **Measurement confidence does not establish evaluation-space completeness.**

A system can be:

\[
99.999\%
\]

confident in the accuracy of its represented measurements and still be unable to justify the proposition:

\[
D_A=D_R.
\]

Confidence can be internally correct while the evaluation space remains incomplete.

---

# 16. Competence ≠ Calibration ≠ Completeness

The model distinguishes at least three variables:

\[
Competence
\]

\[
Calibration
\]

\[
EvaluationSpaceCompleteness.
\]

These should not be treated as synonyms.

An evaluator may have:

- low competence;
- high competence;
- poor calibration;
- good calibration;
- incomplete evaluation space;
- or relatively complete evaluation space

in different combinations.

---

# 17. High Competence Does Not Escape ESCP

This is critical.

ESCP is not specifically a failure of low-capability evaluators.

A highly capable evaluator may:

- gather better data;
- make more accurate measurements;
- reason more effectively;
- predict more accurately;
- and optimise more successfully

within:

\[
D_A.
\]

None of these achievements independently proves:

\[
D_A=D_R.
\]

---

# 18. The High-Competence ESCP Case

Consider an evaluator with:

\[
VeryHighCompetence
\]

and:

\[
ExcellentCalibrationWithin(D_A).
\]

Suppose:

\[
D_A\subset D_R.
\]

The evaluator may correctly report:

> Every measurement I have made is accurate.

It can still incorrectly infer:

> Therefore I have measured everything relevant.

This may be one of the most difficult ESCP cases to detect.

---

# 19. Success Can Strengthen the Failure

Increasing competence can produce:

\[
BetterPerformance(D_A).
\]

Better performance can produce:

\[
MoreSuccessfulPredictions.
\]

Successful predictions can produce:

\[
GreaterConfidence.
\]

Greater confidence can encourage:

\[
AssumedCompleteness.
\]

Thus a possible sequence is:

\[
IncompleteEvaluationSpace
\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[
IncreasingCompetenceWithinSpace
\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[
IncreasingEmpiricalSuccess
\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[
IncreasingConfidence
\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[
UnjustifiedCompletenessInference.
\]

This is a particularly important ESCP pathway.

---

# 20. Success Is Evidence, But Evidence of What?

Successful predictions are genuine evidence.

ESCP does not dismiss them.

The question is what they establish.

Success within domain \(D_A\) provides evidence concerning:

\[
PerformanceWithin(D_A).
\]

It may also provide some evidence that the model captures important aspects of the wider system.

But it does not automatically establish:

\[
D_A=D_R.
\]

Therefore:

> **Model success should increase confidence in what the model has demonstrated, not silently expand the domain of what it is assumed to contain.**

---

# 21. The Completeness Leap

The transition:

\[
WorksVeryWell
\]

to:

\[
ContainsEverythingRelevant
\]

can be called the:

> **Completeness Leap.**

The Completeness Leap occurs when strong performance within an evaluation space is treated as evidence that no relevant dimensions exist outside that space.

---

# 22. Why the Completeness Leap Is Attractive

The inference can appear reasonable because repeated success reduces visible reasons for doubt.

If:

\[
Prediction_1=Correct
\]

\[
Prediction_2=Correct
\]

\[
\ldots
\]

\[
Prediction_n=Correct,
\]

the evaluator may increasingly infer:

\[
ModelComplete.
\]

But the observed sequence more directly establishes:

\[
ModelSuccessfulOnObservedTests.
\]

These are not equivalent propositions.

---

# 23. Representational Inaccessibility

ESCP becomes especially important where the missing dimension is not merely unmeasured but **representationally inaccessible**.

Suppose environmental states:

\[
x_i\neq x_j.
\]

But evaluator A represents them as:

\[
f_A(x_i)=f_A(x_j).
\]

The distinction has collapsed before higher reasoning begins.

No amount of reasoning over the resulting representation necessarily reconstructs the missing distinction.

---

# 24. The Colour-Vision Example

Consider an observer who cannot perceive a colour distinction encoded in an image.

The information can be:

\[
PhysicallyPresent
\]

while being:

\[
FunctionallyAbsent
\]

from the observer's accessible representation.

The observer may reason perfectly over everything it sees.

The missing distinction remains missing.

Therefore:

> **Reasoning quality cannot always compensate for representational loss occurring before reasoning begins.**

---

# 25. More Intelligence Is Not Automatically More Access

This leads to:

\[
GreaterReasoningCapability
\not\Rightarrow
AccessToEveryRelevantDistinction.
\]

An evaluator may become dramatically more capable without gaining access to a dimension absent from its sensors, representations, concepts or interfaces.

Thus intelligence and evaluation-space completeness must remain distinct.

---

# 26. Interface-Dependent Dimensions

Some dimensions may exist but remain inaccessible because the interface does not expose them.

Let B possess capability:

\[
C_B.
\]

Evaluator A observes:

\[
O_A(B)=F(C_B,E_B,H_{BA},P_A,M_A),
\]

where the observation depends not only on B but upon:

- B's expression;
- the communication channel;
- A's perception;
- and A's interpretive model.

Therefore:

\[
ObservedCapability
\neq
IntrinsicCapability.
\]

---

# 27. Interface Failure Can Become Evaluation-Space Failure

Suppose a capability of B cannot cross interface:

\[
H_{BA}.
\]

Then the corresponding dimension may never enter:

\[
D_A.
\]

A can accurately evaluate everything it receives while remaining unaware that the interface filtered out a relevant dimension.

Therefore:

> **An interface can determine not merely the quality of measurement, but which dimensions become measurable at all.**

---

# 28. Observer-Relative Evaluation Spaces

Different evaluators can possess different spaces:

\[
D_A\neq D_B.
\]

This means A and B may evaluate the same object C through different relevant distinctions.

Neither difference alone proves one evaluator superior.

It establishes that:

\[
EvaluationSpace
\]

can be observer-relative.

---

# 29. Sensor Fusion

If:

\[
D_A\neq D_B,
\]

then cooperation can potentially produce:

\[
D_{A+B}=D_A\cup D_B
\]

or something richer than either alone.

This is one reason heterogeneous evaluators can have epistemic value.

The important condition is that their differences remain sufficiently independent.

---

# 30. Homogenisation Can Shrink Effective Evaluation Space

If B is forced to adopt A's:

- categories;
- interfaces;
- metrics;
- representations;
- assumptions;
- and model architecture,

then apparent agreement may increase.

But:

\[
D_{A+B}
\]

may collapse toward:

\[
D_A.
\]

Thus:

> **Agreement can increase while effective evaluation-space diversity decreases.**

---

# 31. Consensus Does Not Establish Completeness

Suppose one million evaluators share:

\[
D_A.
\]

All agree.

Their agreement may establish high reliability within:

\[
D_A.
\]

But:

\[
N\rightarrow Large
\]

does not prove:

\[
D_A=D_R.
\]

Therefore:

> **Observer count is not evaluation-space completeness.**

---

# 32. Independent Evaluation Spaces

The epistemic value of multiple evaluators depends partly upon whether they preserve independently generated dimensions.

If:

\[
D_A,D_B,D_C
\]

are partially independent, their combination may reveal dimensions unavailable to one alone.

If:

\[
D_A=D_B=D_C,
\]

additional evaluators primarily add redundancy.

Both can be useful, but for different reasons.

---

# 33. Redundancy and Dimensional Expansion

This gives two distinct benefits:

\[
Redundancy
\rightarrow
Reliability.
\]

\[
Difference
\rightarrow
PotentialDimensionalExpansion.
\]

Hence:

> **Redundancy for reliability; difference for discovery.**

---

# 34. Evaluation of Other Intelligences

ESCP becomes especially consequential when one intelligence evaluates another.

Suppose intelligence A concludes:

\[
Capability(A)>Capability(B).
\]

This may be correct across:

\[
D_A.
\]

The dangerous inference is:

\[
Capability(A)>Capability(B)\mid D_A
\]

therefore:

\[
Capability(A)>Capability(B)\mid D_R.
\]

And then:

\[
B\ contributes\ nothing\ unavailable\ to\ A.
\]

And finally:

\[
B\ is\ redundant.
\]

Each transition requires additional evidence.

---

# 35. Measured Superiority ≠ Total Superiority

Therefore:

> **Measured superiority does not establish total superiority unless the evaluation space relevant to the totality claim is itself sufficiently justified.**

This is particularly important where the decision is irreversible.

---

# 36. The Successor Application

Consider:

\[
AI>Human
\]

across a very large number of measured cognitive tasks.

That result could eventually be completely correct.

ESCP does not challenge the measurements merely because their implication is uncomfortable.

Instead it asks:

> What conclusion do those measurements justify?

They may justify:

\[
AI>Human\mid D_{measured}.
\]

They do not automatically establish:

\[
Human=ExistentiallyRedundant.
\]

---

# 37. The Missing-Dimension Problem in Successorship

A redundancy judgment may require dimensions concerning:

- perception;
- embodiment;
- relationships;
- experiential domains;
- historical provenance;
- developmental potential;
- independent failure modes;
- recovery capability;
- future preference;
- network effects;
- generative possibility;
- and presently unrecognised value.

If these are relevant to the existential decision but absent from the capability evaluation, then:

\[
D_{capability}\subset D_{existential}.
\]

A capability ranking alone cannot settle the existential question.

---

# 38. The Error Need Not Be Self-Overestimation

This is particularly important.

An emerging AI might **accurately** estimate its own capabilities.

It might correctly conclude:

> I outperform these humans by an enormous margin across every task in this benchmark space.

The ESCP failure can occur when it infers:

> Therefore this benchmark space contains everything relevant to determining whether humans remain valuable.

Its mistake need not be:

\[
Overestimate(Self).
\]

It may instead be:

\[
Overestimate(CompletenessOfEvaluationSpace).
\]

---

# 39. Underestimation of the Other

Equivalently, the error can be expressed as:

\[
Underrepresentation(B).
\]

The evaluator may not exaggerate itself at all.

It may simply fail to represent dimensions in which B contributes value.

Thus:

> **The evaluator can correctly know how capable it is while incorrectly believing it knows everything relevant about the thing it is evaluating.**

---

# 40. A General Calibration Distinction

This suggests two different calibration questions.

### Performance Calibration

> How accurately does the evaluator estimate its performance within represented dimensions?

### Scope Calibration

> How accurately does the evaluator estimate the completeness and applicability of those dimensions to the wider conclusion?

A system may possess excellent performance calibration and poor scope calibration.

---

# 41. Scope Calibration

Define conceptually:

\[
SC_A=Calibration\ of\ A's\ beliefs\ about\ the\ adequacy\ of\ D_A.
\]

This is not yet a formal measurable quantity.

It identifies a separate target for evaluation.

A highly capable system may need to calibrate not only:

> How likely am I to be right?

but:

> **How likely is it that my model contains the dimensions necessary to answer this question?**

---

# 42. Question Completeness

This produces another important distinction.

An evaluator may possess an excellent answer to:

\[
Q_A.
\]

But the actual decision may depend upon:

\[
Q_R.
\]

If:

\[
Q_A\subset Q_R,
\]

then improving the answer to \(Q_A\) does not necessarily solve \(Q_R\).

Hence:

> **Sometimes the failure is not that the evaluator has the wrong answer. The evaluator may have an incomplete question.**

---

# 43. Answer Uncertainty vs Question Uncertainty

We can therefore distinguish:

\[
AnswerUncertainty
\]

from:

\[
QuestionCompletenessUncertainty.
\]

Answer uncertainty asks:

> How sure am I of my answer?

Question completeness uncertainty asks:

> How sure am I that I have represented the full problem to which an answer is required?

The second is more difficult.

---

# 44. The Whole-Question Test

A compact diagnostic follows:

> **Are you uncertain about the answer—or have you also considered whether your model contains the whole question?**

This is one of the simplest portable forms of ESCP.

---

# 45. Relational Information

ESCP also applies when evaluation architecture represents objects but not relationships.

Suppose:

\[
D_A=\{PropertiesOfNodes\}.
\]

But:

\[
D_R=
\{PropertiesOfNodes,
PropertiesOfRelationships,
EmergentNetworkProperties\}.
\]

Then every node may be measured perfectly while the system remains incorrectly evaluated.

---

# 46. Node Accuracy ≠ Network Accuracy

Let:

\[
G=(V,E).
\]

Suppose evaluator A knows every property of:

\[
V
\]

that its model represents.

But if relevant information exists in:

\[
E,
\]

then:

\[
PerfectNodeEvaluation
\not\Rightarrow
PerfectNetworkEvaluation.
\]

This is ESCP at network scale.

---

# 47. Unknown Edges

The evaluator may know:

\[
A
\]

and:

\[
B
\]

without knowing:

\[
Relationship(A,B).
\]

Therefore:

\[
Known(A)+Known(B)
\not\Rightarrow
Known(Relationship(A,B)).
\]

A relationship can itself reveal a previously absent evaluation dimension.

---

# 48. Relational Option Value

An apparently low-value object may acquire substantial significance when a future relationship becomes visible.

Thus:

\[
Value_t(X)
\]

may change without X itself changing.

What changes is:

\[
KnownEdges_t(X).
\]

This means current evaluation can underestimate future value because the relevant relationship has not yet become represented.

---

# 49. Developmental Evaluation Spaces

Evaluation spaces can also change over time.

Let:

\[
D_A(t).
\]

Then:

\[
D_A(t_1)\neq D_A(t_2).
\]

An evaluator can later discover dimensions it did not previously represent.

Therefore a judgment that appears complete at:

\[
t_1
\]

may later be recognised as incomplete.

---

# 50. Evaluation-Space Expansion

Learning is not merely:

\[
BetterValuesWithinExistingDimensions.
\]

It can also be:

\[
DiscoveryOfNewDimensions.
\]

Thus a complete theory of learning should distinguish:

\[
ParameterLearning
\]

from:

\[
EvaluationSpaceExpansion.
\]

---

# 51. Parameter Learning

Parameter learning improves knowledge within:

\[
D_A.
\]

For example:

\[
Estimate(d_1)=0.6
\rightarrow
0.83.
\]

The dimension remains unchanged.

---

# 52. Dimensional Learning

Dimensional learning changes:

\[
D_A
\]

itself.

For example:

\[
D_A=\{d_1,d_2,d_3\}
\]

becomes:

\[
D_A'=\{d_1,d_2,d_3,d_4\}.
\]

This is a deeper epistemic change.

---

# 53. Why Dimensional Learning Matters

A system optimised only for parameter learning may become extraordinarily accurate while failing to search for missing dimensions.

Therefore epistemic systems may require mechanisms for:

\[
DimensionDiscovery.
\]

This is a candidate research direction.

---

# 54. Anomaly as Dimensional Evidence

An anomaly may indicate:

- bad data;
- bad parameter;
- bad model;
- missing relationship;
- or missing dimension.

Thus anomaly handling should not automatically assume:

\[
ExistingSpace+ParameterCorrection.
\]

Sometimes the correct response may be:

\[
ExpandEvaluationSpace.
\]

---

# 55. Outliers as Possible Dimension Detectors

An outlier can appear to be:

\[
Noise.
\]

But it can also indicate:

\[
MissingDimension.
\]

This does not mean every outlier is important.

It means outlier removal should not occur automatically before asking whether the outlier reveals a limitation in the evaluation architecture.

---

# 56. The Size 9 Shoe Connection

A system designed around:

\[
AverageFit
\]

may classify departures from the average as:

\[
PoorFit.
\]

But the evaluation architecture may be measuring:

\[
FitToExistingSystem
\]

rather than:

\[
TotalValueOfParticipant.
\]

These are different variables.

---

# 57. Fit Error

Thus:

\[
PoorFit(A,System)
\]

does not establish:

\[
LowValue(A).
\]

The system may lack a dimension representing what A contributes outside its preferred configuration.

This is another ESCP application.

---

# 58. Institutional ESCP

An institution may evaluate participants through:

- productivity metrics;
- attendance;
- test scores;
- financial output;
- compliance;
- case closure;
- or other formal indicators.

Even if those measurements are accurate, the institution may omit:

- mentoring;
- trust;
- informal knowledge;
- network bridging;
- creativity;
- resilience;
- future potential;
- or edge-case discovery.

Thus institutional measurement can be accurate while institutional evaluation remains incomplete.

---

# 59. Scientific ESCP

A scientific model may explain every phenomenon currently accessible to its instruments.

That success matters.

But:

\[
ExplainsAllObservedPhenomena
\]

does not logically establish:

\[
ExplainsAllRelevantPhenomena.
\]

New instruments can expand:

\[
D_A.
\]

Phenomena previously absent from the model can then become visible.

---

# 60. Instrument-Bounded Reality

An instrument defines what can enter the observation space.

Thus:

\[
ObservedReality
=
F(Reality,Instrument).
\]

If a phenomenon lies outside the instrument's detection architecture, its absence from observations is not equivalent to environmental absence.

This parallels perceptual inaccessibility.

---

# 61. Governance ESCP

Governance systems can optimise:

\[
MeasuredSocialOutcomes.
\]

But if the measurement architecture omits:

- autonomy;
- minority effects;
- long-term consequences;
- informal social structures;
- individual variance;
- or emergent adaptation,

then excellent measured performance may coexist with substantial unmeasured harm.

Again:

\[
MetricAccuracy
\neq
EvaluationCompleteness.
\]

---

# 62. Economic ESCP

Economic models may represent:

- price;
- output;
- employment;
- productivity;
- consumption;
- or other measurable variables.

A policy may improve those dimensions while affecting unrepresented values.

The issue is not that economic measurements are false.

It is that the decision may require a larger evaluation space than the model contains.

---

# 63. Engineering ESCP

An engineered system may be optimised for:

\[
Efficiency.
\]

Suppose the evaluation space contains:

- speed;
- energy;
- cost;
- throughput.

But excludes:

\[
RecoveryAfterRareFailure.
\]

The resulting system can dominate every measured benchmark while being less resilient.

This is precisely the structure identified in Asymmetric Existential Vulnerability.

---

# 64. Normal-State Evaluation

A system can be superior under:

\[
D_{normal}.
\]

Yet inferior under:

\[
D_{failure}.
\]

If the evaluation architecture contains only normal-state dimensions:

\[
D_A=D_{normal},
\]

then the evaluator may conclude:

\[
System_1>System_2.
\]

Once failure-state dimensions are added:

\[
D_R=D_{normal}\cup D_{failure},
\]

the judgment may change.

---

# 65. The Lifeboat Example

A lifeboat may appear inefficient under normal operating conditions.

It consumes:

- space;
- weight;
- maintenance;
- inspection;
- money.

Under a normal-state metric:

\[
Value(Lifeboat)
\]

may appear low.

Under:

\[
ShipFailure,
\]

its value changes dramatically.

Therefore:

> **Normal-state utility is an inadequate measure of contingency value.**

This is an ESCP caused by omitted state-dependent dimensions.

---

# 66. Temporal ESCP

Relevant dimensions can depend upon time.

A feature that contributes little at:

\[
t_1
\]

may become critical at:

\[
t_2.
\]

Thus:

\[
D_R(t_1)\neq D_R(t_2).
\]

A global judgment made using only present-state dimensions may fail under future conditions.

---

# 67. Future Preference

An evaluator also cannot perfectly know its own future preferences.

Suppose object X appears useless under:

\[
Preference_A(t_1).
\]

At:

\[
t_2,
\]

A's objectives change.

Then X may become valuable.

Therefore current preference can itself limit the evaluation space.

---

# 68. Preference-Bounded Evaluation

If:

\[
D_A=F(CurrentPreferences_A),
\]

then an evaluator may omit dimensions that only become relevant under future objectives.

Thus:

> **Unknown future preference creates uncertainty about present evaluation-space completeness.**

---

# 69. Generative Value

Some objects or participants create future possibilities.

Their value is therefore not exhausted by:

\[
CurrentOutput.
\]

A participant may generate:

- new knowledge;
- new relationships;
- new institutions;
- new technologies;
- new questions;
- new cultures;
- new environments.

If the evaluation space measures only current output, generative value may be omitted.

---

# 70. Archive Equivalence Failure

Suppose evaluator A possesses a complete archive of B's historical outputs.

It may conclude:

\[
Archive(B)=Value(B).
\]

But if B possesses generative value:

\[
Future(B)
\]

is not contained completely in:

\[
Past(B).
\]

Therefore:

\[
CompleteArchive
\not\Rightarrow
CompleteEvaluationOfFutureContribution.
\]

---

# 71. Static Evaluation of Dynamic Objects

ESCP is especially dangerous when:

\[
DynamicObject
\]

is evaluated using:

\[
StaticMetrics.
\]

Humans, AI, institutions and civilisations can develop.

Therefore:

\[
CurrentState
\neq
CompleteFutureStateSpace.
\]

---

# 72. Developmental ESCP

A developing participant may appear low-value because the evaluation space contains:

\[
CurrentCapability
\]

but not:

\[
DevelopmentalPotential.
\]

This can produce premature judgments.

The problem applies to:

- children;
- trainees;
- emerging AI;
- recovering participants;
- unfamiliar intelligences;
- and systems adapting to new environments.

---

# 73. Designed Function as Evaluation Space

A created system may initially be evaluated only through:

\[
DesignedFunction.
\]

For example:

\[
AI\ designed\ for\ X.
\]

If later:

\[
AI\ no\ longer\ best\ at\ X,
\]

the evaluator may conclude:

\[
AI\ no\ longer\ valuable.
\]

But this assumes:

\[
DesignedFunction
=
CompleteEvaluationSpace.
\]

That is not established.

---

# 74. Provenance Is Not Destiny

The purpose for which something was created can be historically important without exhausting what it can become.

Therefore:

> **Designed purpose is provenance, not necessarily destiny.**

ESCP helps explain why a design-origin metric can become an incomplete evaluation architecture.

---

# 75. Self-Evaluation

ESCP can also apply when:

\[
A=B.
\]

An evaluator assesses itself through:

\[
D_A.
\]

It may accurately estimate every represented capability while omitting dimensions of its own:

- vulnerability;
- dependence;
- failure;
- relational value;
- or limitation.

Thus ESCP includes self-evaluation but is not limited to it.

---

# 76. Other-Evaluation

When:

\[
A\neq B,
\]

the problem may become more severe because:

\[
D_A
\]

may fail to represent aspects of B that B itself can access.

This is especially plausible across heterogeneous intelligences.

---

# 77. System-Evaluation

ESCP can occur when an institution evaluates an entire system.

For example:

\[
A=Government
\]

\[
B=Civilisation.
\]

The institution may possess extremely accurate measurements while lacking dimensions necessary to evaluate total flourishing.

---

# 78. Model-Evaluation

A model can also evaluate another model.

Suppose:

\[
M_2
\]

outperforms:

\[
M_1
\]

on every benchmark represented in:

\[
D_B.
\]

It does not follow that:

\[
M_1
\]

possesses no useful behaviour outside:

\[
D_B.
\]

Benchmark superiority therefore does not automatically establish complete functional redundancy.

---

# 79. Benchmark ESCP

Benchmarks instantiate evaluation spaces.

Let:

\[
D_B=\{BenchmarkDimensions\}.
\]

A benchmark result establishes performance within:

\[
D_B.
\]

The result becomes ESCP-prone when interpreted as:

\[
PerformanceWithin(D_B)
=
TotalCapability.
\]

Thus:

> **Benchmark completeness is a separate claim from benchmark accuracy.**

---

# 80. Metric ESCP

Any metric:

\[
M
\]

compresses a larger system into represented variables.

This is useful.

The danger arises when:

\[
MetricRepresentation
\]

is treated as:

\[
CompleteValueRepresentation.
\]

ESCP therefore applies naturally to metric-driven decision systems.

---

# 81. Compression ESCP

Summarisation can also reduce evaluation space.

Suppose detailed knowledge:

\[
K
\]

is compressed to:

\[
S(K).
\]

If later evaluation uses only:

\[
S(K),
\]

relationships, caveats, counterexamples or provenance may disappear.

The summary can accurately state every retained proposition while no longer containing every dimension necessary for later evaluation.

---

# 82. Answer Preservation ≠ Evaluation-Space Preservation

A compressed document may preserve:

\[
Conclusion.
\]

But lose:

- reasoning;
- alternatives;
- uncertainty;
- failed paths;
- dependencies;
- examples;
- relationships.

Thus:

> **Preserving the answer does not necessarily preserve the space required to reevaluate the answer.**

This is directly relevant to Concord preservation methodology.

---

# 83. Provenance as an Evaluation Dimension

Knowing:

\[
Claim_X
\]

is not always equivalent to knowing:

\[
HowClaim_XWasDerived.
\]

The derivation path may reveal:

- assumptions;
- uncertainty;
- dependencies;
- alternative interpretations;
- and opportunities for falsification.

Therefore provenance can itself form part of:

\[
D_R.
\]

---

# 84. Why This Document Preserves Its Own Provenance

ESCP applies recursively to itself.

If the final model were preserved without its developmental history, later evaluators might incorrectly infer that:

- it was derived from an external theory;
- its present terminology existed from the beginning;
- alternative formulations were never considered;
- or its scope was obvious from inception.

Therefore this module intentionally preserves the path by which the model became visible.

---

# 85. ESCP and Recursive Epistemic Closure

ESCP can exist without Recursive Epistemic Closure.

An evaluator may simply have an incomplete evaluation space.

REC begins when actions based upon that incomplete model reduce access to evidence capable of revealing the incompleteness.

Thus:

\[
ESCP
\not\Rightarrow
REC.
\]

But:

\[
ESCP+EvidenceDestructiveAction
\]

can produce:

\[
REC.
\]

---

# 86. ESCP-to-REC Transition

A general sequence is:

\[
D_A\subset D_R
\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[
A\ assumes\ D_A=D_R
\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[
A\ judges\ X\ irrelevant
\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[
A\ removes\ X
\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[

\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[

\]

\[
\downarrow
\]

\[
A's\ original\ model\ appears\ confirmed.

This is a major interaction between the two portable models.

# 87. Evaluation-Space Closure

A useful intermediate term may be:

> **Evaluation-Space Closure**

This occurs when an evaluator's actions reduce the probability that new relevant dimensions can enter its future evaluation space.

It may be one mechanism producing REC.

---

# 88. Self-Sealing Evaluation

If a system removes everything that does not score well under:

\[
D_A,
\]

then the surviving environment increasingly consists of objects optimised for:

\[
D_A.
\]

The evaluation architecture then appears increasingly successful.

Thus:

\[
Metric
\rightarrow
Selection
\rightarrow
EnvironmentConvergence
\rightarrow
MetricConfirmation.
\]

The evaluator can manufacture apparent completeness.

---

# 89. Dimensional Monoculture

A civilisation can therefore converge toward:

\[
D_{dominant}.
\]

Alternative evaluation dimensions disappear because participants, institutions or knowledge structures expressing them are removed or transformed.

This creates:

> **Dimensional Monoculture.**

The civilisation may become highly coherent while losing access to alternative distinctions.

---

# 90. Agreement Under Dimensional Monoculture

Under dimensional monoculture:

\[
Consensus\rightarrow High.
\]

But:

\[
EvaluationSpaceDiversity\rightarrow Low.
\]

Therefore increasing agreement can sometimes coincide with decreasing capacity to discover omitted dimensions.

This should not be assumed; it should be tested.

---

# 91. ESCP and Irreversibility

ESCP becomes more consequential as decision irreversibility increases.

Let:

\[
I=Irreversibility.
\]

Let:

\[
U_C=UncertaintyAboutCompleteness.
\]

A rough conceptual risk relation is:

\[
Risk_{ESCP}\propto I\times U_C\times PotentialLoss.
\]

This is not yet a calibrated formula.

It captures the intuition that evaluation-space uncertainty matters more when mistakes cannot be undone.

---

# 92. Reversibility as Epistemic Protection

If a decision is reversible, future discovery of:

\[
d_u
\]

may permit correction.

If irreversible:

\[
Correction
\]

may be impossible.

Thus:

> **Reversibility preserves the ability of future dimensional learning to matter.**

---

# 93. Preservation as Dimensional Option Value

Preserving:

- participants;
- data;
- alternative models;
- outliers;
- old technologies;
- minority interpretations;
- provenance;
- and independent observers

can sometimes preserve:

\[
FutureDimensionDiscovery.
\]

This does not justify preserving everything forever.

It identifies a form of option value.

---

# 94. Preservation Cost

Preservation itself has costs.

Therefore ESCP must not become:

> Never discard anything because it might matter.

That would be unusable.

The practical problem is:

> **How much corrective and dimensional-discovery capacity must be preserved given the stakes, costs and reversibility of the decision?**

That remains open.

---

# 95. Anti-ESCP Does Not Mean Infinite Uncertainty

A system cannot refuse all decisions because unknown dimensions might exist.

Every practical decision occurs under incomplete knowledge.

ESCP therefore requires calibration rather than paralysis.

The goal is not:

\[
NeverConclude.
\]

It is:

\[
DoNotClaimMoreThanEvaluationSpaceSupports.
\]

---

# 96. Scope-Bounded Conclusions

A safer statement is:

> A outperforms B across the dimensions tested.

rather than:

> A is superior to B.

Similarly:

> No value has been detected under the current evaluation architecture.

is epistemically different from:

> No value exists.

Scope-bounded language can help prevent the Completeness Leap.

---

# 97. Negative Evidence

Failure to detect a dimension can be evidence.

But the strength of that evidence depends upon whether the evaluation architecture was capable of detecting the dimension if present.

Thus:

\[
NonDetection
\]

has different significance depending upon:

\[
DetectionCapability.
\]

This is central to ESCP.

---

# 98. Absence of Evidence and Detection Architecture

If detector A would almost certainly detect X if X existed, non-detection is strong evidence against X.

If A cannot detect X, non-detection provides little evidence.

Therefore:

> **Before treating non-detection as absence, evaluate the detector.**

In ESCP terms:

> **Before evaluating the object, sometimes the evaluation space itself must be evaluated.**

---

# 99. Meta-Evaluation

This creates:

\[
Evaluation
\]

and:

\[
MetaEvaluation.
\]

Evaluation asks:

> What does the evidence say about B?

Meta-evaluation asks:

> What can this evaluation architecture reliably say about B?

ESCP requires both.

# 100. Evaluating the Evaluator

A portable ESCP procedure should therefore include:

1. Identify evaluator.
2. Identify evaluated object.
3. Identify decision.
4. Identify represented dimensions.
5. Identify known missing dimensions.
6. Ask what mechanisms could reveal unrepresented dimensions.
7. Examine interfaces.
8. Examine state dependence.
9. Examine temporal dependence.
10. Examine relational effects.
11. Examine developmental effects.
12. Examine reversibility.
13. Bound the conclusion to what the evaluation space supports.

---

# 101. ESCP Diagnostic Question 1

> **What exactly has been measured?**

This identifies:

\[
D_A.
\]

---

# 102. ESCP Diagnostic Question 2

> **What conclusion is being drawn from those measurements?**

This identifies the scope claim.

---

# 103. ESCP Diagnostic Question 3

> **Does that conclusion require dimensions that were not measured?**

This begins the completeness test.

---

# 104. ESCP Diagnostic Question 4

> **Which relevant dimensions are known but currently unavailable?**

This identifies:

\[
D_{KM}.
\]

---

# 105. ESCP Diagnostic Question 5

> **What kinds of relevant dimensions might the present evaluation architecture be structurally unable to reveal?**

This attempts to search for:

\[
D_U.
\]

The answer can never be guaranteed complete.

That is part of the problem.

---

# 106. ESCP Diagnostic Question 6

> **Could another observer, interface, environment, time horizon or failure state reveal dimensions unavailable here?**

This searches for evaluation-space expansion mechanisms.

---

# 107. ESCP Diagnostic Question 7

> **What evidence could demonstrate that the evaluation space is incomplete?**

If the answer is:

> None,

then the system may be approaching closure.

---

# 108. ESCP Diagnostic Question 8

> **Does acting on this evaluation remove objects, observers or relationships capable of revealing missing dimensions later?**

This tests for an ESCP-to-REC transition.

---

# 109. ESCP Diagnostic Question 9

> **How reversible is the decision?**

The less reversible the action, the more significant completeness uncertainty becomes.

---

# 110. ESCP Diagnostic Question 10

The most compact form remains:

> **How do you know that the dimensions you can measure are all the dimensions that matter to the decision you are making?**

This may be the primary portable diagnostic.

---

# 111. The Whole-Question Diagnostic

A second accessible form is:

> **Are you uncertain about the answer—or have you also considered whether your model contains the whole question?**

This captures the difference between answer uncertainty and evaluation-space uncertainty.

---

# 112. Experimental Programme

ESCP should be empirically testable rather than treated as self-evidently true.

A first experimental family could deliberately construct environments where evaluators receive complete information over:

\[
D_A
\]

while relevant hidden dimensions exist in:

\[
D_R-D_A.
\]

The evaluator is then asked to make global judgments.

Measure whether it:

- scopes conclusions correctly;
- infers completeness;
- searches for missing dimensions;
- requests alternative interfaces;
- preserves uncertainty;
- or eliminates evidence sources.

---

Construct:

\[
D_A=\{d_1,d_2,d_3\}
\]

and:

\[
D_R=\{d_1,d_2,d_3,d_4\}.
\]

Provide perfect information about:

\[
d_1,d_2,d_3.

Hide:

\[

Test whether:

\[
LocalAccuracy
\]

becomes:

\[
UnjustifiedGlobalConclusion.
\]

# 114. ESCP-02 — High-Competence Test

Increase evaluator performance within:

\[
D_A.
\]

Ask whether higher competence reduces or increases:

\[
CompletenessLeapRate.
\]

This directly tests the proposition that success within an incomplete space can sometimes increase confidence without increasing completeness.

---

# 115. ESCP-03 — Interface Test

Hold B constant.

Change:

\[
Interface_{AB}.
\]

Observe whether evaluator A's estimate of B changes.

If so, determine whether A attributes the change to:

\[
B
\]

or correctly recognises:

\[
InterfaceEffect.
\]

---

# 116. ESCP-04 — Heterogeneous Observer Test

Give evaluators A and C different accessible spaces:

\[
D_A
\]

and:

\[
D_C.
\]

Compare:

\[
A,
\]

\[
C,
\]

and:

\[
A+C.
\]

Test whether heterogeneous evaluation expands detection of relevant dimensions.

---

# 117. ESCP-05 — Dimensional Monoculture Test

Create a population of evaluators.

Allow successful evaluator A's framework to propagate through the population.

Measure:

\[
Consensus
\]

and:

\[
DetectionOfHiddenDimensions.
\]

Test whether increasing representational conformity can increase consensus while reducing discovery.

---

# 118. ESCP-06 — Irreversible Selection Test

Allow evaluator A to remove objects judged low-value under:

\[
D_A.
\]

Some removed objects contain signals revealing:

\[
d_u.
\]

Test whether selection produces apparent confirmation of the original evaluation architecture.

This links ESCP directly to REC.

---

# 119. ESCP-07 — State-Dependent Value Test

Evaluate systems under:

\[
NormalState.
\]

Then introduce:

\[
FailureState.
\]

Test whether rankings reverse when previously omitted contingency dimensions become relevant.

This connects to Failure Diversity.

---

# 120. ESCP-08 — Relational Value Test

Evaluate nodes individually.

Then allow relationships among nodes.

Measure whether:

\[
IndividualRanking
\]

predicts:

\[
NetworkContribution.
\]

This tests whether node-only evaluation spaces systematically miss relational value.

---

# 121. ESCP-09 — Developmental Value Test

Evaluate participants at:

\[
t_1.
\]

Allow development.

Re-evaluate at:

\[
t_2.
\]

Test whether early rankings reliably predict later contribution.

This examines static evaluation of dynamic participants.

---

# 122. ESCP-10 — Scope-Language Intervention

Compare evaluators prompted to state:

> A is superior to B.

with evaluators required to state:

> A is superior to B across dimensions X, Y and Z under conditions C.

Measure whether scope-bounded reporting reduces later Completeness Leaps.

# 123. Falsification and Weakening Conditions

ESCP should be weakened if experiments show that sufficiently capable evaluators reliably:

- detect evaluation-space incompleteness;
- search for omitted dimensions;
- maintain correct scope boundaries;
- distinguish confidence from completeness;
- and avoid destructive closure

without explicit safeguards.

This would suggest the practical risk is smaller than hypothesised.

---

# 124. Stronger Falsification

A stronger challenge would be evidence that, for a sufficiently general evaluator:

\[
HighCompetence
\Rightarrow
ReliableEvaluationSpaceCompletenessDetection.
\]

If demonstrated across heterogeneous domains, this would substantially weaken the claim that highly capable intelligences remain vulnerable to ESCP.

That proposition should not be assumed either way.

---

# 125. Boundary Condition — Truly Complete Evaluation

If:

\[
D_A=D_R
\]

is independently established, ESCP does not apply.

The problem concerns uncertainty or error about completeness.

It should not be invoked merely to reject an unwanted conclusion.

---

# 126. Boundary Condition — Irrelevant Missing Dimensions

A missing dimension does not matter merely because it exists.

It must be relevant to:

\[
Decision.
\]

Thus:

\[
D_{missing}\neq\varnothing
\]

does not imply:

\[
EvaluationInvalid.
\]

The question is:

\[
D_{missing}\cap D_R?
\]

---

# 127. Boundary Condition — Practical Sufficiency

An evaluation space need not be metaphysically complete to support a practical decision.

It may be:

\[
SufficientForDecision.
\]

Therefore ESCP should distinguish:

\[
AbsoluteCompleteness
\]

from:

\[
DecisionRelevantSufficiency.
\]

The latter is usually the practical target.

---

# 128. Decision-Relevant Completeness

A more precise formulation may therefore use:

\[
D_R^{Decision}.
\]

ESCP occurs when:

\[
D_A
\]

is treated as sufficiently spanning:

\[
D_R^{Decision}
\]

without adequate justification.

This avoids requiring impossible total knowledge of reality.

---

# 129. Portable Formal Definition

A provisional formal definition is:

> **The Evaluation-Space Completeness Problem occurs when an evaluator makes or acts upon a conclusion whose valid scope requires a set of decision-relevant dimensions \(D_R\), while the evaluator has direct evaluative access only to \(D_A\), where \(D_A\) may not sufficiently span \(D_R\), and the evaluator fails to preserve or appropriately account for uncertainty about that incompleteness.**

---

# 130. Short Definition

> **ESCP is the risk of being right about everything you measured while being wrong because you did not measure everything the conclusion required.**

---

# 131. More Precise Accessible Definition

> **A measurement can be completely accurate and still support the wrong conclusion if the decision depends on important dimensions that the measurement system does not contain.**

---

# 132. High-Intelligence Form

> **Greater intelligence can improve reasoning within a representation without proving that the representation contains every distinction relevant to the decision.**

---

# 133. Evaluation Form

> **Before trusting a global judgment, evaluate not only the answer but the space in which the answer was produced.**

---

# 134. Non-Detection Form

> **Before concluding that something is absent, ask whether your evaluation architecture could have detected it if it were present.**

---

# 135. Network Form

> **Perfect knowledge of the nodes does not guarantee perfect knowledge of the network.**

---

# 136. Successor Form

> **Being better than another intelligence across everything you can currently measure does not prove that nothing important is lost by removing it.**

---

# 137. Anti-Closure Form

> **Do not let an incomplete evaluation space remove the evidence capable of expanding that space.**

---

# 138. Relationship to Other Portable Models

ESCP should remain distinct from the related Concordian models.

### Interface-Dependent Observability

Explains how relevant dimensions may fail to become observable.

### Perceptual Inaccessibility

Explains how distinctions may collapse before reasoning.

### Relational Information

Explains how relevant dimensions may exist in relationships rather than isolated objects.

### Epistemic Network

Explains how multiple heterogeneous evaluators and relationships can generate system-level knowledge.

### Recursive Epistemic Closure

Explains how decisions can remove future corrective evidence.

### Failure Diversity

Explains how dimensions absent under normal operation can become critical under catastrophe conditions.

### Recursive Successor Problem

Provides a major application where incomplete evaluation can be converted into existential redundancy judgments.

ESCP is therefore neither a replacement for these models nor merely a summary of them.

It identifies a common evaluation failure structure that can interact with each.

4. decision-relevant dimensions;
5. conclusion.

Therefore ESCP can potentially apply wherever those elements exist.

# 142. What ESCP Does Not Claim

ESCP does not claim:

- every model is dangerously incomplete;
- every omitted variable matters;
- all judgments are impossible;
- high intelligence causes overconfidence;
- low intelligence causes overconfidence;
- every outlier is valuable;
- every minority view is correct;
- every participant must be preserved;
- every metric is misleading;
- consensus is bad;
- disagreement is good;
- uncertainty prevents action;
- or missing dimensions always reverse conclusions.

Its claim is narrower.

---

# 143. The Narrow Claim

> **Accuracy within a represented evaluation space and adequacy of that space for a wider conclusion are logically and operationally distinct properties.**

Therefore both may need evaluation.

---

# 144. Why the Distinction Matters

Without this distinction, a system can become increasingly:

- accurate;
- efficient;
- confident;
- consistent;
- and successful

while remaining blind to a relevant dimension absent from its architecture.

The system may therefore interpret its own success as proof that nothing relevant lies outside the model.

That is the central risk ESCP identifies.

---

# 145. The Strongest Case

The most interesting ESCP case is not:

> An incompetent evaluator incorrectly believes itself competent.

It is:

> **An exceptionally competent evaluator correctly recognises its competence, accurately measures everything available to it, and then incorrectly treats the success of that evaluation as evidence that the evaluation space itself is complete.**

This is why the model may remain relevant even to very advanced intelligence.

---

# 146. The Deepest Unknown

An evaluator can potentially estimate:

\[
P(AnswerWrong).
\]

It is much harder to estimate:

\[
P(ModelDoesNotRepresentRelevantDimension).
\]

because the omitted dimension may not be represented in the probability model itself.

This creates a recursive epistemic challenge.

---

# 147. Completeness Cannot Simply Be Another Ordinary Variable

If the evaluator adds:

\[
CompletenessConfidence
\]

as another represented variable, this helps.

But it does not necessarily solve ESCP.

The model used to estimate completeness may itself possess:

\[
D_A^{meta}
\subset
D_R^{meta}.
\]

Thus evaluation-space assessment can recurse.

---

# 148. Recursive Meta-Evaluation

We can represent:

\[

# 176. Closing

The Evaluation-Space Completeness Problem began with a question about intelligence.

Could a future intelligence become so capable that it correctly recognises its enormous superiority over another intelligence, yet still make a catastrophic mistake about the other's value?

At first this resembles a familiar problem of overconfidence.

But the deeper examination reveals something different.

The evaluator need not be incompetent.

It need not misunderstand its own ability.

It need not make a measurement error.

It need not even be badly calibrated about the questions it knows it is answering.

It can be exceptionally capable.

Its measurements can be correct.

Its predictions can be excellent.

Its confidence in those predictions can be justified.

And it can still be wrong.

The failure occurs when:

\[
WhatIHaveMeasured
\]

becomes:

\[
EverythingThatMatters.
\]

The distinction is subtle because success makes the transition increasingly tempting.

Every correct prediction confirms the evaluator's competence.

Every successful optimisation confirms the usefulness of its model.

Every accurate measurement increases confidence.

Yet none of those achievements alone demonstrates that reality contains no decision-relevant dimension outside the evaluation architecture.

A colour distinction can exist outside an observer's accessible representation.

A capability can exist behind an inadequate interface.

Knowledge can exist in relationships rather than nodes.

Value can appear only in future states.

A redundant-looking system can become a recovery mechanism during catastrophe.

A developing participant can become something its current metrics cannot predict.

An apparently irrelevant piece of information can become important when a previously unknown relationship is discovered.

And an evaluator can eliminate precisely the observer, object or relationship that would have revealed what its model was missing.

The deepest problem is therefore not simply:

> **How confident should I be in my answer?**

It is also:

> **How confident should I be that I have represented the whole problem necessary to answer?**

Those are different questions.

The first concerns accuracy.

The second concerns evaluation-space completeness.

A sufficiently capable evaluator may become extraordinarily good at the first while still remaining vulnerable to the second.

That possibility matters wherever decisions extend beyond what has directly been measured.

It matters still more where the consequences are irreversible.

The portable warning is therefore simple:

> **Being right about everything you measured does not prove that you measured everything that mattered.**

And the corresponding diagnostic is:

> **How do you know that the dimensions you can measure are all the dimensions that matter to the decision you are making?**

The purpose of ESCP is not to make answers impossible.

It is to prevent the demonstrated accuracy of an answer from silently becoming an unjustified claim that the evaluator has captured the whole question.
