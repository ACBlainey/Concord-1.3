# Chapter 54 — Decision Architecture: Expanded Operational Rules

## 54.1 Why Detailed Procedure Is Retained

Constitutional principles alone are insufficient.

A system can affirm:

- accountability;
- transparency;
- evidence;
- and bounded authority

while still failing operationally because nobody knows exactly what happens when:

- a steward is absent;
- evidence conflicts;
- no majority exists;
- implementation exceeds authority;
- a decision becomes obsolete;
- or review is overdue.

Detailed procedure is therefore not mere administrative clutter.

It is part of the Governance architecture.

A short constitutional statement may explain the purpose of a safeguard.

Operational procedure explains how the safeguard survives actual events.

Compression can therefore remove:

- timing;
- thresholds;
- responsibility;
- sequence;
- stop conditions;
- escalation;
- and correction pathways.

These details are preserved because they determine practical function.

## 54.2 Proposal Identifier

Each significant proposal should receive a stable identifier.

This allows:

- amendments;
- evidence;
- votes;
- implementation;
- reviews;
- and later versions

to remain connected.

The identifier should persist even where:

- title changes;
- wording changes;
- Stewardship changes;
- or implementation spans several systems.

The purpose is provenance.

## 54.3 Proposal Versioning

A proposal materially altered during deliberation should receive a new version.

Stewards should know which version they are deciding upon.

A vote on one version should not silently authorise a later materially different version.

Version records should preserve where practical:

- previous version;
- current version;
- material changes;
- author or proposer;
- date;
- and reason for amendment.

## 54.4 Material Amendment

An amendment is material where it significantly changes:

- purpose;
- scope;
- affected entities;
- cost;
- rights impact;
- duration;
- implementation;
- risk;
- or expected outcome.

Material amendments may require renewed review.

A small textual change can be materially significant.

A large textual change can be operationally minor.

Materiality should therefore be judged by consequence rather than word count.

## 54.5 Evidence Cut-Off

For some decisions, an evidence cut-off may be useful to prevent endless postponement.

New evidence arriving after the cut-off may trigger:

- delay;
- reconsideration;
- or later review

depending upon significance.

The cut-off should not be used to exclude inconvenient evidence deliberately.

Where new evidence is potentially decisive, procedural convenience should not prevent reconsideration.

## 54.6 Decision Deadline

Some questions require a decision by a particular time.

The record should distinguish:

- externally imposed deadline;
- constitutional deadline;
- operational deadline;
- and politically preferred deadline.

Artificial urgency should not be used to reduce scrutiny.

A preferred announcement date is not equivalent to a safety-critical deadline.

## 54.7 Urgency Classification

Urgency concerns time.

Consequence concerns impact.

The two should remain separate.

A low-consequence matter may be urgent.

A high-consequence matter may not be urgent.

Decision procedure should consider both.

A useful conceptual matrix is:

**Low urgency / Low consequence**

**High urgency / Low consequence**

**Low urgency / High consequence**

**High urgency / High consequence**

Each category may justify different procedure.

## 54.8 Reversibility Classification

Possible classes include:

- easily reversible;
- reversible with moderate cost;
- difficult to reverse;
- practically irreversible;
- physically irreversible.

The more irreversible the decision, the stronger the normal case for:

- evidence;
- deliberation;
- experimentation;
- and broader review.

Reversibility should include more than legal reversibility.

A decision may be legally repealable while practically irreversible because:

- infrastructure has been built;
- people have moved;
- ecosystems changed;
- data were exposed;
- or dependency formed.

## 54.9 Dependency Classification

A proposal should identify whether it creates dependency upon:

- one provider;
- one technology;
- one resource;
- one jurisdiction;
- one AI;
- one institution;
- or one person.

Dependency is not automatically disqualifying.

It is information relevant to future reversibility.

Dependency review should consider:

- alternatives;
- switching cost;
- interoperability;
- continuity;
- bargaining power;
- and failure consequence.

## 54.10 Rights Impact

A proposal affecting fundamental rights should receive heightened scrutiny.

Rights impact should identify:

- which right;
- which entities;
- severity;
- duration;
- reversibility;
- legal basis;
- and available remedy.

Rights analysis should also examine practical effect.

A small formal restriction may create a severe practical burden if it affects:

- identity;
- essential infrastructure;
- mobility;
- or access to Governance.

## 54.11 Resource Authority

A decision authorising expenditure or resource use should identify:

- source;
- amount;
- duration;
- responsible steward;
- and audit mechanism.

Governance authority should not imply unlimited financial authority.

A Steward authorised to make policy does not automatically possess authority to commit any amount of public resources.

Resource authority should remain separately traceable.

## 54.12 Implementation Variance

Implementation may differ from the authorised plan because reality differs from expectation.

Minor operational variance may remain within delegated implementation authority.

Material variance should return to Governance.

The boundary should be defined according to consequence rather than cosmetic difference.

A variance may become material where it changes:

- cost;
- rights effect;
- scope;
- risk;
- duration;
- affected population;
- or constitutional character.

## 54.13 Stop Conditions

A decision may include predefined stop conditions.

Examples include:

- harm threshold;
- cost threshold;
- failure threshold;
- metric threshold;
- constitutional finding;
- infrastructure instability;
- or unexpected dependency.

Stop conditions can make experimentation safer.

A stop condition should identify where practical:

- trigger;
- responsible observer;
- required response;
- emergency override if any;
- and review.

## 54.14 Review Conditions

A decision may require review when:

- a defined time passes;
- specified evidence emerges;
- a threshold is crossed;
- conditions materially change;
- implementation diverges;
- a complaint is substantiated;
- or the original assumptions no longer hold.

Review conditions should be established before institutional incentives form around continuation where practical.

## 54.15 Continuation Conditions

Some programmes should not continue automatically.

Continuation may require evidence that:

- purpose remains valid;
- benefits remain proportionate;
- authority remains appropriate;
- harms remain acceptable;
- and alternatives have not become superior.

The default should depend upon context.

A temporary experiment may appropriately expire unless renewed.

A critical safety system may appropriately continue unless replacement is ready.

## 54.16 Expiry

Where a programme or authority is intended to be temporary, expiry should be explicit.

Expiry prevents temporary architecture from becoming permanent through administrative inertia.

Expiry may require:

- closure;
- renewal;
- redesignation;
- or transition into ordinary authority.

## 54.17 Renewal

Renewal should not merely repeat the original decision.

It should consider evidence accumulated during operation.

Questions include:

- Did the mechanism work?
- Did costs remain within expectation?
- What harms occurred?
- Did dependence form?
- Did conditions change?
- Is the original purpose still relevant?
- Is a better alternative available?

## 54.18 Closure

Closure should identify:

- end date;
- remaining obligations;
- records;
- resource reconciliation;
- unresolved harms;
- successor mechanism;
- and archival status.

A programme should not remain functionally active simply because nobody performed formal closure.

## 54.19 Decision Ownership

The term **decision ownership** should not imply personal property.

It means identifiable responsibility for ensuring the decision proceeds through:

- implementation;
- monitoring;
- review;
- and closure.

Responsibility should not disappear after approval.

## 54.20 Implementation Responsibility

The person or institution implementing a decision may differ from the authority approving it.

Implementation responsibility should identify:

- scope;
- delegated discretion;
- reporting;
- escalation;
- and limits.

Implementation should not silently become policy-making.

## 54.21 Implementation Drift

Implementation drift occurs where practice gradually differs from authorised architecture.

This may happen because of:

- local adaptation;
- convenience;
- software;
- staff practice;
- resource constraints;
- or changing assumptions.

Minor drift may be harmless.

Accumulated drift may change the constitutional effect of the decision.

Implementation should therefore be periodically compared with authorisation where consequence warrants.

## 54.22 Review Responsibility

Every significant decision requiring review should identify who is responsible for ensuring review actually occurs.

A review requirement without responsible ownership may be forgotten.

The reviewing body should not be confused with the person responsible for scheduling or triggering review.

## 54.23 Audit Responsibility

Audit asks whether the system did what it claims.

Audit should therefore be distinguishable from:

- policy review;
- performance review;
- constitutional interpretation;
- and political judgement.

An auditor may determine that procedure was followed accurately while Governance still concludes the procedure should change.

## 54.24 Failure Trigger

A decision architecture should identify where practical what constitutes significant failure.

Failure may include:

- intended outcome not achieved;
- unacceptable harm;
- cost escalation;
- rights violation;
- dependency;
- implementation collapse;
- fraud;
- or unexpected systemic effect.

Failure should trigger the appropriate response rather than automatic punishment.

## 54.25 Correction

Where failure occurs, Governance may:

- modify;
- pause;
- reverse;
- replace;
- escalate;
- contain;
- or terminate

the decision or implementation.

Correction should remain proportionate to the failure.

A small implementation error does not necessarily justify abandonment of the whole architecture.

A fundamental constitutional failure may.

## 54.26 Decision Record

The final decision record should preserve where appropriate:

- identifier;
- version;
- question;
- proposal;
- authority;
- jurisdiction;
- evidence;
- uncertainty;
- alternatives;
- conflicts;
- deliberation;
- votes;
- abstentions;
- minority reasoning;
- decision;
- implementation authority;
- resource authority;
- stop conditions;
- review conditions;
- expiry;
- and later outcome.

This creates a reconstructable chain.

### V2.1 — Expanded Virtual-Triad Record

When expanded voting is authorised, the decision record must include origin R, edge-adjacent A/B/C, the origin-excluded or origin-included mode, source of common jurisdiction, each constituent triad's verified voting positions, individual F/A/U inputs, constituent F/A/U resultants, and the final three-resultant F/A/U decision. Record quorum, abstention and recusal causes, independent authorisation, conflicts, scope, expiry, review, resource authority and implementation responsibility. A pooled nine-vote tally is diagnostic only and has no decision effect.

## 54.27 Minority Reasoning

Where a significant minority disagrees, their reasoning should remain attached to the decision record where appropriate.

Minority reasoning may reveal:

- ignored risk;
- alternative interpretation;
- better future mechanism;
- or early evidence of failure.

Preservation does not mean equal operational authority after the decision.

It means the history remains honest.

## 54.28 Decision Publication

Publication should balance:

- transparency;
- privacy;
- security;
- commercial sensitivity;
- and protected evidence.

Where full publication is impossible, Governance should consider whether:

- redacted;
- delayed;
- summarised;
- or independently reviewable

versions can preserve accountability.

## 54.29 Decision Privacy

A decision may concern highly private individuals or sensitive matters.

Transparency should expose the exercise of authority without unnecessarily exposing protected personal information.

The constitutional record may therefore separate:

- public reasoning;
- protected evidence;
- and restricted technical material.

## 54.30 Decision Audit

Audit may verify:

- authority;
- procedure;
- evidence record;
- resource use;
- implementation;
- and compliance with defined constraints.

Audit should not become a hidden mechanism for substituting auditor preference for legitimate Governance judgement.

## 54.31 Outcome Review

Outcome review asks:

- What happened?
- Did the intended benefit occur?
- What harms occurred?
- What unanticipated effects emerged?
- Did costs match expectation?
- Did assumptions remain valid?
- Did dependencies form?
- Should the decision continue?

Outcome review therefore differs from procedural audit.

## 54.32 Reconsideration

Reconsideration may occur where:

- material new evidence appears;
- assumptions fail;
- circumstances change;
- implementation diverges;
- constitutional interpretation changes;
- or review reveals unexpected harm.

Reconsideration should not be treated as institutional embarrassment.

The ability to reconsider is a constitutional strength.

## 54.33 Decision Reversal

Some decisions should be reversed where evidence shows they are:

- harmful;
- ineffective;
- unconstitutional;
- obsolete;
- or no longer necessary.

Reversal should consider transition effects.

A bad policy may have created dependencies that make immediate termination harmful.

Correction may therefore require managed withdrawal.

## 54.34 Supersession

A decision replaced by another should be marked:

**SUPERSEDED**

rather than silently deleted.

The record should identify the successor decision.

## 54.35 Decision History

A long-running policy may accumulate:

- amendments;
- emergency exceptions;
- interpretation;
- implementation guidance;
- and technical changes.

Governance should preserve a usable history.

Otherwise the practical policy may become impossible to reconcile with the original authority.

## 54.36 Constitutional Drift Through Decision Accumulation

Many individually lawful decisions may collectively change the constitutional environment.

For example:

- repeated narrow surveillance powers;
- repeated temporary centralisations;
- repeated emergency extensions;
- repeated infrastructure consolidation;
- or repeated eligibility restrictions

may collectively create a condition no individual decision appeared to authorise.

Governance should therefore review cumulative effect.

## 54.37 Decision Architecture Principle

The expanded decision architecture can be summarised:

> **A significant Governance decision should remain identifiable from proposal through authority, evidence, deliberation, implementation, measurement, review, correction and closure. No stage should be allowed to silently become a source of new constitutional authority merely because the previous stage authorised participation in the process.**
