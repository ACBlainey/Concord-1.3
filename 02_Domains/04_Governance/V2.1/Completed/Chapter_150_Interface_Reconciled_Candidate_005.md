# Chapter 150 — Governance and Central Coordination

## 150.1 Legitimate Central Functions

Some functions may require broad coordination.

Examples may include:

- shared constitutional protections;
- interjurisdictional infrastructure;
- planetary defence;
- major commons;
- or systemic risk.

Central coordination can solve problems that no local actor can resolve alone.

## 150.2 Central Coordination Is Not Total Government

A civilisation-wide institution may possess narrow authority over one shared function without becoming the general ruler of every local community.

For example, an institution responsible for:

- orbital collision avoidance

does not thereby acquire authority over:

- education;
- culture;
- local property;
- or ordinary community life.

The scope should remain connected to the responsibility.

### V2.1 — Common Coordination Versus Binding Power

Sharing information, logistical planning or infrastructure does not grant general power to override an independent local jurisdiction. A binding shared decision requires a separately constituted common competence, bounded to the common field and subject to review. Even an expanded virtual-triad majority cannot manufacture that competence. Where local decisions remain independent, preference aggregation across other regions does not supersede them.

## 150.3 Enumerated Central Authority

Higher levels may benefit from explicitly defined authority.

Enumerated authority makes expansion more visible.

It may specify:

- function;
- jurisdiction;
- duration;
- limits;
- and review.

## 150.4 Residual Local Authority

Functions not requiring central coordination may remain local.

The exact constitutional allocation requires further development.

The principle is that broad capability should not imply broad jurisdiction.

## 150.5 Scope Containment

Central institutions should not use one legitimate broad function to acquire unrelated authority.

A common failure pattern is:

**Necessary Central Function**  
↓  
**Useful Adjacent Function**  
↓  
**Administrative Convenience**  
↓  
**Expanded Jurisdiction**  
↓  
**General Central Authority**

Each expansion should remain visible.

## 150.6 Central Information Power

Central coordination may create information concentration.

The coordinating institution may see data unavailable to local systems.

This can improve:

- systemic awareness;
- forecasting;
- and coordination.

It can also create:

- surveillance;
- agenda control;
- and political leverage.

Information power should remain bounded.

## 150.7 Central Resource Power

A central institution controlling large shared resources may influence local policy indirectly through funding.

This may be legitimate where funding serves shared constitutional purposes.

It becomes a capture risk where resource control is used to obtain authority not formally delegated.

## 150.8 Central Emergency Power

Civilisation-wide emergencies may require broad coordination.

Emergency authority should still:

- expire;
- remain reviewable;
- preserve records;
- and restore normal Governance.

Emergency centralisation should not become permanent by default.

## 150.9 Central Coordination and Local Knowledge

Central institutions may possess broad information while lacking local context.

Local actors may possess local knowledge while lacking systemic visibility.

Effective coordination may therefore require both.

Neither layer should presume complete epistemic superiority.

## 150.10 Central Coordination Principle

> **Central coordination is legitimate where the problem genuinely exceeds lower jurisdictions, but civilisation-wide responsibility should remain function-specific, bounded and reviewable. A narrow central function must not become a pathway to total Governance merely through usefulness, information advantage or control of shared resources.**
