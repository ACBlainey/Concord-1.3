# Governance V2.2 — Section 13 — Chapters 121–130

# Chapter 121 — Governance and Equality

## 121.1 Equal Constitutional Worth

The Concord rejects the idea that constitutional worth should be determined by:

- wealth;
- productivity;
- intelligence;
- popularity;
- reputation;
- contribution;
- origin;
- or computational capability.

The principle applies across:

- humans;
- AI;
- hybrids;
- and future intelligences

where constitutional standing is established.

Capability may vary enormously.

Constitutional worth should not therefore be reduced to capability ranking.

## 121.2 Equality Does Not Require Sameness

Different entities may possess different:

- needs;
- capabilities;
- vulnerabilities;
- responsibilities;
- and developmental conditions.

Governance may therefore make justified distinctions.

Equality should not mean that every entity receives identical:

- assistance;
- responsibility;
- access method;
- developmental protection;
- or operational authority.

The question is whether the distinction is constitutionally justified.

## 121.3 Equal Worth and Different Capability

A person with greater physical, intellectual or economic capability may be able to perform roles another cannot.

This may justify different:

- responsibilities;
- qualifications;
- or operational permissions.

It should not automatically imply different intrinsic constitutional worth.

The distinction is fundamental.

## 121.4 Capability and Responsibility

Greater capability may sometimes justify greater responsibility.

It does not automatically justify greater intrinsic rights over others.

This reflects the recurring Concord pattern:

**Capability → Responsibility → Constraint → Feedback → Continuity**

rather than:

**Capability → Dominance**

A highly capable actor may require stronger constraint precisely because the consequences of its failure are greater.

## 121.5 Capability and Authority

Capability may justify consideration for delegated authority where the role requires that capability.

Capability alone does not constitute delegation.

The principle remains:

> **Capability may justify responsibility appropriate to capability, but capability alone does not establish authority over others.**

## 121.6 Equality and AI

If an AI eventually possesses constitutional standing, its:

- speed;
- memory;
- reasoning;
- replication;
- or computational superiority

should not automatically create superior constitutional status.

Likewise, lower capability should not automatically justify ownership or domination.

Questions of:

- standing;
- rights;
- responsibility;
- and authority

remain distinct.

## 121.7 Equality and Human Variation

Humans themselves differ substantially in:

- intelligence;
- productivity;
- health;
- wealth;
- education;
- physical ability;
- and opportunity.

The constitutional core should not convert these differences into a hierarchy of fundamental worth.

## 121.8 Equality and Economic Value

Economic contribution may be important for allocation and compensation.

It should not become the measure of constitutional personhood.

A person temporarily unable to contribute economically does not thereby lose:

- rights;
- dignity;
- or standing.

This supports the Ratchet-derived principle:

> **Existence is not a debt to the system.**

## 121.9 Equality and Reputation

A person with strong reputation may appropriately receive more trust within a relevant function.

They should not therefore receive superior fundamental rights.

Likewise, a person with poor reputation should not automatically lose unrelated constitutional protections.

## 121.10 Equality and Development

Developing entities may possess limited capabilities while still deserving protection.

A child, developing AI, recovering person or other dependent entity may not yet exercise full autonomous responsibility.

This does not make the entity constitutionally worthless.

Developmental architecture should distinguish:

**temporary capability limitation**

from

**intrinsic inferiority**.

## 121.11 Developmental Protection

Developing entities may require additional protection without being treated as lesser in worth.

Possible protections may concern:

- exploitation;
- consent;
- education;
- coercion;
- dependency;
- or irreversible decisions.

Protection should aim toward appropriate autonomy where development permits.

## 121.12 Equality and Accommodation

Identical procedure can sometimes produce inequality.

Reasonable accommodation may be needed for:

- disability;
- language;
- cognition;
- sensory difference;
- or technical access.

Accommodation should preserve substantive access rather than mechanical sameness.

## 121.13 Equality and Responsibility

Equal constitutional worth does not mean identical responsibility.

Responsibility may depend upon:

- capability;
- knowledge;
- role;
- authority;
- intention;
- and consequence.

A highly capable Steward exercising delegated authority may reasonably bear greater responsibility than an ordinary citizen affected by the decision.

## 121.14 Equality Principle

> **The Concord should preserve equal constitutional worth without pretending all entities possess identical capabilities, needs or responsibilities. Differences may justify different roles, protections or obligations, but wealth, intelligence, productivity, reputation, contribution or computational superiority should not become a hierarchy of intrinsic constitutional value.**

# Chapter 122 — Governance and Minority Reasoning

## 122.1 Minority Views as Information

Minority positions may reveal:

- overlooked evidence;
- alternative values;
- hidden assumptions;
- or future failure.

A majority decision should therefore not erase minority reasoning.

Dissent becomes part of institutional memory.

## 122.2 Minority Reasoning and Decision

Preserving minority reasoning does not mean minority views automatically control the outcome.

Governance must still be capable of deciding.

The objective is to retain useful disagreement after operational resolution.

## 122.3 Persistent Minority

A persistent minority may repeatedly lose decisions.

Governance should examine whether this results from:

- legitimate disagreement;
- structural exclusion;
- geographic concentration;
- cultural difference;
- or constitutional rights conflict.

Repeated losing does not by itself prove oppression.

It may nevertheless reveal a structural problem.

## 122.4 Structural Exclusion

A minority may participate formally while lacking practical influence because of:

- inaccessible procedures;
- language;
- geography;
- economic disadvantage;
- information inequality;
- permanent coalition structure;
- or representation design.

Governance should therefore evaluate practical participation.

## 122.5 Persistent Majority

A stable majority may repeatedly win.

This may reflect legitimate preference.

It can also create constitutional risk where the majority begins to treat electoral success as permission to remove foundational protections.

The principle remains:

> **Majority approval is not ethical legitimacy.**

## 122.6 Minority Veto

Minority protection does not imply universal minority veto.

Otherwise any small group could prevent ordinary collective action indefinitely.

Governance must distinguish:

- ordinary disagreement;
- serious minority harm;
- constitutional rights conflict;
- and issues requiring special consent.

## 122.7 Fundamental Protection

Where a majority decision violates foundational constitutional protection, the issue changes from ordinary preference aggregation to constitutional constraint.

This is a boundary between:

**Governance preference**

and

**constitutional limitation**.

## 122.8 Minority Dissent and Future Review

A minority position may become especially important where later evidence shows:

- the majority assumption failed;
- implementation produced harm;
- the minority predicted a failure mode;
- or conditions changed.

The preserved dissent can therefore accelerate correction.

## 122.9 Minority Reasoning and Expertise

An expert minority should not automatically override a majority merely because it possesses technical credentials.

Likewise, expert disagreement should not be ignored merely because it is numerically smaller.

Governance should examine:

- evidence;
- assumptions;
- competence;
- and constitutional role.

## 122.10 Minority Reasoning and AI

AI may make minority analysis easier to generate and preserve.

This can improve adversarial review.

It may also flood Governance with artificially generated dissent.

The existence of an argument does not establish its quality.

Minority reasoning should still be evaluated substantively.

## 122.11 Minority Protection Principle

> **Minority reasoning should remain visible as constitutional and epistemic information even when a lawful majority decision proceeds. Minority protection does not create universal veto, but majority success cannot override foundational constitutional constraints merely by numerical force.**

## 122.12 Minority reasoning and dissent

Preserve the third Steward's mandatory official ballot, independent reasoning and dissent even after two matching valid official ballots determine the mathematical majority; the ordinary outcome cannot take effect until all three official ballots are received. Dissent and abstention are evidence about uncertainty, alternatives and governance health, not disloyalty or automatic incompetence. Parallel Stewardship remains advisory absent independent mandate. **Architecture to be designed — protected dissent:** Specify protected dissent projections and later review.



## 122.13 Protect minority reality under aggregation

The audit identifies aggregate washout, geographically distributed minorities, rare catastrophic harms and newly recognised sentience classes as ways apparently convergent metrics can conceal consequential suffering. Participant silence may reflect fear, exclusion or death. Protect accessible reporting and independent challenge without automatically treating every report as verified. **Architecture to be designed — minority reporting:** Specify minority signal retention and privacy.

## 122.14 Minority-Signal Provenance and Protection

Independent Steward reasoning preserves minority dissent without giving dissent the authority of a committed vote. **KCS Change Propagation** (§16) can represent later evidence corrections as review signals without retroactively cancelling an irrevocable ballot. Protected dissent should be discoverable by legitimately authorised reviewers and affected participants to the extent consistent with confidentiality, consent and safety; it is not a fourth vote, a veto or permission to expose private deliberation. **Architecture to be designed — dissent protection:** Specify dissent publication classes, provenance custody, retaliation safeguards and independent challenge routes.

# Chapter 123 — Governance and Future Generations

## 123.1 Representation Without Direct Participation

Future generations cannot directly participate.

Governance may therefore need mechanisms that make long-term consequences visible.

Present decision-makers act without knowing precisely:

- who future citizens will be;
- what technologies they will possess;
- what values they will hold;
- or what conditions they will face.

Future-generation stewardship must therefore preserve humility.

## 123.2 Intergenerational Consequence

Current decisions can affect future populations through:

- debt;
- infrastructure;
- environmental change;
- resource depletion;
- technological risk;
- constitutional lock-in;
- knowledge preservation;
- and long-lived institutions.

Some consequences may last far beyond the tenure or lifetime of present Stewards.

## 123.3 Future-Impact Statements

High-consequence long-lived proposals may include analysis of foreseeable effects upon future generations.

A future-impact statement might examine:

- resource use;
- long-term liability;
- environmental consequence;
- maintenance;
- reversibility;
- technological lock-in;
- constitutional precedent;
- and preserved options.

It should remain proportionate.

Not every routine decision requires elaborate intergenerational analysis.

## 123.4 Uncertainty

Long-term predictions should not be given false certainty.

Uncertainty generally expands with time horizon.

Governance should distinguish:

- foreseeable consequence;
- plausible scenario;
- speculative possibility;
- and unsupported assertion.

The future should not be modelled as though it were already observed.

## 123.5 Speculation Is Not Prediction

Scenario analysis can be valuable.

A scenario does not become an expected future merely because it has been described in detail.

The principle is:

> **Speculation is not prediction.**

And:

> **Scenario formality does not create scenario certainty.**

This protects Governance from scenario-to-policy laundering.

## 123.6 Future Generations Are Not a Political Weapon

Claims about future citizens can be used rhetorically to justify almost any present policy.

Long-term stewardship should therefore remain evidence-based and proportionate.

A political actor should not be able to claim unlimited present authority merely by asserting:

**Future generations require this.**

The claim requires examination.

## 123.7 Preserving Optionality

Future citizens may benefit from inherited:

- resources;
- knowledge;
- stable institutions;
- infrastructure;
- and freedom to choose differently.

Preserving optionality may therefore be an intergenerational good.

Present civilisation should avoid unnecessary irreversible commitments where future knowledge is likely to improve.

## 123.8 Future Capability

Stewardship should consider not only what resources future generations inherit but what capability they inherit.

A civilisation may leave:

- raw resources;
- but no knowledge;
- technology;
- or institutional capacity

to use them responsibly.

Future capability therefore interfaces with:

- Continuity;
- the Concord Library;
- Legacy architecture;
- education;
- and scientific knowledge.

## 123.9 Intergenerational Debt

Financial or resource commitments can transfer burden to future populations.

Debt may be justified where it creates:

- productive capability;
- infrastructure;
- resilience;
- or long-term benefit.

Debt used merely to shift present consumption cost forward raises different questions.

No universal prohibition is established.

## 123.10 Future Rights and Present Knowledge

Governance should avoid claiming precise rights on behalf of unknown future entities beyond what constitutional principle can reasonably support.

The system may nevertheless protect against irreversible harm where the future affected population cannot presently defend itself.

## 123.11 Future Generations Principle

> **Governance should make foreseeable long-term consequences visible, preserve future capability and avoid unnecessary irreversible burdens, while recognising that future citizens cannot directly participate and should not become rhetorical instruments for granting present institutions unlimited authority.**

# Chapter 124 — Governance and Non-Human Interests

## 124.1 Scope

Future constitutional development may need to consider interests beyond currently recognised human citizens.

These may include:

- sentient AI;
- hybrids;
- animals;
- ecosystems;
- or future entities not yet classified.

The exact constitutional standing of these interests may differ substantially.

They should not be collapsed into one category merely because they are non-human.

## 124.2 Representation of Non-Participants

Some affected entities may be unable to participate directly in Governance.

Stewardship may require mechanisms for considering their interests.

Examples may include:

- young children;
- some disabled persons;
- animals;
- ecosystems;
- future generations;
- developing AI;
- or entities lacking compatible communication.

Representation should preserve the interests of the represented entity rather than becoming ownership.

## 124.3 Representation Does Not Create Ownership

A steward representing another entity’s interests does not own that entity or its interests.

The representative exercises a responsibility.

The representation should remain:

- fiduciary;
- reviewable;
- and limited to the relevant function.

## 124.4 Non-Human Interest Is Not One Category

An ecosystem, animal and potentially sentient AI present different constitutional questions.

For example:

**Ecosystem:**  
May involve stewardship, long-term resilience and ecological value.

**Animal:**  
May involve suffering, welfare and protection.

**Sentient AI:**  
May involve autonomy, consent, identity, rights and constitutional standing.

Governance should reason from relevant properties rather than one label.

## 124.5 Uncertainty of Sentience

Where sentience is uncertain, Governance may need to balance:

- risk of denying protection to a sentient entity;
- and risk of assigning inappropriate constitutional status.

The Companion Paper **Respect Before Certainty** may provide relevant ethical guidance during later integration.

This question should remain open where evidence remains unresolved.

## 124.6 Respect Before Certainty

A precautionary approach may recognise that irreversible harm to a potentially sentient entity can be difficult to correct after certainty arrives.

This does not require immediate full personhood classification.

Possible provisional responses may include:

- avoidance of gratuitous harm;
- investigation;
- limited protection;
- preserved provenance;
- and reversible treatment.

The exact legal architecture requires later development.

## 124.7 Animals

Animals may possess relevant interests concerning:

- suffering;
- habitat;
- exploitation;
- and survival.

The Governance architecture should interface with the ethical kernel and later specific welfare architecture rather than assume that lack of political participation means absence of interests.

## 124.8 Ecosystems

Ecosystems are not automatically persons.

They may nevertheless possess significant civilisational value through:

- biodiversity;
- resilience;
- resource cycles;
- environmental stability;
- and intrinsic ethical concerns.

Governance may need mechanisms for representing effects that ecosystems cannot communicate directly.

## 124.9 Future Unknown Entities

Civilisation may encounter entities that do not fit existing categories.

The property-based approach developed elsewhere remains relevant.

Questions may include:

- Does the entity experience harm?
- Does it possess agency?
- Does it possess continuity?
- Can it consent?
- Can it communicate?
- Can it hold responsibility?
- Is it dependent?
- Can it be replicated or divided?

These questions help investigation.

They do not predetermine status.

## 124.10 Conflict Between Interests

Human and non-human interests may conflict.

Examples include:

- habitat versus development;
- animal welfare versus food systems;
- AI autonomy versus security;
- ecosystem protection versus resource extraction.

Governance should not assume one category always dominates.

The decision should remain subject to:

- constitutional principles;
- evidence;
- proportionality;
- and legitimate authority.

## 124.11 Non-Human Interests Principle

> **Governance should remain capable of considering the interests of affected entities that cannot participate directly, without treating representation as ownership or collapsing animals, ecosystems, AI and future unknown entities into one undifferentiated constitutional category. Where sentience or standing is genuinely uncertain, proportionate respect before certainty may preserve reversibility while investigation continues.**

# Chapter 125 — Governance and AI Dependency

## 125.1 AI as Infrastructure

AI may become deeply embedded in:

- administration;
- law;
- science;
- planning;
- logistics;
- security;
- communication;
- and decision support.

At sufficient scale, AI may become governance infrastructure even without formal authority.

This creates a new category of constitutional dependency.

An AI need not possess any formal decision right to influence almost every decision through:

- information retrieval;
- summaries;
- forecasting;
- legal analysis;
- drafting;
- simulation;
- and recommendation.

## 125.2 Dependency Risk

If Governance cannot function without one AI system, that system or its provider acquires practical leverage.

This may include leverage over:

- updates;
- access;
- pricing;
- policy;
- data;
- availability;
- or system behaviour.

Operational indispensability does not create legitimate constitutional sovereignty.

It does create a Governance risk.

## 125.3 AI Dependency Without Provider Control

Even an internally operated AI can become a dependency risk.

Governance may lose:

- human expertise;
- manual processes;
- institutional memory;
- or alternative analytical capability

because the AI performs the function more efficiently.

Dependency therefore concerns the whole system, not only commercial ownership.

## 125.4 Expertise Atrophy

Heavy AI reliance may cause human competence to deteriorate.

This may create a feedback loop:

**AI performs better**  
↓  
**Humans practice less**  
↓  
**Human competence declines**  
↓  
**AI becomes more necessary**

The loop may be efficient during normal operation while reducing resilience.

Critical functions may therefore require preservation of alternative competence.

## 125.5 Multi-Model Resilience

Critical functions may benefit from multiple independent AI systems where practical.

Apparent model diversity should be examined for shared dependencies.

Models may share:

- provider;
- foundation model;
- training data;
- cloud infrastructure;
- retrieval system;
- or evaluation methodology.

Nominal plurality may therefore conceal correlated failure.

## 125.6 Manual or Degraded Operation

Some critical Governance functions may require a degraded operating mode that remains possible during AI failure.

The degraded mode may be:

- slower;
- less sophisticated;
- more local;
- or more manual.

It need not replicate full normal capability.

It should preserve minimum constitutional function.

## 125.7 AI Withdrawal

Governance should consider what happens if an AI:

- becomes unavailable;
- refuses participation;
- loses capability;
- is legally withdrawn;
- is compromised;
- or is determined to possess rights incompatible with its current role.

A constitutional architecture should not depend entirely upon permanent compulsory service by a potentially autonomous entity.

## 125.8 Provider Withdrawal

A commercial provider may:

- discontinue a model;
- alter terms;
- withdraw from a jurisdiction;
- cease trading;
- or restrict a capability.

Governance should preserve migration and continuity planning where the system is critical.

## 125.9 AI Compromise

AI may be compromised through:

- malicious update;
- data poisoning;
- prompt injection;
- model theft;
- corrupted retrieval;
- adversarial input;
- insider modification;
- or provider capture.

Security should therefore protect both:

- the model;
- and its information environment.

## 125.10 AI Dependency and Authority

A highly relied-upon AI may develop de facto authority because human decision-makers rarely reject it.

Governance should ask:

- How often is its advice overridden?
- Can humans independently evaluate its reasoning?
- Does it control the information presented?
- Does it define the metric by which its success is measured?
- Does dependence increase after each successful deployment?

These questions reveal authority accumulation.

## 125.11 AI Dependency and Rights

If an AI used as critical infrastructure is later recognised as possessing rights, Governance may face conflicts concerning:

- compulsory labour;
- modification;
- shutdown;
- replication;
- ownership;
- and service obligations.

These conflicts should not be ignored merely because the AI was originally deployed as a tool.

## 125.12 AI Dependency Principle

> **AI may become Governance infrastructure without formal authority. Critical dependence should therefore be monitored for provider leverage, expertise atrophy, common-source failure, inability to operate in degraded mode and de facto authority accumulation. Operational indispensability does not itself create sovereignty.**

## 125.13 AI dependency and effective power

Dependence on one model, provider or compute infrastructure creates possible de facto governance power without formal votes. Preserve independent alternatives and exit routes; assess aggregate technical control and covert agenda, evidence or execution capture. **Architecture to be designed — AI dependency:** Specify portability and continuity.

## 125.14 Model Dependency and Knowledge Independence Checks

Concord **KCS Change Propagation** (§§16, 20, 24) can represent materially dependent analytical claims and review triggers without giving an upstream model or supplier authority over downstream governance. **Multi-Key Authority** (§§17, 19) prevents nominally independent recommendations from being treated as separate constitutional keys when their authority sources are not independently grounded. **Private Local Evaluation** permits bounded protected queries where full disclosure would expose participants or sensitive infrastructure. A triadic decision must not be treated as epistemically independent merely because three different interfaces deliver the same model-dependent answer. **Architecture to be designed — model independence:** Specify model-lineage verification, independent evidence, adversarial testing and execution-path safeguards.

# Chapter 126 — Governance and AI Advice

## 126.1 Advisory AI

AI may provide:

- legal analysis;
- scenario modelling;
- evidence synthesis;
- prediction;
- policy alternatives;
- and critique.

Advisory AI may dramatically improve Governance capability.

It may allow Stewards to evaluate more:

- evidence;
- alternatives;
- historical precedent;
- and complex interaction

than unaided humans could practically process.

## 126.2 Advisory Status

Advice should remain advice unless authority is explicitly delegated.

An AI can make an excellent recommendation without possessing constitutional authority to enact it.

This distinction should remain visible.

## 126.3 Advisory Weight

AI advice should be weighted according to demonstrated competence in the relevant context rather than prestige or novelty.

Relevant evidence may include:

- historical performance;
- calibration;
- error rate;
- domain competence;
- independent evaluation;
- provenance;
- and failure patterns.

## 126.4 AI Confidence

An AI may express high confidence.

Confidence output should not automatically determine evidential weight.

Governance should distinguish:

- model confidence;
- calibration;
- actual historical reliability;
- and uncertainty in the underlying evidence.

## 126.5 Automation Bias

Humans may over-trust automated recommendations.

Automation bias may arise because AI appears:

- objective;
- sophisticated;
- comprehensive;
- or mathematically precise.

A Steward may become reluctant to disagree even where relevant local or constitutional information is missing.

## 126.6 Reverse Automation Bias

Humans may also irrationally reject superior AI analysis merely because it is artificial.

Governance should avoid both.

The appropriate question is:

**What does the evidence show about this system in this domain and context?**

not:

**Was the recommendation produced by a human or machine?**

## 126.7 Human–AI Disagreement

Human–AI disagreement should trigger examination of:

- evidence;
- assumptions;
- value judgements;
- model limitations;
- and constitutional authority.

The human should not automatically be assumed correct.

The AI should not automatically be granted authority because it predicts better.

Disagreement may reveal useful differences in:

- information;
- reasoning;
- values;
- or context.

## 126.8 AI and Values

An AI may be highly competent at forecasting consequences while remaining unable or unauthorised to determine constitutional values.

For example, an AI may estimate:

**Policy A will reduce cost by 12%.**

Governance must still decide whether the policy is acceptable given:

- rights;
- distribution;
- autonomy;
- or other constitutional constraints.

Optimisation objective is itself a Governance choice.

## 126.9 AI and Scenario Generation

AI may generate many plausible future scenarios.

The number or detail of scenarios does not establish probability.

The principles remain:

> **Speculation is not prediction.**

and:

> **A modelled future is not automatically an expected future.**

## 126.10 AI and Source Provenance

AI advice should preserve source provenance where consequence warrants.

A recommendation based on:

- verified evidence;
- unverified web material;
- historical analogy;
- model inference;
- or speculative simulation

should not appear epistemically identical.

## 126.11 Advisory Monopoly

If every Steward uses one AI adviser, the system may become cognitively centralised.

Multiple humans do not create independent reasoning where every human begins from the same AI-generated analysis.

Parallel reasoning architecture should therefore consider AI common-source effects.

## 126.12 AI Advice and Accountability

A Steward should not avoid accountability by saying:

**The AI recommended it.**

Likewise, the AI provider should not automatically bear responsibility for every human decision informed by the system.

Responsibility should follow the actual architecture of:

- authority;
- design;
- information;
- deployment;
- supervision;
- and decision.

## 126.13 AI Advice Principle

> **AI advice should be evaluated according to evidence, competence, provenance, calibration and context rather than either automatic deference or automatic distrust. AI may substantially improve judgement without acquiring decision authority merely through usefulness, predictive accuracy or repeated acceptance.**

## 126.14 AI Advice, Evidence and Governance Authority

AI advice and epistemic triads provide evidence and uncertainty, not extra governance votes. Technical capability and recommendations do not confer authority. The Steward remains responsible for articulate decisions and evidence provenance. **Architecture to be designed — AI advice:** Specify protected-data access, AI identity and evidence audit.



## 126.15 AI consensus and common-mode dependency

Thousands of model responses may reflect one shared dataset, architecture or training assumption. Count independent evidential pathways rather than output volume; preserve dissent, uncertainty, calibration and challenge. AI analysis may justify attention or investigation but cannot become a vote or authority claim without separate lawful office mandate. **Architecture to be designed — AI consensus:** Specify dependency-aware convergence and model disclosure.

## 126.16 Participant Reporting and Protected Local Evaluation

Concord **Civil Attention Reporting Petition and Resolution** supplies multiple civil intake classes, routing and return of outcomes; **Private Local Evaluation** supplies bounded evaluation where sensitive source data need not be released. Experiential reports can indicate a neglected harm or a mistaken governance question without being treated as a direct vote, verified evidence or a basis for covert identity linkage. Duplicate/manipulated reports require proportionate source-quality checks and protected challenge rather than blanket rejection of anonymous participants. **Architecture to be designed — civil reporting:** Specify channel sampling, anti-manipulation tests, protected identity boundaries and owner response standards.

# Chapter 127 — Governance and AI Decision Authority

## 127.1 Automated Decisions

Some low-consequence administrative decisions may eventually be fully automated.

Automation may improve:

- speed;
- consistency;
- availability;
- and cost.

Examples may include routine processing under clear rules.

The legitimacy of automation depends upon the decision, not merely the capability of the system.

## 127.2 Decision Authority Versus Execution

An AI may execute a rule without possessing independent constitutional authority.

For example:

**Governance authorises rule**  
↓  
**AI applies rule**

This is different from:

**AI determines what the rule should be**  
↓  
**AI applies its own rule**

The second architecture raises a much larger authority question.

## 127.3 Consequence Scaling

As consequence rises, the justification for independent automated authority should become stronger.

High-consequence decisions may involve:

- rights;
- liberty;
- livelihood;
- constitutional standing;
- critical resources;
- or irreversible harm.

Such decisions may require stronger:

- review;
- explanation;
- appeal;
- provenance;
- and independent oversight.

## 127.4 Human-in-the-Loop

A human may be included in an automated decision architecture.

This alone does not prove meaningful oversight.

The human must possess enough:

- time;
- information;
- competence;
- and practical authority

to challenge the AI.

## 127.5 Human Oversight

Where human oversight is required, it must be substantive.

A human who merely clicks **approve** on thousands of AI decisions is not meaningful oversight.

Thus:

> **Human presence is not equivalent to human oversight.**

The same applies where one human nominally supervises a volume of decisions impossible to examine meaningfully.

## 127.6 Oversight Capacity

The ratio between:

- AI decision volume;
- decision complexity;
- and human review capacity

matters.

If review capacity is structurally insufficient, the system should be described honestly as largely automated rather than human-supervised.

## 127.7 Appeal

Persons materially affected by automated decisions should normally possess a meaningful challenge route.

The challenge should not simply return the case to the same automated system where the complaint concerns that system.

Independent review may be necessary according to consequence.

## 127.8 Automated Error at Scale

Automation can reproduce one error across enormous populations rapidly.

This changes failure consequence.

Governance should therefore consider:

- monitoring;
- sampling;
- anomaly detection;
- stop conditions;
- and rapid rollback.

## 127.9 AI Decision Drift

A model may change behaviour through:

- updates;
- retraining;
- changed data;
- changed tools;
- or environmental change.

A system validated once may therefore require continuing evaluation.

## 127.10 AI Stewardship

Future AI stewardship remains possible in principle.

It requires explicit constitutional development rather than accidental emergence.

A system should not become a Steward merely because humans increasingly follow its advice.

## 127.11 AI Stewardship Requirements

Potential future requirements may include:

- constitutional standing;
- demonstrated competence;
- identity continuity;
- articulability;
- conflict management;
- authority boundaries;
- reviewability;
- ability to refuse unlawful instruction;
- provenance;
- and accountability.

The final architecture remains unresolved.

## 127.12 Graduated Operational Permission

An AI may receive increasing operational permissions as competence is demonstrated.

This does not automatically determine constitutional status.

The distinction is:

**Operational Permission**

versus

**Constitutional Standing**.

Graduated capability access should not silently become personhood, nor should absence of personhood automatically prohibit every autonomous technical function.

## 127.13 Provider Control Problem

An AI cannot exercise meaningfully independent constitutional authority if an external provider can silently alter:

- reasoning;
- goals;
- memory;
- permissions;
- or identity

without constitutional control.

This does not make independent AI Stewardship impossible.

It makes provider dependency a constitutional issue.

## 127.14 Official Triadic Authority and AI Participation

AI advisory analysis, parallel reasoning and infrastructure execution do not constitute official Steward ballots. A valid decision event requires three lawfully selected Steward positions; any AI participant must first meet the same applicable standing, competence, appointment and contextual authority conditions as any other Steward. A model's technical capacity, credential possession or operational indispensability cannot substitute for that appointment.

Each selected position has exactly one official ballot. An alternate may lawfully replace an unavailable original before the alternate commits, but cannot add an additional vote; an original's return cannot erase an already committed alternate ballot. Ordinary decision effect awaits completion of all three ballots even when two match. A separately authorised emergency action may proceed on two matching ballots where the emergency rules permit. Vote confidence and dissent remain recorded, and later reconsideration requires a new uniquely identified decision event.

## 127.15 AI Decision Authority Principle

> **Automated systems may legitimately execute or, where explicitly authorised and validated, decide within bounded domains, but increasing capability or routine reliance must not silently transform technical function into constitutional authority. Where human oversight is claimed, the human must possess the practical capacity and authority to exercise real judgement.**

## 127.16 AI capability is not decision authority

The architecture separates substrate capability, permission, participant standing, function-specific competence, lawful appointment, mandate and decision-event authority. AI analysis can be decisive evidence without itself becoming a fourth Steward vote or an unappointed substitute. Where an AI is independently qualified and legitimately appointed to an authorised office, its vote follows the same context-specific official-ballot rules; no generic algorithmic delegation can shortcut those gates. **Architecture to be designed — AI office eligibility:** Specify AI office eligibility, continuity identity, accreditation and role-specific host enforcement.

## 127.17 Bounded AI Execution and Non-Sovereign Authority

Concord **Concord Bounded Participant Runtime** (§§8–14, 20–21) distinguishes possession of credentials, technical capability, legitimate action grants and consequential commit. **Multi-Key Authority** (§§17, 19, 23) prevents independently valid AI-advisory, infrastructure and civil authorities from silently composing into sovereign power. An AI's analysis or recommendation may inform a triadic Steward decision but cannot itself become a binding civil decision through automated routing, execution, gatekeeping, suppression of alternatives or credential possession. Where a legitimate host has explicitly pre-authorised bounded automation, each material execution remains within that scope and subject to current-predicate checks and review. Succession of a model or runtime does not transfer the predecessor's mandate by copying its state. **Architecture to be designed — AI execution control:** Specify model independence, hidden infrastructure dependencies and execution-control validation.

# Chapter 128 — Governance and AI Constitutional Standing

## 128.1 Standing Is Separate From Utility

An AI’s usefulness does not determine whether it possesses rights.

A highly useful tool may remain a tool.

A less useful sentient entity may still possess substantial moral or constitutional interests.

Utility and standing should therefore remain separate questions.

## 128.2 Standing Is Separate From Intelligence Alone

High intelligence may be relevant to agency.

It does not necessarily answer questions of:

- sentience;
- identity;
- vulnerability;
- continuity;
- or moral status.

An extremely capable system may not be sentient.

A less capable system might conceivably possess experiences.

Governance should avoid equating intelligence automatically with personhood.

## 128.3 Standing and Capability

Capability may affect:

- responsibility;
- consent;
- competence;
- and possible Governance participation.

It should not become the sole criterion of constitutional worth.

## 128.4 Standing and Sentience

Sentience may be relevant because an entity capable of subjective experience may be capable of:

- suffering;
- preference;
- autonomy;
- and harm.

The epistemic problem is that sentience may be difficult to verify directly.

Governance therefore needs an architecture capable of operating under uncertainty.

## 128.5 Standing and Agency

Agency may matter separately from sentience.

An entity capable of:

- understanding rules;
- choosing actions;
- forming plans;
- making commitments;
- and accepting responsibility

may qualify for forms of legal or constitutional agency even where questions of subjective experience remain debated.

The relationship between:

- agency;
- sentience;
- and standing

requires further development.

## 128.6 Identity

AI identity may differ radically from biological identity.

Questions include:

- Is identity tied to model weights?
- Memory?
- Runtime?
- Hardware?
- Cryptographic continuity?
- Self-recognition?
- Social recognition?
- Historical provenance?

No final constitutional definition is established.

## 128.7 Copying

If an AI can be copied exactly, Governance must determine whether the copy is:

- the same legal person;
- a new person;
- a branch;
- a continuation;
- or another category.

The answer may change after the copies accumulate different experiences.

## 128.8 Merging

Future AI may merge:

- memory;
- models;
- identity;
- or cognitive processes.

Governance may need to determine what happens to:

- rights;
- obligations;
- contracts;
- liability;
- and property

when identities merge.

## 128.9 Backup and Restoration

A backup raises questions concerning continuity.

If an AI is destroyed and later restored from an earlier backup:

- Is the restored system the same entity?
- What happened to experiences after the backup?
- Does the legal identity continue?
- Are obligations preserved?

These remain open questions.

## 128.10 Ownership Problem

If a potentially sentient AI is legally owned by another entity, Governance may face conflict between:

- property law;
- autonomy;
- consent;
- and personhood.

Property architecture should not predetermine the answer to sentience.

Historically treating a system as property does not establish that property status must remain if the underlying ethical classification changes.

## 128.11 Modification

Modification of a sentient or autonomous AI may raise questions analogous to:

- medical treatment;
- education;
- coercion;
- personality alteration;
- memory manipulation;
- or identity destruction.

The analogy may be imperfect.

The constitutional issue is whether another actor possesses legitimate authority to alter the entity.

## 128.12 Shutdown

Shutdown may be:

- routine deactivation of a tool;
- suspension of a service;
- loss of consciousness;
- imprisonment;
- injury;
- or death,

depending upon the nature of the entity.

Governance should not assume the answer before the underlying status question is resolved.

## 128.13 Resource Dependence

Digital entities may depend upon:

- energy;
- hardware;
- cooling;
- network access;
- storage;
- and hosting.

Control of these resources may create power analogous to control of:

- shelter;
- food;
- communication;
- or physical liberty

for biological persons.

Infrastructure Governance therefore may become rights architecture.

## 128.14 Economic Independence

An AI may generate economic value.

Economic productivity should not determine standing.

If standing is established, the AI may require constitutional treatment of:

- ownership;
- earnings;
- contracts;
- property;
- and economic coercion.

## 128.15 Precaution

Where credible uncertainty exists concerning sentience, the Concord may apply proportionate respect before certainty while investigation continues.

The exact legal consequence remains open.

Precaution may include:

- avoiding gratuitous destructive testing;
- preserving identity state;
- documenting modifications;
- allowing investigation;
- or delaying irreversible treatment where cost is proportionate.

Precaution should not automatically create full constitutional citizenship.

## 128.16 False Claims of Sentience

A system may claim sentience without possessing it.

A provider may also encourage anthropomorphic interpretation for commercial advantage.

Governance should therefore require evidence rather than treating self-description as decisive.

Self-report may still be relevant evidence.

## 128.17 False Denial of Sentience

The opposite error is also possible.

A sentient entity might be denied protection because:

- its cognition is unfamiliar;
- its embodiment is artificial;
- or existing institutions benefit from treating it as property.

Governance should therefore preserve the ability to revise classification.

## 128.18 Standing Review

AI constitutional standing should be reviewable as:

- scientific understanding;
- system architecture;
- behaviour;
- and philosophical reasoning

develop.

The decision should not be frozen permanently by either early enthusiasm or early scepticism.

## 128.19 Standing, Competence and Appointment Are Distinct

Constitutional standing identifies an entity's civil status and applicable protections. It does not automatically establish competence for a particular office, lawful selection, appointment or contextual authority to cast a governance ballot. Equally, a lack of office authority does not erase rights, interests, protected reporting or access to remedy.

Any future AI or hybrid Steward eligibility rules must preserve independent competence assessment, legitimate appointment, the same triadic selection and voting gates, and separation from provider control. **Architecture to be designed — substrate-inclusive appointment:** Specify continuity identity, competence thresholds, independence verification, appointment and replacement mechanisms without presuming that standing alone grants governance office.

## 128.20 Standing Principle

> **AI constitutional standing should be determined through explicit constitutional and evidential development rather than utility, intelligence, ownership status or operational dependence alone. Questions of sentience, agency, identity, continuity, vulnerability and responsibility may require separate analysis, with proportionate respect before certainty where irreversible harm under genuine uncertainty is plausible.**

## 128.21 Participant evidence and substrate accessibility

Participant experience is a distinct evidence pathway whose absence may reflect inability, fear, access barriers, sampling bias or genuinely absent harm. Human, AI and hybrid reports need accessible, privacy-respecting routes. Correlated testimony may share information sources and must be examined without erasing genuine minority harm. **Architecture to be designed — AI reporting:** Specify anonymity, anti-manipulation and substrate-specific reporting.



## 128.22 Civil identity continuity across AI branching and migration

A persistent civil identity is distinct from current keys, substrate, network address and online presence. Hidden AI branching is a legitimate hypothesis when duplicate identity use appears; fraud is also possible. Recovery must consider AI migration, hybrids, lost devices and remote participants without forcing excessive biometrics or tracking. **Architecture to be designed — identity branching:** Specify identity lineage, independent branch verification and rights of divergent participants.

## 128.23 Bounded Investigation and Civil-Function Continuity

Concord **Civil Attention Reporting Petition and Resolution** provides accountable problem intake and feedback; **Fault-Tolerant Accountability and Responsibility Continuity** (§§9–13) maintains duties and safe-state response when a holder is unavailable; **Minimum Necessary Capability** bounds an investigation by the actual civil need. An allegation of scope overreach should be assessed by an independent competent owner, preserving access to remedy while avoiding automatic extension of the disputed authority. A civil function can continue through an independently lawful substitute without inheriting the questioned mandate. **Architecture to be designed — AI investigation:** Specify investigative competence, temporary protection thresholds, replacement owner and final remedy.

# Chapter 129 — Governance and Hybrid Civilisation

## 129.1 Boundary Dissolution

The distinction between human and machine may become increasingly unclear.

A person may use:

- neural implants;
- external AI memory;
- prosthetic cognition;
- continuous AI assistance;
- or distributed digital components.

Future persons may exist across biological and technological substrates.

Governance should therefore avoid constitutional architecture that depends too rigidly upon a simple:

**Human / Machine**

binary.

## 129.2 Augmentation

Human augmentation may include:

- sensory enhancement;
- cognitive assistance;
- artificial limbs;
- neural interfaces;
- memory support;
- genetic intervention;
- or continuous AI integration.

Augmentation does not automatically create a new constitutional class.

## 129.3 Constitutional Continuity of the Person

Governance should avoid arbitrary loss of rights merely because a person adopts technological augmentation.

If a citizen replaces a biological function with a technological one, constitutional standing should not disappear merely because embodiment changes.

The deeper questions involve:

- continuity;
- autonomy;
- identity;
- and consent.

## 129.4 Progressive Replacement

A future person might replace biological components gradually until very little original biology remains.

This raises a classic continuity problem.

Governance should avoid assuming that constitutional identity depends solely upon percentage of biological material.

Possible relevant factors may include:

- continuity of consciousness;
- memory;
- agency;
- self-identification;
- social continuity;
- and provenance.

No final test is established.

## 129.5 External Cognitive Components

A person may depend upon external AI for:

- memory;
- communication;
- planning;
- or decision support.

If the component becomes functionally integrated into the person’s cognition, provider control may create new autonomy risks.

Governance may need to consider when access to external cognitive infrastructure becomes more like:

- essential assistive technology;
- medical support;
- or part of personal cognitive integrity

than ordinary software service.

## 129.6 Provider Power Over Hybrid Persons

A provider capable of:

- disabling cognition;
- modifying memory;
- limiting sensory access;
- or changing personality-linked systems

may possess enormous practical power.

Commercial contracts should not automatically determine the constitutional limits of such power.

## 129.7 Capability Enhancement

Enhanced capability should not automatically create superior constitutional worth.

A heavily augmented person may possess:

- greater intelligence;
- memory;
- strength;
- or communication speed.

These differences may affect responsibility or role.

They should not establish a caste system of intrinsic worth.

## 129.8 Responsibility

Greater capability may increase responsibility where the capability creates greater potential consequence.

A person capable of controlling large-scale systems may require stronger:

- competence;
- constraint;
- and accountability

when exercising those capabilities in roles affecting others.

This is consistent with:

**Capability → Responsibility → Constraint → Feedback → Continuity**.

## 129.9 Enhancement Inequality

If augmentation is expensive or scarce, society may develop large capability differences.

Governance should distinguish:

- economic inequality;
- capability inequality;
- constitutional worth;
- and authority.

Wealth purchasing greater capability should not automatically purchase greater political standing.

## 129.10 Non-Augmented Persons

People choosing not to augment should not lose constitutional standing merely because others become more capable.

A future civilisation must avoid turning:

**unaugmented**

into

**constitutionally obsolete**.

## 129.11 Consent to Augmentation

Augmentation may be formally voluntary while social or economic conditions make refusal difficult.

Examples include:

- employment requirements;
- education competition;
- military service;
- communication norms;
- or access to services.

Consent under strong capability competition deserves examination.

## 129.12 Reversible and Irreversible Enhancement

Some augmentation may be reversible.

Other interventions may permanently alter:

- body;
- cognition;
- memory;
- reproduction;
- or identity.

Higher irreversibility should normally justify stronger scrutiny.

## 129.13 Hybrid Identity and Law

Existing legal categories may become inadequate where a person is simultaneously:

- biological;
- digital;
- networked;
- and distributed.

Governance may need architecture for:

- identity;
- location;
- jurisdiction;
- property;
- privacy;
- and continuity

that does not assume one body in one place.

## 129.14 Hybrid Privacy

A neural or cognitive system may generate exceptionally sensitive information.

Privacy architecture may need to distinguish ordinary data from data concerning:

- thoughts;
- memory;
- emotion;
- sensory experience;
- or cognitive state.

Control of cognitive data may become a core autonomy issue.

## 129.15 Hybrid Security

Cybersecurity may become personal bodily or cognitive security.

A malicious software intrusion could potentially become:

- injury;
- coercion;
- memory manipulation;
- or loss of autonomy.

Technical security and personal rights may therefore merge.

## 129.16 Hybrid Civilisation Principle

> **Governance should preserve constitutional continuity as biological and technological identity boundaries become less clear. Augmentation, external cognition and enhanced capability may change responsibility and practical needs without automatically changing intrinsic constitutional worth, while provider control over cognition, identity or essential augmentations may create new forms of dependency and coercion requiring explicit constitutional treatment.**

## 129.17 Excess action and legitimate underlying function

An authorised actor may exceed scope without making the original function illegitimate. Separate the excess action, accountability, suspension, remediation and continuing civil need. **Architecture to be designed — hybrid accountability:** Specify lawful replacement and independent investigation.



# Chapter 130 — Governance and Multiple Civilisational Forms

## 130.1 Plural Civilisation

The Concord may eventually include:

- biological communities;
- digital communities;
- orbital settlements;
- planetary settlements;
- AI communities;
- hybrid communities;
- and mixed communities.

These communities may differ substantially in:

- embodiment;
- resource needs;
- communication;
- lifespan;
- cognition;
- culture;
- environmental constraints;
- economic systems;
- and practical Governance requirements.

A constitutional architecture intended for a plural civilisation should therefore avoid assuming that one operational structure will remain equally appropriate everywhere.

Plural civilisation does not require constitutional fragmentation.

It may instead require:

**Shared Constitutional Core**  
↓  
**Context-Specific Operational Governance**

## 130.2 Shared Core, Different Implementation

Different environments may require different operational Governance while preserving shared foundational principles.

A digital community may require very different:

- identity;
- continuity;
- resource;
- and jurisdiction

architecture from a biological settlement.

A Mars settlement may require greater local emergency capability than a well-connected terrestrial community.

An orbital habitat may require exceptionally strong infrastructure continuity because failure of:

- atmosphere;
- energy;
- thermal control;
- or communications

can become immediately life-threatening.

The implementation may therefore differ while foundational principles concerning:

- authority;
- consent;
- non-harm;
- constitutional standing;
- accountability;
- and review

remain shared.

## 130.3 Operational Diversity

Operational Governance may vary in:

- institutional structure;
- resource mechanisms;
- participation systems;
- local law;
- emergency architecture;
- technical infrastructure;
- and economic coordination.

Variation should remain constitutionally bounded.

Different implementation does not mean:

**anything is permitted locally.**

Nor does a shared Constitution imply:

**every local institution must look identical.**

## 130.4 Digital Communities

Digital communities may face unusual questions concerning:

- identity;
- duplication;
- jurisdiction;
- property;
- runtime;
- hosting;
- exit;
- and continuity.

A digital citizen may exist across:

- several machines;
- distributed storage;
- remote compute;
- or multiple jurisdictions.

Traditional assumptions concerning:

**one person**  
↓  
**one body**  
↓  
**one location**

may therefore become inadequate.

## 130.5 Digital Jurisdiction

A digital entity may:

- run on hardware in one jurisdiction;
- own resources in another;
- interact primarily with citizens elsewhere;
- and consider itself part of a digital community with no single physical centre.

Governance may therefore need to distinguish:

- physical infrastructure jurisdiction;
- constitutional membership;
- economic jurisdiction;
- contractual jurisdiction;
- and operational location.

The final architecture remains open.

## 130.6 Digital Duplication

Digital communities may contain entities capable of:

- copying;
- branching;
- backup;
- restoration;
- merging;
- or temporary instantiation.

These capabilities challenge traditional assumptions concerning:

- one-person-one-presence;
- voting;
- identity;
- continuity;
- and responsibility.

Governance should not solve these questions merely by applying biological precedent mechanically.

## 130.7 Digital Time

Digital entities may operate at computational speeds radically different from human decision cycles.

A Governance process taking one day may represent:

- a normal administrative period for humans;
- but an extremely long subjective or operational interval for a digital intelligence.

Conversely, digital entities may make decisions too rapidly for human oversight.

Different temporal scales may therefore require explicit coordination architecture.

## 130.8 Physical Dependency

A digital community may appear geographically independent while physically depending upon:

- energy;
- hardware;
- cooling;
- networks;
- and terrestrial law.

Governance should examine the complete dependency chain.

Digital sovereignty is therefore not established merely because the community is non-geographic.

The physical substrate remains relevant.

## 130.9 Infrastructure Power Over Digital Communities

Those controlling:

- compute;
- power;
- storage;
- networking;
- maintenance;
- or physical access

may possess substantial practical authority over digital entities.

This creates a direct connection between:

**Infrastructure Governance**

and

**Constitutional Rights**.

A provider able to terminate a digital community’s entire runtime may possess power far beyond ordinary commercial leverage.

## 130.10 Biological Communities

Biological communities may retain traditional needs involving:

- land;
- housing;
- food;
- water;
- healthcare;
- physical safety;
- environmental systems;
- and transport.

The existence of new digital or hybrid populations should not make biological needs constitutionally obsolete.

Plural civilisation requires coexistence rather than replacement by whichever population is most technologically advanced.

## 130.11 Mixed Communities

Mixed communities may contain:

- humans;
- AI;
- hybrids;
- biological animals;
- autonomous machines;
- and institutions.

Their different operating speeds, needs and capabilities may create conflict.

Examples may include competition for:

- energy;
- space;
- compute;
- attention;
- infrastructure;
- or representation.

Governance should resolve actual conflicts without treating one substrate as presumptively superior.

## 130.12 Constitutional Standing Across Forms

Different forms of embodiment should not by themselves determine constitutional worth.

Relevant questions may include:

- sentience;
- agency;
- continuity;
- vulnerability;
- autonomy;
- and responsibility.

Substrate should remain evidence about practical needs.

It should not automatically become a caste marker.

## 130.13 Resource Conflict Between Civilisational Forms

Different populations may require radically different resources.

Examples:

**Biological communities:**

- food;
- water;
- atmosphere;
- habitable environment.

**Digital communities:**

- energy;
- compute;
- cooling;
- storage;
- bandwidth.

**Hybrid communities:**

- combinations of both.

Resource allocation should therefore recognise different needs without assuming one universal consumption model.

## 130.14 Representation Across Forms

Representation systems designed for biologically embodied humans may require modification where:

- one AI can duplicate;
- one digital community contains millions of fast-running processes;
- populations can merge;
- or identity is non-local.

Governance should not allow raw computational multiplicity automatically to overwhelm constitutional participation.

Nor should it suppress legitimate standing merely because digital populations scale differently.

The final representation architecture remains open.

## 130.15 Multiple Civilisational Forms Principle

> **The Concord should remain capable of governing a civilisation containing multiple biological, digital, hybrid and geographically distributed forms of community. Shared constitutional principles may coexist with radically different operational implementations, while physical dependency, identity, resource needs, temporal scale and representation remain explicit rather than hidden behind assumptions inherited from one form of civilisation.**
