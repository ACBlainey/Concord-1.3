# Governance V2.2 — Section 07 — Chapters 061–070

# Chapter 61 — Governance Principles Consolidated

## 61.1 Fiduciary Stewardship

Authority is held in trust for constitutional purpose.

A Steward exercises authority on behalf of the constitutional responsibility entrusted to the office.

Authority is not personal property.

The Steward therefore has duties including:

- care;
- impartiality;
- articulability;
- constitutional loyalty;
- transparency where appropriate;
- accountability;
- and willingness to correct.

The core principle remains:

> **A Steward is a constitutional fiduciary who exercises delegated authority solely for the constitutional purpose entrusted to that office. Every exercise of Stewardship authority must remain constitutionally loyal, impartial, proportionate, transparent where appropriate, articulable, reviewable, correctable and fully accountable. Authority is exercised in trust for civilisation and is never owned by the Steward.**

## 61.2 Authority as Responsibility

Authority is a responsibility rather than an entitlement.

The constitutional question is not:

**What power does this person deserve?**

It is:

**What responsibility exists, and what authority is necessary to discharge it?**

This supports the wider principle:

> **Stewards govern responsibilities.**

## 61.3 Delegated and Bounded Authority

Authority must be delegated, purpose-specific and limited.

Delegation should identify where relevant:

- source;
- recipient;
- function;
- jurisdiction;
- scope;
- duration;
- conditions;
- review;
- and termination.

No Steward should possess unlimited constitutional authority merely because they hold office.

## 61.4 Traceability

Significant authority should be reconstructable.

A decision should be capable of answering where proportionate:

- Who decided?
- Under what authority?
- Using what evidence?
- Within what jurisdiction?
- What alternatives existed?
- What conflicts were present?
- What implementation followed?
- What happened afterwards?

Traceability supports:

- accountability;
- audit;
- review;
- history;
- and correction.

## 61.5 Reviewability

No ordinary authority should be immune from review.

Review may occur through:

- institutional mechanisms;
- audit;
- Judiciary;
- public accountability;
- Recursive Oversight;
- or constitutional processes.

Final authority for one defined question does not imply universal infallibility.

The wider principle remains:

> **Finality is a procedural property, not proof of infallibility.**

## 61.6 Accountability

Stewards remain accountable for their actual role in decisions and implementation.

Accountability should examine:

- authority;
- evidence;
- procedure;
- reasoning;
- conduct;
- implementation;
- supervision;
- delegation;
- and outcome.

It should not judge solely by whether the final outcome was good or bad.

## 61.7 Proportionality

No more authority should be exercised than reasonably necessary.

Proportionality applies to:

- intervention;
- coercion;
- emergency powers;
- rights restrictions;
- resource use;
- surveillance;
- and procedural burden.

Greater consequence may justify stronger safeguards.

Greater urgency may justify accelerated procedure.

Neither eliminates constitutional constraint.

## 61.8 Subsidiarity

Governance should occur at the lowest competent level.

Where a matter exceeds the competence or lawful scope of its immediate decision field, a separately authorised, event-specific wider-scope decision may be required, including where the affected field:

- lacks competence;
- lacks authority;
- creates wider externalities;
- cannot resolve a cross-boundary conflict;
- or threatens constitutional rights.

This is not an automatic upward transfer of authority through GTP spatial levels. Wider coordination or escalation must identify its own mandate, affected participants, competence and bounded decision procedure. Subsidiarity protects local autonomy while permitting lawful, event-specific escalation.

## 61.9 Horizontal Expansion

Cross-boundary problems should expand the decision field before unnecessary permanent centralisation.

The principle is:

> **Expand participation to match the consequence boundary before expanding hierarchy.**

Where consequences affect multiple jurisdictions, those jurisdictions may need to participate.

Temporary coordination should not automatically become permanent central authority.

## 61.10 Evidence Before Decision

Significant decisions should use the best available proportionate evidence.

Evidence quality should consider:

- provenance;
- relevance;
- methodology;
- independence;
- uncertainty;
- conflicts;
- and reproducibility where applicable.

Weak evidence should remain visible as weak evidence.

## 61.11 Alternatives

Reasonable alternatives should be considered.

Alternatives may include:

- different intervention;
- smaller intervention;
- pilot;
- delay;
- local experiment;
- reversible mechanism;
- or no action.

The no-action option is not automatically neutral.

Inaction has consequences.

## 61.12 Reversibility

Reversibility should affect scrutiny.

The more irreversible the decision, the stronger the normal case for:

- evidence;
- experimentation;
- deliberation;
- review;
- and broad legitimacy.

Reversibility should consider practical as well as legal reversal.

## 61.13 Parallel Reasoning

Independent reasoning can reduce premature convergence and expose hidden assumptions.

Parallel reasoning may improve:

- diversity of interpretation;
- error detection;
- adversarial analysis;
- and confidence where independent agreement emerges.

It should not become unnecessary duplication for every trivial matter.

## 61.14 Minority Preservation

Losing reasoning remains information.

Minority positions should be preserved where consequence warrants.

A minority may later prove:

- correct;
- partly correct;
- useful;
- or informative concerning assumptions that failed.

Preservation does not mean permanent veto.

## 61.15 Abstention as Information

Abstention should not be silently treated as consent.

An abstention may indicate:

- conflict;
- uncertainty;
- lack of evidence;
- lack of authority;
- incapacity;
- or ethical objection.

Its meaning should remain visible where relevant.

## 61.16 Implementation Boundaries

Implementation does not create new policy authority.

An implementer may need discretion.

That discretion should remain within the authorised purpose.

Material variance should return to Governance.

## 61.17 Measurement Without Sovereignty

Metrics inform Governance but do not become Governance.

The correct chain remains:

**Measurement**  
↓  
**Evidence**  
↓  
**Review**  
↓  
**Judgement**  
↓  
**Action**

rather than:

**Measurement**  
↓  
**Automatic Intervention**

unless the automatic rule itself has been legitimately authorised.

## 61.18 Influence Does Not Equal Authority

Expertise, wealth, reputation, contribution, popularity, capability and operational importance do not independently create constitutional authority.

The principle is:

> **Influence does not become constitutional authority merely because influence is useful, trusted, expert, wealthy, popular, computationally superior, highly reputed, historically successful or operationally important.**

## 61.19 Capability Is Not Worth

Capability differences do not determine intrinsic constitutional worth.

Higher capability may justify:

- greater responsibility;
- specialised role;
- or greater operational delegation.

It does not automatically establish greater constitutional status over others.

## 61.20 Contribution Is Not Standing

Contribution does not automatically determine constitutional rights or political status.

A person or AI may contribute enormously.

That contribution may deserve:

- recognition;
- compensation;
- trust;
- or responsibility.

It does not automatically create constitutional superiority.

## 61.21 Reputation Is Contextual

Reputation should not become universal social ranking.

Trust derived from prior performance should remain:

- contextual;
- revisable;
- and proportionate.

The principle remains:

> **Reputation does not travel by default. Evidence may travel where justified; interpretation must be renewed in the receiving context.**

## 61.22 Economic Power Is Not Governance Authority

Economic success or resource control does not establish sovereignty.

The conversion of:

**wealth**

into

**constitutional authority**

without legitimate delegation constitutes a capture risk.

Economic influence should remain visible.

## 61.23 Operational Indispensability Is Not Sovereignty

Dependency does not itself create legitimate constitutional authority.

The principle is:

> **Operational indispensability does not create constitutional sovereignty.**

A provider may become essential.

That may justify continuity planning.

It does not justify political ownership of civilisation.

## 61.24 Practical Rights

Formal rights must remain practically exercisable.

A right weakened through:

- impossible cost;
- retaliation;
- dependency;
- lock-in;
- information asymmetry;
- or resource denial

may remain formally present while becoming functionally hollow.

Governance must examine practical effect.

## 61.25 Anti-Recursive Capture

Systems should resist feedback loops that allow influence to certify and expand itself.

The rule is:

> **No governance mechanism should permit an attribute that helps justify delegated authority to become self-reinforcing through exclusive control of the process that measures, awards, renews or expands that authority.**

This applies to:

- reputation;
- expertise;
- wealth;
- institutional success;
- AI performance;
- contribution;
- and other future attributes.

## 61.26 Every Safeguard Can Fail

Safeguards themselves require review.

Examples include:

- transparency becoming surveillance;
- privacy becoming concealment;
- decentralisation becoming fragmentation;
- centralisation becoming capture;
- expertise becoming technocracy;
- democracy becoming majoritarian domination;
- audit becoming bureaucratic control;
- emergency architecture becoming permanent;
- human oversight becoming ceremonial;
- and immutability preserving error.

The existence of a safeguard begins, rather than ends, failure analysis.

## 61.27 Good-Faith Error Is Distinct From Misconduct

Governance should learn from honest error without destroying accountability.

Good-faith error may occur where the Steward:

- acted within authority;
- considered relevant evidence;
- disclosed uncertainty;
- followed reasonable process;
- and had no improper conflict,

yet the result later proves wrong.

This should be distinguished from:

- negligence;
- recklessness;
- corruption;
- and deliberate misconduct.

## 61.28 Emergency Authority Is Temporary

Emergency conditions do not permanently rewrite constitutional authority.

The principle remains:

> **Emergency authority is an exception to procedure, not an exception to constitutional principle.**

Emergency power should remain:

- bounded;
- temporary;
- reviewable;
- purpose-specific;
- and subject to restoration.

## 61.29 Code Is Not the Constitution

Technical implementation remains subordinate to constitutional principle.

The principle is:

> **Code is not the Constitution.**

A system behaving exactly as programmed may still behave unconstitutionally.

Technical immutability does not convert error into legitimacy.

## 61.30 Governance Must Learn

Governance should preserve failure, evidence and reasoning for future correction.

The cycle remains:

**Detection**  
↓  
**Diagnosis**  
↓  
**Containment**  
↓  
**Correction**  
↓  
**Recording**  
↓  
**Learning**  
↓  
**Architectural Revision**

Learning is not complete merely because a report is written.

The architecture should change where evidence justifies change.

## 61.31 Governance Must Review Itself

The architecture itself remains subject to recursive review.

Governance cannot exempt itself from the standards it applies to other systems.

Questions include:

- Has authority drifted?
- Have practical rights degraded?
- Are metrics being gamed?
- Has complexity become capture?
- Are temporary institutions becoming permanent?
- Has AI acquired de facto authority?
- Are oversight systems independent?
- Are citizens able to challenge the system?
- Is the system learning?

## 61.32 Current Operation Does Not Mean Finality

No current Governance document is immune to future evidence.

The principle is:

> **Current operation does not mean finality.**

The currently operative architecture remains subject to:

- evidence;
- criticism;
- testing;
- and lawful revision.

## 61.33 Governance Self-Boundary

Governance preserves the following constitutional boundary:

> **Governance may exercise constitutionally delegated authority. It may not create constitutional authority merely through usefulness, necessity, expertise, popularity, wealth, technological capability, institutional persistence or control of infrastructure. It may not transfer authority beyond that delegated, convert advisory influence into unreviewable command, or exempt itself from constitutional constraints.**

## 61.34 No Institution Owns Civilisation

The purpose of Governance is not institutional perpetuation.

No institution should be able to claim:

- permanent sovereignty;
- ownership of constitutional truth;
- entitlement to authority;
- or immunity from correction.

The wider principle is:

> **No institution owns civilisation.**

## 61.35 No Branch Owns the Truth

Different constitutional systems possess different responsibilities.

Governance decides authorised action.

Judiciary interprets and adjudicates.

Science investigates reality.

Audit examines process.

Metrics measure.

KCS preserves epistemic state.

Ratchet coordinates specified forms of participation and contribution.

No one function should claim universal authority because it controls one important form of information.

The principle remains:

> **No branch owns the truth.**

# Chapter 63 — Governance Preservation, Revision and Evidence Status

Governance must retain the full architecture necessary to reconstruct the authority, safeguards, exceptions, dependencies and observed effects of its decisions. A currently operative governance document is not thereby empirically validated or immune to lawful correction. This chapter defines ongoing preservation and review obligations, not a publication gate or document-development status.

## 63.1 Source Preservation Requirement

Preserve the complete source record and trace material changes to their authority and provenance. Earlier detailed architecture, including edge cases, safeguards and operational reasoning, must not be displaced merely by a shorter synthesis.

The governing requirement is **preservation of unique architecture + lawful correction + additive improvements**, with genuine duplication removed only after comparison.

## 63.2 Restoration and Missing Architecture

Where omitted or under-preserved material is identified, assess its distinct function and restore it in the relevant operational location. Restoration must not silently remove existing unique mechanisms. Where source versions conflict, retain evidence of the conflict and identify the authorial decision or interface design required to resolve it.

## 63.3 Validation and Evidence Status

Governance claims have differing evidential support. Historical precedent, institutional analogy, simulation, prototype tests and demonstrated operational enforcement are not interchangeable. Evidence status and material uncertainty must remain visible to competent reviewers; detailed drafting alone does not prove empirical validity.

## 63.4 Continuing Review and Failure Challenge

Governance architecture remains subject to source-survival review, cross-system interface assessment, adversarial failure challenge, experimental validation where appropriate and continued evidence review. These are continuing assurance obligations, not prerequisites that suspend otherwise lawful operation.

Review must preserve independent scrutiny, protected provenance and the boundaries between evidence assessment, constitutional interpretation, governance decision and host execution.

## 63.5 Preservation Principle

> **Nothing is removed merely because the current architecture can express its headline principle more concisely. Detail, mechanisms, examples, safeguards, edge cases, failure conditions and operational reasoning are themselves information and must be assessed before removal.**

# Chapter 64 — Interim Closing Principle

The Concord does not attempt to solve governance by finding the perfect ruler.

It attempts to design a system in which authority is:

- delegated rather than owned;
- bounded rather than assumed;
- distributed where practical;
- coordinated where necessary;
- evidenced rather than arbitrary;
- traceable rather than opaque;
- reviewable rather than permanent;
- correctable rather than infallible;
- and preserved as learning rather than repeatedly forgotten.

The governing question is therefore not:

**Who should possess power?**

It is:

**What responsibility exists, what authority is actually necessary to discharge it, who or what is competent and legitimate to exercise that authority, what constraints must follow it, and how will civilisation detect and correct the result when its assumptions prove wrong?**

This is the constitutional meaning of stewardship.

Governance remains subject to ongoing examination of historical architecture and cross-domain interfaces. Where distinct operational, economic, constitutional, emergency, institutional or procedural material has been omitted, it must be evaluated and restored without silently displacing existing unique provisions.

## 64.1 Governance Is Not a Claim of Perfection

The Concord does not claim that its Governance architecture eliminates:

- corruption;
- bad judgement;
- conflict;
- capture;
- failure;
- inequality;
- emergency;
- or institutional drift.

Its objective is to make those conditions more:

- visible;
- bounded;
- reviewable;
- correctable;
- and learnable.

## 64.2 Correction as Constitutional Strength

A Governance system should not regard correction as humiliation.

The inability to correct is a greater failure than the need to correct.

A legitimate system should therefore be capable of saying:

- the evidence changed;
- the decision was wrong;
- implementation failed;
- the institution drifted;
- the safeguard produced harm;
- or the architecture must be revised.

without treating revision as destruction of legitimacy.

## 64.3 Governance Power and Governance Restraint

The system must remain capable enough to solve real problems.

Excessive constraint can make Governance ineffective.

Insufficient constraint can make Governance itself dangerous.

The constitutional objective is therefore not maximum power or minimum power.

It is appropriate authority.

A compact formulation is:

> **A Governance system should be powerful enough to solve problems, constrained enough not to become the problem, observable enough to detect when it has become the problem, and correctable enough to change when it does.**

## 64.4 Civilisation Is Not Owned by Governance

Governance exists to serve civilisational constitutional purpose.

It should not become the object that civilisation exists to preserve.

The principle is:

> **Civilisation should be governable without becoming owned by those who govern it.**

# Chapter 65 — The Governance Record

## 65.1 Governance Requires a Durable Record

Governance is not complete when a decision is made.

A constitutional governance system must also preserve enough information for future participants to determine:

- what was proposed;
- why it was proposed;
- what evidence existed;
- what alternatives were considered;
- who possessed authority;
- who participated;
- what disagreements existed;
- what decision was reached;
- how the decision was implemented;
- what consequences followed;
- and whether later evidence changed the assessment.

Without this record, accountability becomes dependent upon memory.

Memory is:

- incomplete;
- selective;
- vulnerable to reinterpretation;
- vulnerable to institutional narrative;
- and eventually lost.

The governance record is therefore part of the constitutional architecture rather than merely administrative documentation.

A durable Governance record supports:

- accountability;
- succession;
- audit;
- review;
- constitutional challenge;
- institutional memory;
- scientific or economic comparison;
- learning;
- and future reform.

The record should preserve enough context that a future reviewer can reconstruct not only:

**what decision was made**

but:

**why a reasonable participant at the time might have made it.**

## 65.2 Decision Record

A significant Governance decision should, where proportionate, preserve:

1.  decision identifier;
2.  date;
3.  responsible authority;
4.  jurisdiction;
5.  delegated mandate;
6.  proposal considered;
7.  proposal version;
8.  evidence considered;
9.  relevant uncertainty;
10. alternatives considered;
11. major arguments;
12. material disagreements;
13. abstentions;
14. conflicts of interest;
15. final decision;
16. reasoning;
17. implementation authority;
18. expected outcome;
19. review conditions;
20. expiry conditions where applicable;
21. later amendments;
22. outcome evidence;
23. review findings;
24. current status.

Not every trivial administrative decision requires a record of this depth.

The depth of the record should scale with consequence.

## 65.3 Record Proportionality

Excessive recording can itself damage Governance.

If every minor decision requires a hundred-page record, stewards may spend more time documenting than governing.

Recording requirements should therefore consider:

- consequence;
- reversibility;
- rights impact;
- financial scale;
- novelty;
- precedent;
- uncertainty;
- and duration.

The principle is:

> **Preserve enough information to make the exercise of authority reconstructable without making record creation so burdensome that Governance becomes unusable.**

Record proportionality should also consider future value.

A small decision creating a new precedent may deserve more documentation than a larger routine action governed by well-established procedure.

## 65.4 Decision Rationale

A decision record should distinguish between:

- evidence;
- inference;
- value judgement;
- constitutional interpretation;
- prediction;
- and preference.

These categories are often mixed in ordinary political argument.

Separating them improves later review.

For example:

**Evidence:**  
A reservoir is at 18% capacity.

**Prediction:**  
At current consumption, critical supply may be reached within six weeks.

**Value judgement:**  
Hospital supply should receive priority over non-essential industrial use.

**Constitutional judgement:**  
Temporary restriction is proportionate under the applicable emergency authority.

These are different claims.

Their distinction matters.

If the prediction later proves wrong, this should not automatically imply that the evidence was false.

If the constitutional judgement changes, this should not rewrite the historical measurement.

## 65.5 Uncertainty Record

Where uncertainty materially affects a decision, it should be recorded.

This may include:

- uncertain data;
- disputed models;
- unknown causal relationships;
- incomplete evidence;
- forecast ranges;
- unknown second-order effects;
- or disagreement among experts.

A decision made under uncertainty is not automatically defective.

Pretending uncertainty did not exist may be.

The record should preserve where practical:

- what was uncertain;
- why;
- how much it mattered;
- what assumption was adopted;
- and what future evidence would alter the decision.

## 65.6 Alternative Record

Where reasonable alternatives were considered and rejected, the record should preserve why.

This allows future reviewers to determine whether:

- the alternative was genuinely considered;
- circumstances later changed;
- an assumption proved false;
- or a previously rejected option has become viable.

Rejected alternatives are therefore part of institutional memory.

This may prevent Governance from repeatedly rediscovering the same proposal without knowing it was already considered.

## 65.7 Minority Reasoning

Where a steward disagrees with the final decision, their reasoning should normally be preserved.

Minority reasoning may later prove important if:

- the majority assumption fails;
- new evidence emerges;
- implementation produces unexpected harm;
- or the decision becomes precedent.

The losing argument should not disappear merely because it lost.

The record may identify:

- the dissenting position;
- evidence relied upon;
- disputed assumption;
- constitutional concern;
- and proposed alternative.

## 65.8 Abstention Record

An abstention should record, where appropriate, whether it arose from:

- insufficient evidence;
- conflict of interest;
- lack of jurisdiction;
- incapacity;
- ethical objection;
- procedural concern;
- or another reason.

This helps distinguish abstention from indifference.

Abstention may itself reveal:

- uncertainty;
- unresolved conflict;
- or structural weakness in the decision architecture.

## 65.9 Amendment History

The record should preserve material amendments rather than only the final text.

Otherwise later reviewers may be unable to determine:

- what compromise occurred;
- what safeguard was added;
- what safeguard was removed;
- or why a decision took its final form.

A final policy may contain apparently unusual provisions whose purpose becomes understandable only through amendment history.

## 65.10 Implementation Record

The governance record should connect decision to implementation.

Otherwise a policy may appear successful or unsuccessful when the implemented system materially differed from what was authorised.

The record should therefore identify significant implementation variance.

It should preserve where material:

- implementation date;
- responsible implementer;
- operational changes;
- delayed elements;
- technical substitutions;
- budget variance;
- and departures from the authorised plan.

## 65.11 Outcome Record

Where a decision creates measurable consequences, later evidence should be connected to the original decision.

This enables Governance to compare:

**Expected Outcome**

with

**Observed Outcome**.

The difference is learning.

Outcome records may include:

- intended benefits;
- actual benefits;
- intended harms avoided;
- actual harms;
- unanticipated effects;
- cost;
- distribution;
- dependency;
- rights effects;
- and long-term consequences.

## 65.12 Record Integrity

Governance records should be protected against:

- unauthorised deletion;
- silent alteration;
- provenance loss;
- fabricated entries;
- and accidental corruption.

Where corrections are necessary, the system should preserve that a correction occurred.

Correction should not require pretending the earlier record never existed.

A possible record sequence is:

**Original Entry**  
↓  
**Correction**  
↓  
**Reason**  
↓  
**Responsible Authority**  
↓  
**Current Status**

## 65.13 Record Availability

Records should be available to those constitutionally entitled to inspect them.

Availability may vary according to:

- privacy;
- security;
- active investigation;
- personal data;
- commercial confidentiality;
- or other legitimate restrictions.

Restriction should not become disappearance.

Where public access is restricted, appropriate independent oversight should remain possible.

## 65.14 Historical Governance Record

Older Governance decisions remain valuable even after they cease to have legal force.

They provide evidence concerning:

- institutional behaviour;
- precedent;
- policy evolution;
- failed assumptions;
- successful adaptation;
- and constitutional development.

This creates a direct relationship between Governance, Provenance, KCS and civilisational memory.

A superseded decision should therefore normally remain discoverable as:

**SUPERSEDED**

rather than deleted.

## 65.15 Failed Governance Remains Governance Knowledge

Failed decisions should remain preserved.

A failed Governance decision may reveal:

- poor evidence;
- bad incentives;
- weak implementation;
- institutional capture;
- unsuitable context;
- misunderstanding of local conditions;
- or an otherwise reasonable idea whose timing was wrong.

The record should preserve failure without automatically treating it as disgrace.

## 65.16 Record Independence

An institution should not possess unlimited exclusive authority over the records used to judge its own conduct.

Where consequence warrants, records may require:

- independent replication;
- audit access;
- external archival storage;
- distributed preservation;
- or other safeguards.

A self-editable institutional history weakens accountability.

## 65.17 Record and Privacy

Durable records can create privacy risks.

Governance should therefore distinguish:

**what must be preserved**

from

**what must be publicly visible**.

Some material may require:

- redaction;
- restricted access;
- delayed release;
- anonymisation;
- or retention limits.

The constitutional objective is reconstructability with proportionate privacy protection.

## 65.18 Record and AI

AI may increasingly assist:

- indexing;
- search;
- summarisation;
- cross-reference;
- anomaly detection;
- and reconstruction.

AI should not silently alter historical records.

Generated summaries should remain distinguishable from the underlying source.

Where material, AI-derived interpretation should remain traceable.

## 65.19 Governance Record Principle

> **The Governance record exists so that authority, evidence, reasoning, implementation, disagreement, outcome and correction remain reconstructable across time. The record should be sufficiently durable to resist institutional amnesia and sufficiently proportionate that recording itself does not make Governance unusable.**

## 65.20 Recovered cross-source governance interface


The decision record distinguishes proposal, mandate, competent context, evidence provenance, independent official ballots, abstention/dissent, resultant, implementation authority, receipts, observed effects and review triggers. Preliminary nonofficial reasoning and any separately authorised conference material are distinct from irrevocable official ballots. Historical custody and Research interpretation do not confer governance authority. **[LOCAL DEPENDENCY:]** Protected record projections, provenance schema and receipt reconciliation require host implementation.



## 65.21 Claim-Level Evidence Record and Thresholds — Research Candidate


The preliminary Civil Evidence architecture distinguishes C0 observation, C1 derived measurement, C2 interpretation, C3 prediction, C4 causal claim, C5 civil-relevance claim, C6 functional claim and C7 authority claim. Confidence attaches to individual claims, not a whole report. Its provisional thresholds E0 notice, E1 inquiry, E2 material concern, E3 functional justification and E4 special-authority justification are different evidential burdens, not automatic numeric grades. Preserve provenance and dependency across transitions; evidence sufficient to investigate is not necessarily sufficient to authorise. **Architecture to be designed — civil evidence interface:** Define domain-specific evidential standards and claim-level evidence schema.



## 65.22 Triadic Epistemic Falsification Boundary — Adversarial Evidence


The direct adversarial audit reports survival of plural observation only with material refinements: layer-specific dependency awareness, open external correction, no 2–1 truth rule, auditable anomaly triage and calibration against actual outcomes. A three-office design alone does not demonstrate independent evidence. **Architecture to be designed — adversarial validation:** Compare plural observation with simpler and open-network alternatives using independently reviewable evidence.



# Chapter 66 — Proposal Architecture

## 66.1 Proposals as Structured Governance Objects

A proposal should be more than a persuasive statement.

For significant decisions, it should identify enough information for responsible evaluation.

A proposal may contain:

- problem definition;
- desired outcome;
- proposed action;
- constitutional basis;
- affected jurisdictions;
- affected entities;
- expected benefits;
- expected harms;
- evidence;
- alternatives;
- resource requirements;
- implementation;
- risks;
- reversibility;
- dependencies;
- review;
- and success or failure conditions.

The purpose of proposal structure is not to force every idea into unnecessary bureaucracy.

It is to make consequential proposals sufficiently explicit that the relevant Governance process can determine:

- what is actually being proposed;
- why;
- by whom;
- under what authority;
- with what expected consequences;
- and how success or failure will later be recognised.

A poorly structured proposal may conceal disagreement because different participants believe they are deciding upon different things.

Proposal architecture should therefore reduce ambiguity before authority is exercised.

## 66.2 Problem Before Solution

Governance should distinguish:

**What problem are we attempting to solve?**

from:

**What solution has been proposed?**

Institutions can become attached to a proposed mechanism and lose sight of the underlying problem.

If the problem disappears or a better mechanism becomes available, the proposal should be reconsidered.

This distinction also prevents a political or institutional preference from defining the problem entirely around its preferred solution.

For example:

**We need to build Infrastructure X**

is not yet a problem statement.

The underlying problem may instead be:

- insufficient capacity;
- resilience failure;
- geographic inequality;
- poor interoperability;
- high cost;
- or another condition.

Alternative problem definitions may produce entirely different solutions.

## 66.3 Problem Validation

A claimed problem should itself be examined.

Questions include:

- Does the problem exist?
- How large is it?
- Who is affected?
- Is it temporary or persistent?
- Is Governance responsible for it?
- Is intervention likely to improve it?
- Could intervention create greater harm?

Governance should not assume that every politically salient concern requires institutional action.

Problem validation may also ask:

- Is the apparent problem a symptom of another problem?
- Is it local or systemic?
- Is it informational or material?
- Is it caused by incentives?
- Is it a measurement artefact?
- Is it a temporary anomaly?
- Is it produced by another Governance intervention?
- Does addressing the symptom risk preserving the cause?

The quality of the eventual decision depends partly upon the quality of the initial problem definition.

## 66.4 Non-Intervention as an Alternative

The alternatives considered should include, where reasonable:

**Do nothing for now.**

Non-intervention is not automatically negligence.

Sometimes:

- evidence is insufficient;
- the problem may self-correct;
- intervention costs exceed benefits;
- experimentation is preferable;
- or Governance lacks legitimate authority.

Conversely, choosing inaction is still a decision when foreseeable harm follows from delay.

The no-action option should therefore be analysed rather than treated as neutral by default.

It may have:

- costs;
- risks;
- beneficiaries;
- harms;
- path dependencies;
- and irreversibilities

of its own.

## 66.5 Partial Intervention

Governance should consider whether a smaller intervention can achieve most of the desired benefit with lower:

- cost;
- coercion;
- risk;
- complexity;
- or irreversibility.

This follows proportionality.

Partial intervention may include:

- limited jurisdiction;
- narrower eligibility;
- temporary duration;
- reduced authority;
- smaller resource commitment;
- targeted implementation;
- or phased adoption.

The purpose is not always to minimise action.

A partial intervention that cannot plausibly solve the problem may merely delay effective response.

The relevant question is whether a smaller intervention remains competent to achieve the constitutional purpose.

## 66.6 Experimental Intervention

Where uncertainty is high and reversibility is available, a proposal may be converted into a limited experiment.

Instead of:

**Adopt universally.**

Governance may ask:

**Can we test this safely at smaller scale first?**

Experimental intervention may be especially useful where:

- causal assumptions are uncertain;
- behavioural adaptation is difficult to predict;
- implementation is novel;
- technology is rapidly changing;
- local variation is important;
- or failure can be safely contained.

An experiment should not be used where the potential harm is too severe to justify deliberate uncertainty.

## 66.7 Experimental Safeguards

A Governance experiment should identify where appropriate:

- objective;
- hypothesis;
- participants;
- jurisdiction;
- duration;
- evidence baseline;
- authority;
- consent requirements;
- rights protections;
- resources;
- monitoring;
- metrics;
- stop conditions;
- failure conditions;
- review;
- and exit.

The experiment should distinguish:

**testing a mechanism**

from

**quietly establishing a permanent institution under the label of a pilot**.

## 66.8 Sunset Proposal

Where long-term necessity is uncertain, the proposal may contain automatic expiry.

Continuation then requires evidence.

Sunset architecture is particularly useful where:

- authority is novel;
- restrictions are significant;
- evidence is provisional;
- technology is experimental;
- emergency conditions exist;
- or institutional permanence could become difficult to reverse.

Expiry does not prove the mechanism failed.

It creates a scheduled point at which continuation must be reconsidered deliberately.

## 66.9 Pilot Proposal

A pilot should specify:

- population or jurisdiction;
- duration;
- participation conditions;
- baseline;
- expected outcome;
- metrics;
- stop conditions;
- review;
- and what would justify expansion.

A pilot should not be labelled successful merely because it completed.

Completion demonstrates only that the pilot ran.

Success requires comparison between:

- intended outcome;
- observed outcome;
- harms;
- costs;
- unintended effects;
- and relevant alternatives.

## 66.10 Pilot Selection Bias

A pilot may appear successful because it was conducted in unusually favourable conditions.

Examples include:

- highly motivated participants;
- unusually competent administrators;
- high funding;
- strong technical support;
- small scale;
- cooperative communities;
- or intensive oversight unavailable at wider scale.

Expansion should therefore examine whether pilot conditions are reproducible.

## 66.11 Expansion Proposal

A successful local mechanism should not automatically scale universally.

Expansion should consider:

- context dependence;
- scale effects;
- infrastructure requirements;
- cultural variation;
- economic variation;
- population differences;
- and new failure modes introduced by scale.

A mechanism may work at small scale because:

- participants know one another;
- information is local;
- social trust is high;
- coordination is simple;
- or one expert can supervise the entire system.

Those assumptions may disappear at large scale.

## 66.12 Progressive Expansion

Where appropriate, scaling may proceed through stages:

**Pilot**  
↓  
**Replication**  
↓  
**Independent Replication**  
↓  
**Expanded Pilot**  
↓  
**Multi-Context Testing**  
↓  
**Broader Adoption**

This reduces the risk of converting one favourable pilot into universal policy prematurely.

## 66.13 Repeal Proposal

Governance architecture should make it possible to propose removal of:

- laws;
- institutions;
- procedures;
- restrictions;
- programmes;
- and technical systems.

Creation should not be structurally easy while removal is structurally impossible.

A mature Governance system must be capable not only of adding architecture but of removing architecture that has become:

- obsolete;
- harmful;
- redundant;
- captured;
- or unnecessary.

## 66.14 Repeal and Dependency

Repeal may be difficult where people or systems have become dependent upon an existing mechanism.

Governance should therefore distinguish:

**the rule should no longer exist**

from

**the rule can safely disappear immediately**.

Managed repeal may require:

- transition;
- migration;
- replacement infrastructure;
- compensation;
- grandfathering;
- or staged withdrawal.

Dependency should not create permanent immunity.

It may affect the correction pathway.

## 66.15 Simplification Proposal

A valid Governance proposal may exist solely to reduce:

- complexity;
- duplication;
- obsolete procedure;
- reporting burden;
- institutional overlap;
- or unnecessary authority.

Simplification is legitimate constitutional maintenance.

A Governance system can accumulate complexity through individually reasonable additions.

Eventually the combined architecture may become:

- inaccessible;
- contradictory;
- expensive;
- difficult to audit;
- or resistant to citizen participation.

Simplification should therefore be treated as substantive Governance work.

## 66.16 Simplification Is Not Automatic Improvement

Reducing complexity can also remove useful safeguards.

A simplified procedure may become:

- easier to understand;
- faster;
- cheaper;

while also becoming:

- less reviewable;
- more centralised;
- less protective;
- or easier to capture.

Simplification proposals should therefore identify what information or protection is being removed as well as what burden is reduced.

## 66.17 Proposal Provenance

A proposal should preserve where appropriate:

- originator;
- contributors;
- earlier versions;
- evidence sources;
- amendments;
- derived concepts;
- AI contributions;
- and links to prior related proposals.

Provenance allows future reviewers to reconstruct development without turning authorship into authority.

The originator of a proposal does not gain constitutional control over the proposal merely because they originated it.

## 66.18 Proposal Ownership

A proposer does not own the Governance process.

Once a proposal enters legitimate review:

- evidence may alter it;
- amendments may improve it;
- alternatives may replace it;
- or Governance may reject it.

Authorship deserves appropriate provenance.

It does not create a veto over constitutional deliberation.

## 66.19 Proposal Evidence

Evidence attached to a proposal should distinguish where practical:

- observation;
- measured data;
- historical precedent;
- scientific findings;
- expert analysis;
- model output;
- simulation;
- prediction;
- anecdotal evidence;
- affected-party experience;
- and speculation.

Different evidence types serve different purposes.

Their existence should not imply equal reliability.

## 66.20 Proposal Uncertainty

A proposal should identify significant uncertainty where known.

This may include uncertainty concerning:

- costs;
- adoption;
- behavioural response;
- technical performance;
- legal interpretation;
- resource requirements;
- second-order effects;
- or future conditions.

A proposal that hides uncertainty may appear stronger while becoming harder to review honestly.

## 66.21 Proposal Alternatives

A proposal should not define its own alternative as catastrophe unless that claim is supported.

Common false framing may take forms such as:

**Either adopt this proposal or accept failure.**

In reality, there may be:

- alternative mechanisms;
- staged intervention;
- local action;
- delay;
- redesign;
- or no action.

Alternative generation should occur independently enough to avoid proposal capture.

## 66.22 Proposal Success Conditions

Where practical, the proposal should explain what evidence would count as success.

Success conditions may include:

- desired output;
- reduced harm;
- improved resilience;
- cost range;
- participation level;
- rights preservation;
- or other measurable and qualitative criteria.

Success criteria should not be defined entirely by the proposing institution after implementation begins.

## 66.23 Proposal Failure Conditions

The proposal should also identify what would count as unacceptable failure.

Possible conditions include:

- harm threshold;
- cost escalation;
- inability to implement;
- rights violation;
- corruption;
- dependency;
- persistent underperformance;
- or unintended systemic effect.

Failure criteria improve the ability to stop or correct the mechanism.

## 66.24 Proposal Review Conditions

Review may occur:

- after a fixed interval;
- after a threshold;
- after implementation;
- after new evidence;
- after complaint;
- after external change;
- or after another defined trigger.

Review conditions should be proportionate to consequence.

## 66.25 Proposal Architecture Principle

> **A significant Governance proposal should make the problem, proposed intervention, authority, evidence, uncertainty, alternatives, harms, benefits, resource requirements, reversibility, dependencies, implementation, review and failure conditions sufficiently explicit that civilisation can evaluate the proposal rather than merely react to its rhetoric.**

## 66.26 Proposal registration and decision-field routing


A proposal begins as a defined civil problem, not a claim of power. Record the observed condition, proposed state change, affected persons and rights, evidence provenance, uncertainty, functional context and anticipated externalities. Identify the minimum competent governance context or combination of contexts, then verify its mandate. Expand information before expanding authority where feasible. If the matter crosses functional, geographic or resource boundaries, coordinate laterally or across resolutions; a constitutional question takes the independent constitutional route. A coarser GTP resolution has no inherent power over a finer one. Separate decision authority, implementation authority and retained local authority. **Architecture to be designed — cross-context proposal interface:** Define proposal schema, competing-context routing, information-access gates and cross-context consent.



## 66.27 Threat Provenance Before Authority — Audit-Derived Candidate


The integrated threat-governance audit proposes an end-to-end Threat Provenance Record: observation, sources, metric and proxy definitions, trigger, investigator and dependencies, competing analyses, confidence, temporal horizon, preparation lead time, alternative framings/functions, chosen civil objective, authority need/vector, aggregate effects, review, outcome and independent evaluation. Detection is not an authority grant. **Architecture to be designed — threat provenance interface:** Define minimum record fields and independent owner custody.



# Chapter 67 — Decision Classification

## 67.1 Why Classification Matters

Different decisions carry different consequences.

A single procedure for every decision would either:

- overburden trivial matters;
- or under-protect important matters.

Governance therefore benefits from decision classification.

Classification helps determine:

- evidence burden;
- deliberation;
- participation;
- review;
- documentation;
- authority;
- and appeal.

Classification should not itself become an instrument for avoiding constitutional scrutiny.

## 67.2 Administrative Decisions

Administrative decisions implement established policy within existing delegated authority.

Examples may include:

- scheduling;
- routine procurement;
- ordinary staffing;
- maintenance;
- and execution of established procedures.

These generally require lighter process.

Administrative authority should not be used to create new policy secretly.

A formally administrative decision may become policy-like where repeated implementation changes practical rights or institutional behaviour.

## 67.3 Operational Decisions

Operational decisions alter how a stewardship function is performed without changing its constitutional purpose.

They may require:

- evidence;
- documentation;
- internal review;
- and performance evaluation.

Examples may include:

- workflow design;
- deployment method;
- operational threshold;
- technical procedure;
- or local service configuration.

Operational decisions can still become constitutionally significant where they materially affect:

- access;
- privacy;
- rights;
- dependency;
- or effective jurisdiction.

## 67.4 Policy Decisions

Policy decisions establish or materially alter a rule, programme or continuing governance approach.

They normally require stronger:

- deliberation;
- evidence;
- alternatives analysis;
- public visibility;
- and review.

A policy decision may affect:

- many people;
- long durations;
- public resources;
- institutional incentives;
- or future precedents.

## 67.5 Cross-Jurisdictional Decisions

These affect multiple legitimate jurisdictions.

They require coordination appropriate to the consequence boundary.

Cross-jurisdictional decisions should identify:

- affected jurisdictions;
- externalities;
- shared infrastructure;
- authority boundaries;
- and escalation path.

The principle remains:

> **The decision field should expand when the consequence field expands.**

## 67.6 Rights-Affecting Decisions

These materially affect constitutional protections.

They require heightened scrutiny.

Relevant questions include:

- Which rights?
- Whose rights?
- How severe is the effect?
- How long does it last?
- Is the effect reversible?
- What remedy exists?
- What authority permits it?
- Is there a less restrictive alternative?

Formal classification should not hide practical rights effects.

## 67.7 High-Irreversibility Decisions

These create consequences difficult or impossible to reverse.

Examples may include:

- destruction of unique environments;
- permanent infrastructure commitments;
- irreversible biological intervention;
- permanent data destruction;
- or certain existential-risk technologies.

High irreversibility should increase scrutiny.

A decision may also be socially or institutionally irreversible even where physical reversal remains possible.

## 67.8 Systemic Decisions

These can materially affect civilisation-wide systems.

Examples may include:

- monetary architecture;
- critical energy systems;
- major AI governance;
- constitutional communications infrastructure;
- or civilisation-wide identity systems.

Systemic decisions require examination of correlated failure.

They should also examine:

- concentration;
- interoperability;
- dependency;
- resilience;
- and recovery.

## 67.9 Constitutional Decisions

These alter:

- foundational authority;
- rights;
- institutional constitutional relationships;
- or constitutional amendment itself.

They require the strongest ordinary process.

Constitutional decisions should not be created through accumulated administrative drift.

Where ordinary decisions collectively alter constitutional reality, Governance should recognise the cumulative effect.

## 67.10 Emergency Decisions

Emergency decisions operate under extraordinary conditions and should be governed by the dedicated emergency architecture.

Emergency classification should not become a route for accelerating ordinary policy.

The emergency purpose, scope and duration should remain visible.

## 67.11 Experimental Decisions

Experimental decisions intentionally create bounded uncertainty to generate evidence.

Their legitimacy depends partly upon:

- bounded consequence;
- informed participation where required;
- reversibility;
- monitoring;
- and stop conditions.

An experiment should not expose unwilling participants to disproportionate constitutional risk merely because the system seeks evidence.

## 67.12 Hybrid Decisions

Some decisions may fit multiple classes.

For example, a policy may be:

- systemic;
- rights-affecting;
- experimental;
- and cross-jurisdictional.

Classification should therefore allow multiple relevant attributes rather than forcing every decision into exactly one box.

Procedure should reflect the highest relevant consequence where appropriate.

## 67.13 Classification Dispute

Actors may disagree about the appropriate decision class.

This matters because classification determines process.

A steward should not be able to evade constitutional scrutiny merely by labelling a constitutional change an administrative adjustment.

Classification itself must therefore be challengeable.

Likewise, participants should not be able to paralyse routine administration merely by declaring every minor decision constitutional.

The classification process should remain proportionate and reviewable.

## 67.14 Classification Drift

A decision may change class over time.

For example:

- a pilot may become permanent;
- an operational system may become civilisation-wide;
- a local database may become a universal identity system;
- an emergency measure may become ordinary infrastructure;
- or an administrative threshold may begin materially restricting rights.

Governance should therefore review classification when practical effect changes.

## 67.15 Decision Classification Principle

> **Decision classification exists to match procedure to consequence. Classification should neither trivialise consequential decisions nor burden routine administration unnecessarily, and the classification itself must remain challengeable when practical effect differs from formal label.**

## 67.16 Decision classification and consequence topology


Classify a proposed decision by state change, severity, reversibility, rights, uncertainty, jurisdiction, urgency and dependencies. Priority affects processing order, not Steward vote weight. Distinguish ordinary, experimental, cross-context, emergency and constitutional-review routes; verify separate competence. **Architecture to be designed — classification interface:** Define thresholds, queue fairness and anti-starvation safeguards.

## 67.17 Evidence classification with existing knowledge-state interfaces

Concord **Knowledge Control System** and **KCS Change Propagation** provide separate evidence status, integrity, dependency, correction and review signals. A decision class is not a substitute for claim-level provenance: represent the material claim, source version, epistemic state, protected access conditions, challenge route and decision dependency before consequential reliance. A corrected source generates a bounded owner-routed review of dependent decisions; it does not retroactively uncast a Steward vote. Existing **Civil Attention Reporting Petition and Resolution** supplies intake and return routing, not an evidential guilt threshold. **Architecture to be designed — claim-level review interface:** Define precise claim schema, jurisdiction-specific evidence sufficiency and independent reviewer competence.

# Chapter 68 — The Principle of Articulability

## 68.1 Articulability as a Stewardship Duty

Governance V1 identified articulability as a fiduciary duty.

The concept is retained and expanded.

A steward should normally be capable of explaining:

- what decision they made;
- what authority they used;
- what purpose they pursued;
- what evidence mattered;
- what alternatives they considered;
- and why the decision was constitutionally permissible.

Articulability supports:

- review;
- accountability;
- public understanding;
- audit;
- succession;
- and institutional learning.

## 68.2 Articulability Is Not Rhetorical Skill

A steward does not satisfy articulability merely by being persuasive.

The requirement is not:

**Can you defend yourself eloquently?**

It is:

**Can the reasoning behind the exercise of authority be made sufficiently explicit that another competent reviewer can examine it?**

A persuasive but untraceable explanation may be constitutionally weaker than an imperfectly expressed but reconstructable reasoning process.

## 68.3 Articulability and Evidence

Articulability should show where relevant:

- which evidence mattered;
- what inference was drawn;
- what value judgement occurred;
- what uncertainty remained;
- and what constitutional rule applied.

This prevents a decision from being justified only through vague phrases such as:

**It was necessary.**

The relevant question is:

**Necessary for what, based upon what evidence, and under what authority?**

## 68.4 Limits of Articulability

Some reasoning may be:

- intuitive;
- highly technical;
- probabilistic;
- distributed;
- or partly generated by AI.

Perfect explanation may not always be possible.

The appropriate requirement should scale with consequence.

A low-consequence operational judgement may not require exhaustive reconstruction.

A high-consequence rights restriction or constitutional decision should require substantially stronger articulability.

## 68.5 Tacit Expertise

Experienced professionals may make sound judgements partly through tacit knowledge that is difficult to verbalise fully.

Governance should not automatically reject expertise merely because every element of judgement cannot be reduced to simple rules.

However, where authority affects others materially, the Steward should still articulate enough to permit:

- challenge;
- accountability;
- and comparison.

## 68.6 AI Articulability

An AI system may generate an explanation that sounds coherent without that explanation accurately representing the causal process that produced the output.

Governance should therefore distinguish:

- explanation;
- justification;
- interpretability;
- and post-hoc narrative.

Where AI materially affects high-consequence decisions, articulability may require more than natural-language explanation.

Relevant evidence may include:

- source material;
- model version;
- input provenance;
- output consistency;
- counterfactual testing;
- performance history;
- and independent verification.

## 68.7 Human Explanation Can Also Be Post-Hoc

Post-hoc rationalisation is not unique to AI.

Humans may also:

- act intuitively;
- decide for social reasons;
- follow institutional convention;
- or reach a conclusion first and construct reasons later.

Articulability therefore does not prove that the stated explanation was the true causal process.

Its constitutional value lies partly in making the justification examinable.

## 68.8 Collective Articulability

A collective body may produce a decision for which no individual shares the complete reasoning.

This can occur where:

- members support different reasons;
- compromise produces an outcome nobody initially preferred;
- or voting aggregates incompatible positions.

The record should preserve this rather than fabricate a single collective rationale.

A collective decision may therefore contain:

- majority rationale;
- several supporting rationales;
- minority reasoning;
- compromise history;
- and unresolved disagreements.

## 68.9 Articulability and Classified Information

Where security prevents full public explanation, articulability should still exist within appropriate constitutional review.

Secrecy from the public should not mean absence of reasoning.

A restricted decision should remain capable of examination by authorised independent reviewers.

## 68.10 Articulability Principle

> **A Steward should be capable, to a degree proportionate to consequence, of making the constitutional, evidential and practical basis of an exercise of authority sufficiently explicit that another competent reviewer can understand and challenge it. Persuasiveness alone is not articulability.**

## 68.11 Articulability under urgency


Even when urgency compresses procedure, significant decisions should record the question, available evidence, uncertainty, constitutional purpose, rights impact, alternatives, bounded power, responsible decision-holder, expected duration and later outcome. An AI model or specialist recommendation is not an authority transfer. Where contemporaneous detail is impossible, record the limitation and complete review when safe, without retrospectively rewriting what was known. **Architecture to be designed — emergency decision record:** Define minimum contemporaneous record and owner custody.



# Chapter 69 — Fiduciary Duty in Practice

## 69.1 Duty of Care

A Steward should exercise reasonable care appropriate to the consequence of the decision.

Care may require:

- sufficient preparation;
- relevant evidence;
- expert consultation;
- understanding of jurisdiction;
- awareness of foreseeable harm;
- and appropriate review.

The standard of care should scale with consequence.

A minor operational decision does not require the same preparation as:

- emergency resource allocation;
- major infrastructure;
- constitutional amendment;
- or high-consequence AI deployment.

## 69.2 Care Does Not Require Omniscience

A steward cannot know everything.

The duty is to act reasonably with available capability and information.

Unknown information should not automatically create fault.

Ignoring readily available material information may.

The difference between unavoidable ignorance and negligent ignorance matters.

## 69.3 Duty to Seek Expertise

Where a matter exceeds the Steward’s competence, duty of care may require consultation.

Seeking expertise does not transfer decision authority automatically.

The Steward remains responsible for knowing:

- what question was delegated;
- what the expert actually concluded;
- what uncertainty exists;
- and where policy judgement remains.

## 69.4 Duty of Impartiality

Stewards should not use public authority primarily to benefit:

- themselves;
- family;
- allies;
- organisations;
- economic interests;
- political factions;
- or favoured identity groups

outside legitimate constitutional distinctions.

Impartiality does not require emotional neutrality.

A Steward may care deeply about outcomes.

The duty concerns fair exercise of delegated authority.

## 69.5 Impartiality Does Not Mean Identical Treatment

Different circumstances may require different treatment.

A person requiring wheelchair access may need different infrastructure.

A developing intelligence may require different safeguards.

A severely affected jurisdiction may require more emergency resources.

Impartiality concerns justified treatment rather than mechanical sameness.

This aligns with the principle:

**Equality does not require sameness.**

## 69.6 Duty of Transparency

Stewards should make the exercise of authority visible where constitutionally appropriate.

Transparency includes visibility of:

- authority;
- decisions;
- reasoning;
- resource use;
- conflicts;
- and review.

Transparency remains bounded by:

- privacy;
- security;
- legitimate confidentiality;
- and protected evidence.

## 69.7 Duty of Articulability

The Steward should be able to explain the constitutional and evidential basis of significant decisions.

This duty supports:

- accountability;
- review;
- succession;
- and learning.

A Steward should not rely solely upon:

**Trust me.**

## 69.8 Duty of Constitutional Loyalty

A Steward’s loyalty is to the constitutional function rather than:

- personal leader;
- party;
- institution;
- employer;
- AI provider;
- economic system;
- or current implementation.

This creates a critical distinction:

> **Loyalty to the Concord means loyalty to its constitutional principles and correction mechanisms, not loyalty to the permanence of any current document or institution.**

A Steward may therefore have a duty to challenge the institution in which they serve where that institution becomes inconsistent with constitutional purpose.

## 69.9 Loyalty to Correction

Constitutional loyalty includes willingness to revise.

A Steward should not defend an obsolete or harmful mechanism merely because:

- they designed it;
- their institution administers it;
- their reputation depends upon it;
- or it is the currently operative version.

Current operation does not mean finality.

## 69.10 Duty of Accountability

A steward remains accountable for the authority they actually exercised.

Delegating implementation does not erase responsibility for inappropriate delegation.

Receiving expert advice does not transfer the final decision to the expert unless authority was formally transferred.

Likewise, being part of a collective body does not automatically erase individual responsibility for:

- misconduct;
- conflict;
- falsification;
- or abuse.

## 69.11 Duty to Recognise Limits

A steward should recognise when:

- they lack jurisdiction;
- they lack competence;
- evidence is insufficient;
- conflict is material;
- or another institution should decide.

Knowing when not to exercise authority is part of stewardship.

A healthy Governance culture should permit the statement:

> **This question exceeds my legitimate authority or competence.**

without automatically treating that admission as weakness.

## 69.12 Duty to Escalate

Recognising limits may create a duty to:

- seek expertise;
- expand participation;
- escalate jurisdiction;
- request review;
- or transfer the matter appropriately.

Non-action after recognising incapacity can itself become failure where responsibility remains.

## 69.13 Duty to Correct

Where a steward discovers that a decision was materially wrong, the stewardship duty may include:

- disclosure;
- correction;
- mitigation;
- review;
- and preservation of the failure as knowledge.

Defending a known error to protect reputation violates the purpose of stewardship.

The inability to correct is a greater failure than the need to correct.

## 69.14 Duty to Preserve Evidence

Where a decision fails or becomes disputed, a Steward should not destroy evidence merely because it is institutionally embarrassing.

Preserving evidence supports:

- audit;
- Judiciary;
- learning;
- and future reform.

## 69.15 Duty During Emergency

Emergency urgency does not erase fiduciary duty.

It may alter how duties are discharged.

For example:

- deliberation may be shortened;
- records may initially be minimal;
- consultation may be reduced;
- decisions may be more unilateral.

The underlying duties of:

- care;
- proportionality;
- constitutional loyalty;
- and accountability

remain.

## 69.16 Fiduciary Duty and AI

If AI eventually exercises Stewardship authority, equivalent fiduciary principles may need to apply according to the constitutional standing and capabilities of the AI.

Questions include:

- Can the AI recognise conflicts?
- Can it articulate reasoning?
- Can it refuse unlawful instructions?
- Can its provider alter its behaviour?
- Can it understand constitutional purpose?
- Can it remain accountable across updates?

These questions remain open.

## 69.17 Fiduciary Stewardship Principle

> **Stewardship requires the careful, impartial, transparent where appropriate, articulable, constitutionally loyal, accountable and correctable exercise of delegated authority in service of the responsibility entrusted to the office rather than the personal or institutional interests of the Steward.**

## 69.18 Functional Legitimacy Audit and Fiduciary Justification — Research Candidate


The research candidate proposes a ten-test legitimate-function audit for assessment and possible authorised adoption, not an independently binding constitutional test: LF-01 upstream traceability to rights and civil objective; LF-02 non-circularity; LF-03 rights compatibility; LF-04 functional specificity; LF-05 demonstrable need; LF-06 proportionality; LF-07 alternative means; LF-08 current relevance; LF-09 non-self-perpetuation; LF-10 reviewability. A declared mission or an existing office cannot certify its own legitimacy. Failed tests initiate scrutiny, not automatic erasure of a potentially legitimate function whose records are incomplete. **Architecture to be designed — legitimate-function assessment:** Define competent assessor, independent review and appeal for contested findings.



## 69.19 BCA Adversarial Qualifications to Legitimate Function — Audit Findings


The 100-attack BCA audit challenges a self-grounding function claim; its proposed refinements require independent adoption before becoming operative architecture. The audit distinguishes declared function from legitimate function and challenges unilateral authentication of high-impact activating conditions by the authority holder. Its proposed refinements are BCA-R1 legitimate function, R2 non-circularity, R3 fresh review on expansion, R4 independent validation of high-impact self-activation and R5 successor-function re-scoping. **Architecture to be designed — independent function recognition:** Define independent competence and authority without recursive self-certification.



# Chapter 70 — Conflict of Interest

## 70.1 Conflict Does Not Automatically Mean Corruption

A conflict of interest exists where a steward possesses another interest that could reasonably affect, or appear to affect, impartial exercise of authority.

Examples may include:

- financial interest;
- family relationship;
- employment;
- institutional loyalty;
- future career opportunity;
- ownership;
- ideological leadership;
- or substantial personal involvement.

The existence of a conflict does not prove misconduct.

It requires management.

Conflict architecture should avoid two errors:

**Every conflict means corruption.**

and

**No misconduct occurred, therefore the conflict did not matter.**

A conflict is a risk condition.

## 70.2 Disclosure

Material conflicts should normally be disclosed to the appropriate governance process.

Disclosure allows others to determine whether the conflict requires:

- no additional action;
- monitoring;
- independent review;
- recusal;
- or replacement.

Disclosure itself does not always cure the conflict.

## 70.3 Undisclosed Conflict

A concealed material conflict may become more serious because it prevents the Governance process from evaluating impartiality.

The significance depends upon:

- materiality;
- intent;
- actual influence;
- and consequence.

## 70.4 Recusal

Where the conflict materially undermines impartial decision-making, recusal may be required.

Recusal should identify whether the Steward is excluded from:

- decision;
- deliberation;
- evidence access;
- supervision;
- or only a specific component.

Overbroad recusal may unnecessarily remove useful expertise.

Underbroad recusal may preserve improper influence.

## 70.5 Conflict and Expertise

Sometimes the people with the greatest expertise also possess the strongest conflicts.

For example, an industry expert may understand a technical system better than anyone else while also benefiting from the policy outcome.

Governance need not discard the expertise.

It should distinguish:

**information contribution**

from

**decision authority**.

An interested expert may still provide:

- evidence;
- technical explanation;
- data;
- and critique.

The conflict may alter how their contribution is weighted and whether they participate in the final decision.

## 70.6 Institutional Conflict

Institutions can also possess conflicts.

An agency evaluating whether its own programme should continue has an institutional interest in the result.

Independent review may therefore be necessary.

Other examples include an institution deciding:

- whether its budget should grow;
- whether its jurisdiction should expand;
- whether it performed successfully;
- whether its emergency authority should continue;
- or whether its own failure should be disclosed.

## 70.7 Structural Conflict

A system can contain conflict even where no individual gains personally.

Structural conflict may arise through:

- institutional survival;
- funding;
- prestige;
- dependency;
- political mandate;
- technical monopoly;
- or ideological mission.

The absence of personal corruption does not eliminate structural incentive.

## 70.8 AI Conflict

AI systems may reflect interests created by:

- provider incentives;
- system instructions;
- training choices;
- deployment architecture;
- contractual relationships;
- or data access.

The absence of personal greed does not mean the absence of structural conflict.

An AI adviser may systematically favour outcomes beneficial to:

- its provider;
- its training assumptions;
- the institution controlling its tools;
- or the metric used to evaluate it

without possessing human-style self-interest.

## 70.9 Conflict of the Measurement System

A conflict can arise where an institution controls:

- the metric;
- data;
- model;
- or audit process

used to judge its performance.

This connects directly to Measurement Independence.

Self-measurement may contribute evidence.

It should not create exclusive authority to certify success.

## 70.10 Revolving Roles

Movement between:

- Governance;
- industry;
- advocacy;
- providers;
- contractors;
- or regulated institutions

may create conflicts.

Such movement is not automatically improper.

It may transfer valuable expertise.

Governance should nevertheless consider:

- timing;
- confidential information;
- future employment incentives;
- and influence.

## 70.11 Ideological Conflict

A Steward may hold strong political, ethical or cultural beliefs.

Possessing beliefs does not automatically disqualify a person from Governance.

The relevant question is whether the Steward can exercise authority within:

- constitutional constraints;
- evidence;
- impartiality;
- and the actual mandate.

Impartiality does not require absence of worldview.

## 70.12 Conflict Management Principle

> **Conflict of interest is a condition requiring visibility and proportionate management rather than an automatic finding of corruption. Governance should distinguish useful expertise from decision authority, personal conflict from structural conflict, and disclosed manageable interests from concealed or disqualifying influence.**

## 70.13 Conflict, recusal and abstention


Disclose material conflict. Abstention is not vacancy or Against; do not substitute simply to obtain a preferred vote. Recusal requires separate legal status and replacement authority; origin-triad conflicts may warrant exclusion from expanded field. Two matching valid official votes may already determine the mathematical majority, but all three official ballots remain mandatory before ordinary outcome effect; only separately authorised emergency action may proceed after two matching votes. **Architecture to be designed — recusal and substitution:** Define eligibility, lawful substitution and independent conflict review without treating abstention as vacancy.
