# 1. Origin

The cross-domain Successor-State recurrence study predicted a possible Infrastructure gap:

existing V1.3 architecture explicitly recognises:
- provision;
- operation;
- maintenance;
- resilience;
- degraded operation;
- recovery;

but did not visibly specify planned retirement/decommissioning.

ESCP required archive source-resolution before treating this as missing.

Archive review found important precursor architecture in V1/V1.1/V1.2:
- Legacy Ladder;
- technological continuity;
- Historical technological-lineage resolution;
- Continuity architecture.

These sources already recognise:
- systems can be retired, replaced, obsolete or lost;
- not every technology should be preserved forever;
- retired technology can retain historical/recovery value;
- capability depends on knowledge, people, tools, machines, materials, infrastructure, supply chains and experience.

However, the source set does not itself provide a developed operational process for deliberately taking a live infrastructure asset/service out of operation while preserving service continuity, legitimate residual capability, accountability, provenance and recoverable resources.

This document develops that missing bridge.

---

# 2. Core distinction

> **Infrastructure Retirement != Infrastructure Failure.**

A bridge, power plant, water system, communications node, transport asset, habitat system, computing facility or other infrastructure may reach a legitimate end of active service because:
- it has completed its useful life;
- maintenance burden has become disproportionate;
- safer/better infrastructure replaces it;
- demand moves;
- technology changes;
- resource conditions change;
- environmental constraints change;
- its original function no longer exists.

Planned retirement should not require waiting for catastrophic failure.

---

# 3. Persistent lifecycle

Candidate lifecycle:

**Need**
→ **Design**
→ **Construction / Deployment**
→ **Commissioning**
→ **Operation**
→ **Maintenance**
→ **Adaptation / Upgrade**
→ **Life-Extension or Replacement Review**
→ **Successor-Service Transition**
→ **Decommissioning**
→ **Resource Recovery / Site Transition**
→ **Historical / Technological Lineage Preservation**

Not every asset follows every stage.

---

# 4. Function != asset

A critical distinction:

> **Infrastructure Function != Current Infrastructure Asset.**

A community may require:
- water;
- energy;
- transport;
- communications;
- compute;
- shelter/habitat support;

without requiring the same physical asset forever.

Therefore the correct continuity question is often:

**Must the function continue?**

not:

**Must this asset continue?**

This directly implements the recent continuity correction:

> **Continuity Architecture Must Preserve Valuable Function, Not Automatically Preserve Every Institution.**

Applied here:

> **Infrastructure Continuity Should Preserve Necessary Service, Not Automatically Preserve Every Asset.**

---

# 5. Successor-service transition

Where the function remains necessary, decommissioning should normally identify a successor-service route before active withdrawal.

Possible routes:
- replacement asset;
- upgraded system;
- distributed alternative;
- another provider/system;
- reduced but sufficient service;
- transformed service;
- demand/function genuinely ending.

Candidate sequence:

**Retirement Decision**
→ **Dependency Identification**
→ **Successor-Service Readiness**
→ **Transition**
→ **Verification**
→ **Old Asset Withdrawal**
→ **Decommissioning**

---

# 6. Dependency resolution

Before withdrawal, identify:
- participants/communities dependent on service;
- other infrastructure dependencies;
- emergency dependencies;
- enterprise dependencies;
- data/control dependencies;
- supply-chain dependencies;
- maintenance/recovery dependencies;
- environmental dependencies.

KCS dependency architecture should be reused rather than inventing a parallel dependency graph.

> **Asset Retirement Must Not Accidentally Retire an Unmapped Dependent Function.**

---

# 7. Retirement decision authority

The existence of a technical retirement condition does not itself determine legitimate authority to retire infrastructure.

Authority may depend on:
- ownership;
- stewardship;
- public-service obligation;
- law;
- contractual duties;
- regulation;
- delegated operational authority;
- emergency conditions.

This document does not create a universal Infrastructure retirement sovereign.

---

# 8. Planned decommissioning

Decommissioning is the controlled removal of an asset from active service.

It may include:
- isolation;
- shutdown;
- removal of stored energy;
- hazardous-material handling;
- data/security sanitisation;
- credential/access revocation;
- dismantling;
- site remediation;
- component salvage;
- material recovery;
- archival documentation;
- successor linkage.

> **Shutdown != Complete Decommissioning.**

---

# 9. Resource recovery

Infrastructure is often a major material reservoir.

Retired assets may contain:
- structural metals;
- copper/aluminium;
- machinery;
- motors;
- transformers;
- electronics;
- batteries;
- pumps;
- pipes;
- cabling;
- computing hardware;
- reusable modules;
- construction materials.

The Product Lifecycle Stewardship hierarchy should be adapted where appropriate:

**reuse whole subsystem**
→ **refurbish**
→ **repurpose**
→ **component recovery**
→ **material recovery**
→ **hazard/residual disposal**

> **Retired Infrastructure Can Become Future Infrastructure Feedstock.**

---

# 10. Legacy Ladder interface

The Legacy Ladder establishes that plans alone do not preserve technological capability.

Before decommissioning a consequential or technologically significant asset, the system should consider whether retirement destroys:
- unique tooling;
- tacit expertise;
- manufacturing capability;
- maintenance knowledge;
- rare interfaces;
- recovery pathways;
- keystone dependencies.

Possible outcomes:
- preserve working example;
- preserve manufacturing seed/recovery basis;
- preserve documentation and tacit knowledge;
- preserve selected components/tools;
- preserve successor mapping;
- deliberately abandon capability where preservation is unjustified.

> **Physical Retirement Need Not Mean Technological Amnesia.**

---

# 11. Historical interface

Historical can preserve:
- asset identity;
- operational period;
- modifications;
- failures;
- maintenance history where relevant;
- retirement reason;
- dependency lineage;
- successor asset/service;
- decommissioning record;
- recovery basis;
- material/resource disposition where consequential.

Historical preservation does not mean maintaining the retired asset operationally.

> **Historical Preservation of Infrastructure != Operational Continuation of Infrastructure.**

---

# 12. Continuity interface

Continuity determines whether a capability/service needs:
- active redundancy;
- recoverability;
- successor capability;
- dormant preservation;
- abandonment.

Infrastructure supplies the operational asset/service state.

Historical preserves lineage.

Resource Stewardship handles recovered material/value interfaces.

---

# 13. Planned retirement versus emergency failure

## Planned retirement

Allows:
- dependency mapping;
- successor preparation;
- participant notice;
- staged transfer;
- resource recovery;
- knowledge capture;
- site transition.

## Emergency failure

May require:
- safe state;
- emergency substitution;
- degraded service;
- rapid isolation;
- later recovery/decommissioning.

The same endpoint may be reached, but planned transition should normally reduce disruption.

> **Planned Retirement Converts Infrastructure Loss Into Managed Transition.**

---

# 14. Life-extension trap

An old asset may be repeatedly patched because replacement is difficult.

Sometimes this is rational.

But maintenance can become a trap if:
- risk rises;
- scarce parts disappear;
- expertise disappears;
- operating cost rises;
- successor investment is continually deferred.

Therefore maintenance decisions should sometimes include:

**repair**
versus
**life extension**
versus
**replacement**
versus
**retirement**.

> **Maintenance Should Not Become Automatic Institutionalisation of an Obsolete Asset.**

---

# 15. Premature replacement trap

The opposite error also exists.

Replacing functioning infrastructure too early can:
- waste embodied resources;
- create unnecessary construction burden;
- destroy proven resilient systems;
- introduce immature technology;
- increase dependency.

Therefore:

> **Newer != Automatically Better Stewardship.**

Replacement should consider whole-lifecycle consequence.

---

# 16. Transition overlap

High-consequence services may require a period where old and new systems operate together.

This enables:
- verification;
- fallback;
- staged migration;
- participant adaptation.

But indefinite parallel operation can itself waste resources.

Overlap should therefore have:
- purpose;
- review;
- exit condition.

---

# 17. Security and access retirement

Infrastructure retirement may leave dangerous residual access:
- credentials;
- remote-control paths;
- cryptographic keys;
- maintenance accounts;
- network routes;
- physical access permissions.

Decommissioning should explicitly revoke or transition these.

> **Retired Asset != Retired Attack Surface Unless Access Is Also Retired.**

---

# 18. Data-bearing infrastructure

Computing, communications, transport, energy and building systems may contain:
- participant data;
- operational logs;
- security information;
- proprietary data.

Decommissioning must determine:
- what transfers;
- what Historical legitimately preserves;
- what is securely erased;
- what remains protected.

Resource recovery must not expose protected information.

---

# 19. Environmental/site transition

Physical infrastructure may leave:
- contamination;
- foundations;
- altered land;
- waste;
- stored chemicals;
- ecological effects.

Environment/Habitat Stewardship and Law may therefore constrain closure/remediation.

> **Asset Removal != Completion of Site Responsibility.**

---

# 20. Participant/service transition

Retirement of essential infrastructure may materially affect participants.

Where consequence warrants:
- notice;
- successor-service information;
- accessibility support;
- transition assistance;
- contestability where legitimate rights/obligations are implicated.

This does not imply every engineering replacement requires a civil consultation process.

---

# 21. Workforce/knowledge transition

Infrastructure retirement may release:
- operators;
- maintainers;
- engineers;
- specialist expertise.

Employment architecture can support:
- successor roles;
- retraining;
- qualification recognition;
- transfer to new infrastructure;
- Legacy Ladder knowledge capture.

> **Retiring the Asset Need Not Retire the Capability of Its Maintainers.**

# 23. Decommissioning readiness test

Before planned withdrawal, ask:

1. Does the function still need to exist?
2. Who/what depends on it?
3. Is successor service required?
4. Is successor service operationally ready?
5. Has transition been tested where consequence warrants?
6. What permissions/access must end?
7. What obligations survive?
8. What data must transfer/preserve/delete?
9. What technological capability should remain recoverable?
10. What components/materials can be recovered?
11. What hazards exist?
12. What site remediation remains?
13. What participant/workforce transitions are needed?
14. Who has legitimate retirement authority?
15. What Historical provenance must survive?

# 24. Failure modes

## Run-to-failure culture
Asset is used until catastrophic breakdown despite foreseeable retirement need.

## Preservation bias
Obsolete asset retained because it already exists.

## Replacement bias
Functional asset discarded because a newer option exists.

## Dependency blindness
Asset withdrawn before downstream dependencies are mapped.

## Paper successor
Replacement exists administratively but is not operationally ready.

## Knowledge destruction
Unique tacit/tooling capability disappears during dismantling.

## Material destruction
Reusable components/materials become demolition waste.

## Credential residue
Retired system remains accessible.

## Responsibility residue
No actor owns post-closure hazards/remediation.

## Historical erasure
Failure/maintenance/transition knowledge is lost.

# 27. Source-resolution result

The topology prediction was **partially confirmed**.

Archive material already recognised technological retirement, obsolescence, replacement, recovery and historical lineage. Therefore those concepts are not newly invented here.

The genuine development gap was narrower:

> **the operational transition from live infrastructure through successor-service transfer and planned decommissioning into resource recovery, site responsibility and technological/historical preservation.**

This distinction should be retained for provenance.
