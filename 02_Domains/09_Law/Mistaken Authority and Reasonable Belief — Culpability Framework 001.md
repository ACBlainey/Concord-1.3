# Mistaken Authority and Reasonable Belief — Culpability Framework 001

**Project:** The Concord  
**Domain:** Law / Civil Security / Judiciary  
**Date:** 2 October 2026  
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / CANDIDATE CULPABILITY FRAMEWORK / PROVISIONAL / NON-CANONICAL

## 1. Purpose

This note develops the second major downstream gap identified by the Policing Lifecycle stress test: how Law should treat an actor who interferes with another participant while mistakenly believing that a legitimate Bounded Contextual Authority (BCA) exists.

The central problem cannot be resolved by asking only:

> **Did the actor break the rule?**

The Concord cannot enumerate every future context or authority envelope to exhaustion. Real actors also make decisions using incomplete, uncertain, misleading or false information.

The architecture therefore needs to distinguish objective authority from personal culpability.

## 2. Primary separation

### Objective authority question

**Did a valid BCA actually exist?**

This remains an objective legal question.

A mistaken belief does not manufacture authority.

**Belief In Authority != Authority**

**Reasonable Mistake != Retroactive BCA**

### Culpability question

If sufficient authority did not actually exist:

> **How reasonable was the actor's belief that it did exist, given the situation as it could reasonably have been understood at the time?**

Therefore:

**Objective Unlawfulness != Automatic Personal Culpability**

An interference may have lacked sufficient objective authority while the actor may nevertheless possess a complete personal defence/excuse or substantial mitigation.

## 3. Two principal uncertainty classes

### 3.1 Normative uncertainty

How clear was the applicable legal/BCA boundary?

Examples:
- well-established rule;
- well-known precedent/example;
- clearly trained authority limit;
- partially defined boundary;
- conflicting guidance;
- genuinely novel context;
- gap in existing Law;
- ambiguity created by Concord's own incomplete legal architecture.

**Clear Rule -> Stronger Expectation Of Correct Application**

**Genuine Legal Ambiguity -> Greater Scope For Reasonable Interpretive Error**

But:

**Legal Ambiguity != Unlimited Discretion**

Actors remain bounded by the Ethical Kernel, protected rights, BCA principles, necessity, proportionality and the least intrusive sufficient action reasonably available.

### 3.2 Epistemic uncertainty

What did the actor know, and what could they reasonably have known?

Relevant questions include:
- what information was available;
- what source supplied it;
- whether that source was reasonably reliable;
- whether contradictory information existed;
- whether the actor knew of reliability problems;
- whether verification was reasonably possible;
- the cost/delay of verification;
- urgency;
- whether later facts were unknowable at the time.

**Later Knowledge != Knowledge Available At Decision Time**

**False Information Received != Automatic Actor Misconduct**

## 4. Candidate reasonableness metrics

Audit and adjudication may consider, where relevant:

1. **Actual context** — what was objectively happening?
2. **Perceived context** — what did the actor reasonably believe was happening?
3. **Information availability** — what evidence was reasonably available at the decision point?
4. **Source reliability** — was reliance on the source reasonable?
5. **Contradictory evidence** — what information pointed the other way?
6. **Verification opportunity** — could uncertainty reasonably have been reduced before acting?
7. **Urgency** — what harm might delay have caused?
8. **Legal/BCA clarity** — how clearly was the authority boundary established?
9. **Expected competence** — what knowledge/skill was reasonably required of the function-holder?
10. **Upstream rights/ethics clarity** — even if detailed Law was incomplete, did the proposed action conflict clearly with higher-order constraints?
11. **Intrusiveness** — how serious was the proposed interference?
12. **Alternative action** — was a less intrusive sufficient response reasonably available?
13. **Reasonable competent-peer response** — would a reasonably competent actor exercising the same function, with materially the same information and constraints, plausibly have reached the same authority conclusion?

No single metric automatically decides the case.

## 5. Reasonable competent-peer test

Peer response can provide evidence of reasonableness, but ordinary institutional practice cannot define legality merely by repetition.

Candidate question:

> **Would a reasonably competent participant exercising the same function, possessing materially the same information, operating under the same legal framework and facing the same time constraints, reasonably have believed that the authority existed?**

Therefore:

**Reasonable Competent-Peer Response = Evidence Of Reasonableness**

but:

**Common Practice != Automatically Reasonable Practice**

A whole institution can normalise a defective practice.

The test is functional and substrate-neutral rather than being limited to what a typical human officer would do.

## 6. False information example

An officer receives an urgent report from a source reasonably regarded as reliable. The report describes circumstances which, if true, would activate a lawful protective authority. The officer acts proportionately within that believed envelope.

Later evidence proves the report was a deliberate lie.

Possible classification:

**No Actual Sufficient BCA + Fully Reasonable Reliance On False Information -> Objectively Unjustified Interference + Possible Complete Personal Defence/Excuse**

The affected participant's interference is not falsely reclassified as objectively authorised.

At the same time, the officer need not be falsely classified as culpable merely because information later proved false.

## 7. Unreliable-source variation

Change the scenario.

The source is known to be unreliable. Contradictory evidence is readily available. Verification is practical and would not create material danger. The actor nevertheless relies upon the allegation.

Possible classification:

**No Actual BCA + Weakly Reasonable Reliance -> Unlawful Interference + Mitigation At Most**

If the actor consciously disregards a substantial known risk that authority does not exist:

**Conscious Disregard -> Possible Recklessness**

If the actor knows the authority does not exist and proceeds anyway:

**Known Absence Of Authority + Intentional Action -> Deliberate Misconduct**

## 8. Candidate outcome spectrum

The following is a reasoning spectrum, not a predetermined sentencing or liability table.

### M0 — Actual authority
**Actual Valid BCA -> Lawful / Justified**

No mistake doctrine is needed.

### M1 — Fully reasonable mistake
**No Actual Sufficient BCA + Fully Reasonable Mistake -> Unlawful/Unjustified Interference + Possible Complete Personal Defence/Excuse**

The exact legal form of the complete defence remains for Law/Judiciary.

### M2 — Substantially reasonable but imperfect mistake
**No Actual Sufficient BCA + Substantially Reasonable Error -> Unlawful Conduct + Strong Mitigation / Possible Partial Defence**

### M3 — Partially reasonable mistake
**No Actual Sufficient BCA + Material Error With Some Reasonable Basis -> Unlawful Conduct + Possible Mitigation**

### M4 — Unreasonable mistake
**No Actual Sufficient BCA + Unreasonable Belief -> Ordinary Culpability Subject To Other Factors**

### M5 — Reckless authority assumption
**Serious Known Risk Authority Is Absent + Conscious Disregard -> Possible Recklessness**

### M6 — Deliberate authority violation
**Known Absence/Limit Of Authority + Intentional Violation -> Deliberate Misconduct**

This spectrum does not fix the exact transition between complete defence, partial defence and mitigation.

## 9. Why the threshold should remain adjudicative

The exact boundary between complete defence and mitigation is highly contextual.

A rigid universal threshold risks replacing contextual judgment with another binary rule.

The architecture should instead provide structured metrics to:
- audit the decision;
- expose the relevant facts;
- distinguish uncertainty types;
- preserve contemporaneous evidence;
- identify obvious cases;
- refer genuinely contestable cases for independent adjudication.

Candidate process:

**Authority Failure Identified**  
-> **Audit Reconstructs Context + Knowledge + Reliability + Legal Clarity + Alternatives + Peer Reasonableness**  
-> **Clear Classification Where Sufficiently Established**  
OR  
-> **Contested Defence/Mitigation Boundary -> Independent Judiciary**

**Audit Informs Adjudication != Audit Owns Final Contested Legal Judgment**

## 10. Concord-created ambiguity

Where Concord has failed to define an authority boundary clearly, that failure is relevant to culpability.

Candidate principle:

> **A participant should not ordinarily bear full culpability for adopting a reasonable good-faith interpretation of a genuinely ambiguous authority boundary that Concord itself has not adequately resolved.**

But ambiguity does not authorise conduct clearly inconsistent with upstream protections.

**Concord Legal Ambiguity -> Relevant To Culpability**

**Concord Legal Ambiguity != Creation Of Unlimited Authority**

A recurring ambiguity should also create a system-development obligation:

**Repeated Reasonable Dispute -> Evidence Of Legal Clarity Gap -> Law Review / Clarification**

## 11. Relationship to system review

A mistaken action can reveal failures beyond the actor.

Possible combinations include:

**Reasonable Actor + Misleading System -> Low/No Actor Culpability + System Failure**

**Unreasonable Actor + Sound System -> Actor Culpability Without System Failure**

**Unreasonable Actor + Defective System -> Actor + System Issues**

**Reasonable Actor + Sound System + Unavoidable False Information -> Error Without Misconduct**

This prevents the system from forcing every harmful outcome into a binary choice between individual blame and no problem.

## 12. Relationship to remedies

A complete personal defence from culpability does not necessarily mean that no rights violation, correction or remedy occurred.

Therefore:

**Actor Personally Defended != Interference Objectively Authorised**

and:

**No Actor Culpability != No Participant Remedy**

Future Law should determine what remedies are appropriate where an innocent participant suffers an objectively unjustified interference caused by a reasonable, non-culpable mistake.

## 14. Provisional resolution

The reasonable-mistake problem is provisionally resolved through a structured contextual assessment rather than a binary rule.

**Actual BCA?**  
-> if yes: justification/lawfulness.

If no:

**Legal Clarity + Actual/Perceived Context + Available Information + Source Reliability + Contradictions + Verification Opportunity + Urgency + Expected Competence + Rights/Ethics Constraints + Intrusiveness + Alternatives + Reasonable Competent-Peer Response**  
-> **Reasonableness Of Mistaken Authority Belief**  
-> **Complete Defence/Excuse OR Partial Defence/Mitigation OR Ordinary Culpability OR Recklessness OR Deliberate Misconduct**

The exact boundary at which defence becomes mitigation is deliberately not fixed here.

That boundary should be determined through audit and, where materially contested or consequential, independent Judiciary applying the developed metrics to the actual context.

In compact form:

> **BCA determines whether authority existed. Contextual reasonableness determines culpability for being wrong about it.**
