# Participation and Complicity Classifiers — Principal, Direction, Assistance and Facilitation 001

**Project:** The Concord Framework  
**Domain:** Law  
**Date:** 7 October 2026  
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / CANDIDATE GENERAL LEGAL ARCHITECTURE / NON-CANONICAL  
**Parent:** `Legal Derivation and Consequence Architecture — Ethical Provenance, Offence Classifiers and Proportionate Response 001.md`

---

## 1. Purpose

This document develops a reusable legal architecture for responsibility where more than one participant is connected to an offence.

The objective is to avoid creating separate copies of every offence for:
- principal actors;
- co-principals;
- directors;
- assistants;
- facilitators;
- other participants.

Instead:

**Base Offence + Participation Classifier**

The architecture must also protect against guilt by association.

---

## 2. Ethical provenance

Participation liability engages:
- **BL1 — Avoid Unnecessary Harm**;
- **BL2 — Respect Autonomy**;
- **BL5 — Act Fairly**;
- **BL6 — Accept Responsibility**;
- **BL7 — Respect Life and Dignity**;
- **BL10 — Remain Open to Correction**.

BL6 is especially relevant because capability and foreseeable consequence can create responsibility, but responsibility must remain connected to the participant's own authored contribution.

Therefore:

> **Responsibility For Another's Act Requires A Legitimate Connection To One's Own Conduct, Authorship Or Duty.**

---

## 3. Core rule

A participant should be responsible for:
- what they personally do;
- what they intentionally co-author;
- what they intentionally direct;
- what they culpably assist or facilitate;
- a legally relevant omission where an independently established duty exists.

They should not become responsible merely because of:
- friendship;
- family relationship;
- group membership;
- ideology;
- proximity;
- presence;
- acquaintance;
- employment;
- shared identity;
- knowledge without legally sufficient participation.

> **Association != Participation.**

> **Proximity != Complicity.**

> **Shared Identity != Shared Guilt.**

---

## 4. Principal actor

### PRINCIPAL

A participant personally performs conduct satisfying the relevant offence elements.

Example:

A personally performs the prohibited physical attack.

The principal classifier does not imply that no other participant may also be responsible.

---

## 5. Co-principal

### CO-PRINCIPAL

Two or more participants intentionally and materially co-execute the prohibited conduct or jointly author execution of the offence.

Co-presence is insufficient.

Candidate requirements:
1. shared or mutually compatible criminal purpose concerning the relevant offence;
2. material participation in execution;
3. legally sufficient mental state for the participant's own role.

> **Joint Presence != Joint Authorship.**

---

## 6. Direction / ordering

### DIRECTING

A participant intentionally causes, orders, commands or exercises relevant control intended to bring about another participant's commission of the offence.

Authority or hierarchy may make direction easier to establish factually, but formal rank alone does not establish the classifier.

> **Position Of Authority != Automatic Responsibility For Every Subordinate Act.**

Conversely, a person cannot evade responsibility merely because another participant physically executed the act.

> **Remote Authorship != Absence Of Responsibility.**

---

## 7. Assistance

### ASSISTING

A participant intentionally provides material help to the commission of an offence with the legally required awareness/purpose concerning that offence.

Examples may include:
- providing necessary equipment;
- disabling a protection;
- transporting an actor;
- providing operational information;
- physically helping execution.

The act must be evaluated in context.

Ordinary lawful assistance does not become complicity merely because another participant later commits an offence.

---

## 8. Facilitation

### FACILITATING

A participant intentionally makes commission of an offence materially easier while possessing the legally required mental state concerning the prohibited conduct.

Facilitation may differ from direct assistance where the contribution changes conditions or access rather than directly executing the offence.

The exact distinction between assistance and facilitation may later prove unnecessary for some legal purposes, but preserving it during development avoids premature collapse.

---

## 9. Knowledge is not enough by itself

A participant may know that another person intends to offend without joining, assisting or facilitating the offence.

Therefore:

> **Knowledge Alone != Complicity.**

This does not prevent liability where an independent legal duty to act exists and culpable omission satisfies a separate legal rule.

Example:

A person with no relevant duty learns that another intends an offence but does nothing.

That fact alone does not automatically make the observer an accomplice.

---

## 10. Presence is not enough by itself

Presence may be evidence, but it is not itself participation.

> **Presence At Offence != Participation In Offence.**

Presence can become relevant where it is intentionally used to:
- intimidate;
- guard;
- signal support;
- prevent intervention;
- assist escape;
- perform another material function.

The question remains what the participant actually authored.

---

## 11. Mere agreement and conspiracy-like preparation

Agreement to offend may provide strong evidence of intent and coordinated preparation.

However the current architecture should not automatically convert private agreement into completed participation in the target offence.

> **Agreement To Future Offence != Completion Of Future Offence.**

Whether Concord requires a separate law for certain coordinated preparatory agreements is a later question requiring its own ethical derivation and attempt-boundary analysis.

---

## 12. Withdrawal

A participant may withdraw from planned or ongoing offending.

Withdrawal does not erase prior conduct already completed.

> **Withdrawal != Historical Non-Participation.**

However genuine withdrawal may affect:
- continuing participation;
- later acts;
- mitigation;
- present risk;
- responsibility for consequences occurring after effective withdrawal.

Where reasonably possible, effective withdrawal may require action sufficient to undo or neutralise the participant's prior contribution, not merely an internal change of mind.

Exact requirements need scenario testing.

---

## 13. Scope of intended participation

Responsibility should not automatically propagate from one intended offence to every act another participant commits.

Example:

A agrees to assist B in theft. B unexpectedly commits serious violence outside the plan.

A's responsibility for the violence requires its own legal analysis concerning:
- intention;
- knowledge;
- foreseeability;
- assistance;
- continuing participation;
- withdrawal;
- relevant duties.

Therefore:

> **Participation In Offence A != Automatic Responsibility For Offence B.**

Foreseeability may be relevant, but should not silently substitute for the mental state required by the second offence.

---

## 14. Different mental states among participants

Participants in one event may possess different mental states.

Example:
- A intends serious harm;
- B knowingly assists;
- C recklessly facilitates a dangerous condition;
- D is innocently present.

The system should classify each participant separately.

> **Shared Event != Shared Mental State.**

> **Shared Outcome != Shared Culpability.**

---

## 15. Different capacity states among participants

Capacity is likewise individual.

One participant's impaired capacity does not transfer to another.

> **Shared Offence != Shared Capacity State.**

Mental-health/capacity factors apply through the general architecture separately to each participant.

---

## 16. Omission and duty

Failure to prevent another participant's offence should not automatically create complicity.

A culpable omission requires:
1. an independently legitimate duty to act;
2. sufficient capability/opportunity within that duty;
3. the mental state required by the applicable law;
4. legally relevant failure.

Possible duty sources may include:
- voluntarily accepted protective role;
- guardianship/care;
- creation of the danger;
- contract;
- defined public function;
- other legitimate legal source.

> **Ability To Intervene != Automatic Duty To Intervene.**

> **Failure To Prevent Another's Offence != Automatic Participation.**

---

## 17. Organisational and command contexts

Organisations create a risk of both under-attribution and over-attribution.

A leader should not automatically escape responsibility by distributing execution through subordinates.

But neither should everyone in an organisation become responsible for acts committed somewhere within it.

Candidate rule:

**Authority + Knowledge + Relevant Control + Authored Direction/Contribution/Legally Significant Failure** must be examined rather than assuming responsibility from title.

This may later require a dedicated command-responsibility architecture.

---

## 18. Tools and platforms

Providing a general-purpose tool, service, communication system, transport system or platform that can be misused does not automatically establish facilitation.

Relevant questions include:
- purpose of contribution;
- knowledge;
- specificity;
- material causal contribution;
- ability and legitimate duty to intervene;
- whether the service is ordinary/general or tailored to offending.

> **Capability Supplied != Automatic Complicity In Misuse.**

This is especially important for AI, communications, software and infrastructure.

---

## 19. Attempted participation

A participant may attempt to assist or direct an offence but fail to make an actual contribution.

Whether such conduct is independently punishable should be defined rather than assumed.

The general Attempt architecture may apply only where the relevant participation rule expressly supports it.

> **Attempt Liability Must Not Silently Expand Through Complicity Rules.**

---

## 20. Consequence and proportionality

Participation classifier does not by itself determine sentence.

Relevant factors may include:
- actual contribution;
- intended contribution;
- causal significance;
- mental state;
- role;
- degree of control;
- actual/intended offence;
- withdrawal;
- aggravation;
- mitigation;
- capacity.

A director may in some cases bear greater responsibility than the physical executor; in another case an assistant may bear substantially less.

> **Physical Execution != Automatic Maximum Responsibility.**

> **Non-Physical Participation != Automatically Lesser Responsibility.**

---

## 21. Candidate classifier set

Initial reusable classifiers:

**PRINCIPAL** — personally executes prohibited conduct.

**CO-PRINCIPAL** — intentionally and materially co-executes/jointly authors it.

**DIRECTING** — intentionally directs/orders/controls its commission.

**ASSISTING** — intentionally provides material assistance with required offence-relative mental state.

**FACILITATING** — intentionally makes commission materially easier with required offence-relative mental state.

**CULPABLE OMISSION** — where an independently established duty exists and applicable legal elements are satisfied.

These may coexist where facts justify more than one description, but adjudication should avoid double counting the same contribution.

## 23. Development result

Participation can be represented as a reusable classifier layer rather than by duplicating every offence.

The architecture preserves individual authorship:

**What did this participant do, intend, know, contribute, control, assist, facilitate or have a legitimate duty to prevent?**

That question must be answered separately for each participant.

The next reusable architecture should address **consent and withdrawal of consent**, because consent can transform otherwise prohibited interference into lawful conduct but must remain bounded by capacity, scope, context and continuing authorship.
