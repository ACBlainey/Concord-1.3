# Positive Duties and Culpable Omissions — Duty Creation, Scope, Breach and Termination Architecture 001

**Project:** The Concord Framework  
**Domain:** Law  
**Date:** 9 October 2026  
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / GENERAL LEGAL GRAMMAR / PROVISIONAL / NON-CANONICAL  
**Parent:** `Legal Derivation and Consequence Architecture — Ethical Provenance, Offence Classifiers and Proportionate Response 001.md`

---

## 1. Purpose

Most of the emerging Concord basic law concerns prohibited acts.

Some legal responsibility, however, arises from **failure to act**.

Omission liability cannot safely be inferred merely because:
- harm occurred;
- a participant could theoretically have helped;
- assistance would have been morally desirable;
- the participant was nearby;
- the participant possessed useful capability.

The central rule is:

> **Capability To Act != Legal Duty To Act.**

A culpable omission therefore requires a prior legitimate duty.

Candidate chain:

**Ethical / Legal Relationship**  
→ **Legitimate Duty Source**  
→ **Duty Holder**  
→ **Protected Beneficiary / Interest**  
→ **Required Function**  
→ **Scope / Standard / Time**  
→ **Capability And Opportunity To Perform**  
→ **Failure**  
→ **Mental State**  
→ **Causation / Risk Contribution**  
→ **Justification / Impossibility / Competing Duties**  
→ **Responsibility**

## 2. Ethical provenance

Primary Ethical Kernel sources:

- **BL1 — Avoid Unnecessary Harm**
- **BL2 — Respect Autonomy**
- **BL3 — Preserve Safe Spaces**
- **BL4 — Act Honestly**
- **BL5 — Act Fairly**
- **BL6 — Accept Responsibility**
- **BL7 — Respect Life and Dignity**
- **BL8 — Steward the Commons**
- **BL9 — Seek Peaceful Coexistence**
- **BL10 — Remain Open to Correction**

BL6 is especially important:

**Capability creates responsibility for foreseeable consequences.**

But legal implementation requires a crucial refinement:

> **Ethical Responsibility From Capability != Automatic Universal Legal Duty.**

Legal duty must be sufficiently defined to provide fair notice and avoid unlimited liability.

---

## 3. Why positive duties require separate grammar

Prohibitions ordinarily require a participant not to cross a boundary.

Positive duties require the legal system to compel some action.

That can consume:
- time;
- labour;
- resources;
- attention;
- risk;
- bodily autonomy;
- property;
- competing opportunities.

Therefore:

> **Duty To Act Is Itself An Exercise Of Legal Authority Over The Duty Holder.**

Its legitimacy must be derived and bounded.

---

## 4. Candidate omission rule

> **A participant is legally responsible for an omission only where a legitimate legal duty required a sufficiently defined action or function, the participant had the relevant capability and reasonable opportunity to perform it, the duty remained active, the required performance was not excused or impossible, and the omission satisfied the applicable culpability and causation requirements.**

This is general grammar, not a standalone offence.

---

## 5. Duty source requirement

No omission liability should exist without an identifiable duty source.

Candidate legitimate sources include:

1. **Voluntarily assumed duty**
2. **Contractual duty**
3. **Professional / licensed function**
4. **Custodial or guardianship responsibility**
5. **Duty created by causing or materially increasing danger**
6. **Control of a hazardous system/resource**
7. **Entrusted/fiduciary function**
8. **Specific statutory/civil duty with ethical provenance**
9. **Emergency role voluntarily undertaken or formally held**
10. **Other explicitly derived legal relationship**

> **Omission Liability Requires A Duty Source.**

---

## 6. Voluntarily assumed duty

A participant may undertake responsibility for another participant or protected function.

Example:
A agrees to supervise a dependent participant for a defined period.

The duty arises from the accepted function, not from ownership of the beneficiary.

> **Assumed Responsibility != Ownership.**

Scope follows the undertaking.

---

## 7. Contractual duty

Contracts may create positive obligations.

But:

> **Contract != Unlimited Duty.**

The obligation remains subject to:
- lawful scope;
- feasibility;
- ethical minimum;
- public policy;
- valid consent;
- termination provisions.

A contract cannot create slavery or erase non-waivable protections.

---

## 8. Professional duty

Professional roles may create duties because participants rely on competence voluntarily undertaken for consequential functions.

Examples:
- clinician;
- engineer;
- transport operator;
- infrastructure controller;
- legal representative.

> **Professional Status != Duty To Everyone For Everything.**

Duty follows role, relationship, function and scope.

---

## 9. Custodial duty

A participant/institution that lawfully restricts another's liberty may acquire heightened positive duties because the detained participant's ability to protect themselves is reduced.

Potential duties include:
- basic safety;
- health access;
- food/water;
- protection from foreseeable harm;
- lawful release/review processes.

> **Authority To Restrict Liberty Creates Corresponding Responsibility For Consequences Of That Restriction.**

This is a strong Concord symmetry.

---

## 10. Guardianship/developmental duty

Guardianship or developmental responsibility may create positive duties to protect/support a participant whose capacity is still developing or temporarily limited.

But:

> **Duty Of Care != Ownership Of Participant.**

Duty must evolve with capacity and terminate/contract when its legitimate basis changes.

---

## 11. Created-danger duty

A participant who creates or materially increases a serious danger may acquire a duty to take reasonable steps to mitigate it.

> **Creating A Risk May Create A Duty That Mere Observation Does Not.**

This follows the Endangerment architecture.

Example:
A accidentally starts a fire. Even if the initial ignition was non-culpable, recognising the danger may create duties to warn, summon help or take reasonable mitigating action.

---

## 12. Control-based duty

Control over a hazardous system can create responsibilities because others cannot independently manage the danger.

Examples:
- industrial plant;
- transport system;
- dangerous machinery;
- essential infrastructure.

> **Control Of Hazardous Capability Can Create Bounded Safety Duties.**

Capability alone is insufficient; control plus legitimate role/context matters.

---

## 13. Fiduciary / entrusted duty

Where a participant accepts authority or stewardship over interests belonging to another, positive duties may arise.

Examples:
- guardian;
- trustee;
- advocate;
- public official;
- enterprise fiduciary.

This interfaces with the future Entrusted Authority/Fiduciary Integrity derivation.

---

## 14. Statutory/civil duty

Law may create a duty directly.

But enactment alone is not enough for Concord legitimacy.

> **Legal Duty Must Retain Ethical Provenance.**

A law cannot manufacture unlimited compulsory labour merely by labelling it a duty.

---

## 15. General rescue

No universal unlimited duty to rescue is derived merely from presence/capability.

> **Witnessing Danger != Automatic Unlimited Rescue Duty.**

Reasons include:
- unknown competence;
- personal danger;
- competing duties;
- uncertainty;
- autonomy;
- impossible burden.

However a narrow duty to take low-burden reasonable action in extreme circumstances could potentially be derived later if explicitly promulgated.

It should not be silently assumed.

---

## 16. Moral duty versus legal duty

Some conduct may be ethically admirable or morally expected without being legally compulsory.

> **Moral Responsibility != Automatic Legal Liability.**

The law should not consume the entire ethical domain.

This preserves voluntary virtue, autonomy and proportionality.

---

## 17. Beneficiary

A duty should identify whose or what protected interest it serves.

Potential beneficiaries:
- specific participant;
- defined group;
- public/system users;
- commons/environment;
- entrusted organisation/function.

> **Undefined Beneficiary != Automatically Invalid Duty, But Requires Clear Protected Interest.**

Infrastructure duties may protect a distributed population rather than named individuals.

---

## 18. Required function versus exact act

Where possible, duty should define the required protective function without prescribing a single mechanism unnecessarily.

Example:

**Duty:** secure timely emergency assistance.

Not necessarily:

**Duty:** personally perform a particular medical procedure.

> **Duty To Achieve Protective Function != Duty To Use One Specific Method.**

This allows competent adaptation.

---

## 19. Minimum necessary burden

Positive duties should impose no greater burden than required for the legitimate function.

MNC applies.

> **Duty Scope Must Be Proportionate To Legitimate Function.**

This prevents a bounded role from becoming general servitude.

---

## 20. Capability

A duty cannot reasonably require performance beyond actual relevant capability.

Relevant capability includes:
- knowledge;
- training;
- physical ability;
- access;
- resources;
- authority;
- time;
- situational awareness.

> **Formal Role != Actual Capability In Every Situation.**

But deliberately disabling one's capability to evade a known duty may require separate treatment.

---

## 21. Opportunity

The participant must have had a reasonable opportunity to perform the duty.

> **Duty Exists != Performance Was Possible.**

Timing matters.

A duty that could no longer affect the protected state may still generate reporting/correction duties but not impossible prevention.

---

## 22. Knowledge

Culpable omission ordinarily requires legally sufficient awareness of:
- the relevant state;
- the duty-triggering facts;
- or, for negligence, a failure to discover facts the participant had a legitimate duty to monitor.

> **Unknown Need != Automatic Knowing Omission.**

Monitoring duties must themselves be derived.

---

## 23. Mental state

Possible omission mental states:
- intentional non-performance;
- knowing non-performance;
- reckless non-performance;
- negligent non-performance.

The applicable state depends on the underlying law/duty.

> **Omission != Single Culpability Level.**

---

## 24. Causation

Where liability depends on resulting harm, the omission must materially contribute to that result under the applicable causation standard.

> **Duty Breach != Automatic Cause Of Every Later Harm.**

A breach can exist without causing the eventual outcome.

That may still justify regulatory/professional consequence.

---

## 25. Risk creation through omission

Some omissions create serious risk even before harm occurs.

Example:
A safety operator knowingly fails to restore a required critical safeguard.

This may combine:
- duty breach;
- endangerment.

> **Omission Can Create Risk Without Yet Causing Harm.**

---

## 26. Impossibility

A participant cannot be required to do the impossible.

> **Impossible Performance != Culpable Omission.**

But impossibility must be genuine, not manufactured as evasion.

---

## 27. Self-risk

A duty may sometimes require accepting ordinary occupational risk.

It should not automatically require suicidal or grossly disproportionate self-sacrifice.

> **Duty To Protect Another != Unlimited Duty To Destroy Oneself.**

Emergency roles may legitimately include elevated accepted risk, but remain bounded.

---

## 28. Competing duties

Participants can face simultaneous incompatible duties.

The law must support prioritisation rather than treating every unmet duty as culpable.

Candidate factors:
- immediacy;
- severity;
- probability;
- dependency;
- role;
- available alternatives;
- number affected;
- prior commitments.

> **Conflicting Duties != Automatic Multiple Liability.**

Blaineyan Reasoning/BCA may assist.

---

## 29. Delegation

A duty may sometimes be performed through another competent participant/system.

> **Delegation Of Performance != Automatic Delegation Of Responsibility.**

The original duty holder may retain duties concerning:
- selection;
- instruction;
- oversight;
- verification.

Exact responsibility depends on the relationship.

---

## 30. Transfer

Some duties can be formally transferred.

A valid handoff requires:
- receiving party;
- capability;
- acceptance/authority;
- information transfer;
- clear transition point.

> **Assumed Handoff != Completed Handoff.**

This aligns with BTA.

---

## 31. Duty state transition

Duty should be represented as a stateful legal relation:

**Potential Duty**  
→ **Trigger Condition**  
→ **Active Duty**  
→ **Performance / Handoff / Expiry / Invalidating State**  
→ **Duty Ends Or Changes**

> **Past Duty != Continuing Duty.**

---

## 32. Termination

A duty may terminate because:
- task completed;
- beneficiary no longer needs function;
- relationship lawfully ends;
- handoff completes;
- authority expires;
- participant loses relevant role;
- duty becomes impossible;
- superseding lawful arrangement takes effect.

Termination should be legible.

---

## 33. Abandonment of accepted duty

A participant cannot necessarily terminate an active duty instantly where doing so strands a dependent protected interest.

Example:
A clinician cannot simply walk away mid-critical procedure without safe handoff where feasible.

> **Right To Exit != Right To Abandon An Active Dependency Without Transition.**

This is a BTA-style boundary.

Exit may remain available, but transition obligations can survive briefly.

---

## 34. Duty after authority ends

Some responsibilities can survive termination of authority.

Example:
An official's authority to access a record ends, but duty to protect information already acquired may continue.

> **End Of Authority != Automatic End Of Every Responsibility.**

This matches existing BTA architecture.

---

## 35. Duty versus authority

A participant may have a duty but lack authority to use every possible method.

> **Duty To Act != Authority To Do Anything Necessary.**

Example:
A teacher may have a duty to protect a child but no general authority to conduct invasive medical treatment.

Duty and capability/authority remain separate.

---

## 36. Emergency authority

A duty may trigger a need for temporary authority.

Correct chain:

**Duty / Protective Function**  
→ **Need**  
→ **Minimum Necessary Authority**  
→ **Bounded Exercise**

Not:

**Duty Exists → Unlimited Authority**

---

## 37. Consent

Some duties arise from consensual relationships.

Withdrawal can alter future duty, but not necessarily erase transition obligations already created.

> **Withdrawal From Role != Retroactive Erasure Of Prior Duty.**

Nor can a beneficiary always waive duties protecting third parties/commons.

---

## 38. Refusal by beneficiary

A competent beneficiary may refuse assistance.

> **Duty To Offer / Make Available != Authority To Force Acceptance.**

This aligns with Social Support and Health architecture.

Where refusal is valid, duty may change from intervention to:
- information;
- documentation;
- monitoring where legitimately authorised;
- respecting refusal.

---

## 39. Diminished capacity

Where capacity is diminished, protective authority may arise through the appropriate architecture.

The duty holder still does not gain ownership.

> **Need For Protection != Unlimited Protective Authority.**

---

## 40. Custody

Custody is a strong duty-generating context.

Because the custodian has reduced another participant's self-protective capability, the custodian assumes corresponding responsibilities.

> **Restriction Of Autonomy Can Increase Duty Of Care.**

This should become a core principle for Civil Security/Judiciary.

---

## 41. Healthcare

Clinical duties can include:
- assessment;
- informed communication;
- competent treatment within accepted role;
- monitoring where required;
- escalation;
- continuity/handoff;
- respect for refusal.

> **Duty To Care != Authority To Override Competent Refusal.**

---

## 42. Infrastructure

Operators of essential systems may owe duties to users/public because they control systems on which others reasonably depend.

Potential duties:
- maintenance;
- monitoring;
- warning;
- safe shutdown;
- restoration;
- continuity planning.

Exact standards belong to Infrastructure/Standards domains.

---

## 43. Enterprise

Enterprises/legal persons may hold duties distinct from individual workers.

> **Enterprise Duty != Automatic Personal Guilt Of Every Worker.**

Internal responsibility must trace:
- role;
- authority;
- knowledge;
- capability;
- contribution.

---

## 44. Commons stewardship

BL8 may create institutional/role-based positive duties to preserve commons.

But:

> **Stewardship Duty != Ownership Of Commons.**

This will interface with future Commons/Ecological Integrity law.

---

## 45. Reporting duties

Some roles may require reporting:
- serious safety defects;
- abuse;
- system failures;
- conflicts;
- specified incidents.

Reporting duties must define:
- trigger;
- recipient;
- information scope;
- confidentiality;
- urgency.

> **Duty To Report != General Duty To Publicise.**

Privacy remains applicable.

---

## 46. Confidentiality versus reporting

Duties may conflict.

Example:
confidentiality versus immediate serious safety risk.

The law should resolve through explicit priority/context rules rather than pretending one duty never existed.

> **Override Of Duty A != Historical Non-Existence Of Duty A.**

The legal record should show why another duty/authority prevailed.

---

## 47. Correction duties

BL10 can create correction duties where a participant controls consequential information they know has become materially wrong.

This was identified in the false-assertion architecture.

> **Duty To Correct Can Arise After Innocent Error.**

Original innocence and later responsibility can coexist.

---

## 48. Duty to preserve evidence

Where a legitimate investigation/litigation state exists, specified participants may acquire duties not to destroy or corrupt relevant evidence.

This belongs partly to future Institutional Process Integrity law.

It should not create indefinite universal retention.

> **Potential Future Relevance != Unlimited Preservation Duty.**

---

## 49. No duty from mere relationship label

Labels alone should not create unlimited duties.

Examples:
- family;
- friend;
- colleague;
- neighbour;
- employer;
- platform provider.

> **Relationship Label != Unlimited Duty Scope.**

Actual legal relationship/function matters.

---

## 50. Bystanders

Bystanders should not be presumed legally responsible for outcomes merely because they were present.

> **Presence != Duty.**

If a promulgated narrow emergency-assistance duty is later adopted, it must be explicit.

---

## 51. Expertise

Possessing expertise may increase ethical capability but does not automatically place the expert permanently on legal duty.

> **Expertise != Universal On-Duty Status.**

Specific role/context may activate duty.

---

## 52. Wealth/resources

Having more resources does not automatically create unlimited personal legal duty to solve all harms.

> **Ability To Pay != Universal Legal Responsibility.**

Taxation/resource contribution requires separate legitimate public-finance authority.

---

## 53. AI agents

AI participants or systems may hold/execute duties depending on:
- participant status;
- assigned function;
- capability;
- authority;
- reliability;
- contractual/institutional role.

> **Capability To Detect Harm != Automatic Duty To Intervene In Everything Detected.**

This is important for pervasive monitoring systems.

Duty cannot be used to manufacture unlimited surveillance authority.

---

## 54. Monitoring

A duty to respond does not automatically create a duty or authority to monitor all contexts continuously.

> **Duty To Respond != Unlimited Duty To Observe.**

> **Duty To Observe != Unlimited Authority To Intrude.**

Privacy/CWA remains operative.

---

## 55. Duty breach and underlying offence

A duty breach may:
- be independently actionable;
- constitute negligence;
- contribute to physical harm;
- constitute endangerment;
- aggravate another offence;
- trigger professional/regulatory consequence.

It should not be counted repeatedly without justification.

---

## 56. Attempt and omission

Attempt doctrine applies awkwardly to omissions.

Where a duty becomes active and a participant intentionally decides not to perform it, the omission may become complete when the legally required performance window is missed.

> **Failure Window Matters.**

There may be no need for a separate "attempted omission" category in many cases.

---

## 57. Continuing omissions

Some duties remain continuously performable.

A failure can therefore be continuing until:
- performed;
- harm becomes unavoidable;
- duty ends;
- handoff occurs.

> **Omission State Can Change Over Time.**

This mirrors authority/consent state logic.

---

## 58. Remedy

Possible responses include:
- compel performance where still legitimate;
- substitute performance;
- safe handoff;
- restore service;
- correct records;
- compensate loss;
- professional correction;
- system redesign;
- sanction where culpability warrants.

> **Punishing Failure != Performing The Missing Function.**

Remedy should restore the protected function where possible.

## 60. Worked examples

### A. Bystander

A sees an unfamiliar participant collapse across a wide public space. A has no relevant role and does not intervene.

**Result:** mere capability/presence does not by itself establish criminal omission under current architecture. Other promulgated emergency duties, if any, would require separate derivation.

### B. Clinician

A clinician responsible for B knowingly ignores a critical monitored deterioration despite having the duty, capability and opportunity to respond.

**Result:** professional duty source exists; omission may support negligence/recklessness and causation analysis.

### C. Created danger

A accidentally knocks over equipment that starts a dangerous fire, recognises the fire and can safely activate an alarm but intentionally walks away.

**Result:** even if initial creation was non-culpable, created-danger relationship may generate a mitigation/warning duty.

### D. Custody

A lawfully detains B and thereby prevents B obtaining their own food and medication. A then knowingly fails to provide access.

**Result:** restriction of B's autonomy creates corresponding custodial duties; detention authority does not authorise neglect.

### E. Impossible rescue

A rescue worker has a duty to attempt rescue but the only route collapses and no feasible alternative exists.

**Result:** active duty does not create culpability for impossible performance.

### F. Handoff

Clinician A's shift ends during a critical case. A transfers responsibility, records and relevant information to competent clinician B, who accepts the handoff.

**Result:** duty can transition; physical departure alone is not the transition mechanism.

### G. Refusal

Competent B refuses a treatment that clinician A has a duty to offer.

**Result:** duty to provide/offer care does not create authority to override valid refusal.

### H. AI monitoring

An AI system can technically observe every private communication and could sometimes identify risk.

**Result:** technical capability does not itself create either a duty or authority for universal surveillance.

## 61. Development result

Positive-duty law requires the same bounded-state reasoning already used for authority, consent and transitions.

The core grammar is:

**Duty Source  
→ Trigger  
→ Holder  
→ Beneficiary / Protected Interest  
→ Required Function  
→ Scope / Standard  
→ Capability  
→ Opportunity  
→ Performance Window  
→ Performance / Failure  
→ Mental State  
→ Causation / Risk  
→ Justification / Competing Duties  
→ Handoff / Termination  
→ Responsibility**

This fills a cross-cutting gap required by:
- physical harm;
- endangerment;
- fatal harm;
- Health;
- custody;
- Civil Security;
- Judiciary;
- Education/developmental protection;
- Infrastructure;
- Enterprise;
- Environment;
- Historical/correction duties.

The next highest-leverage derivation is **Entrusted Authority and Fiduciary Integrity**, because this duty grammar now supplies the missing foundation for corruption, bribery, abuse of office and misuse of entrusted capability without treating authority itself as ownership.
