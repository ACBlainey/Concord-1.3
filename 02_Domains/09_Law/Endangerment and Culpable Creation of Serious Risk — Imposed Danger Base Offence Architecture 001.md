## 1. Purpose

This document derives candidate law governing culpable creation or imposition of serious risk where the prohibited harm does not necessarily occur.

It fills the space between:
- completed harm;
- intentional attempt;
- ordinary acceptable risk;
- accident.

Core problem:

A participant may knowingly or recklessly create a serious unjustified danger to another participant, yet chance, intervention or good fortune prevents injury.

That state should not be misclassified as:
- completed physical harm, because the harm did not occur; or
- attempted harm, where there was no intent to cause the prohibited result.

Candidate structure:

**Conduct / Omission**  
→ **Created Or Increased Risk**  
→ **Protected Interest Exposed**  
→ **Risk Magnitude + Probability + Immediacy**  
→ **Mental State**  
→ **Consent / Context / Authority / Social Function**  
→ **Endangerment Classification**

## 3. Protected interest

The protected interest is not freedom from all risk.

> **Safe Space != Risk-Free Space.**

The protected interest is freedom from **unjustifiably imposed material danger** to protected interests such as:
- life;
- bodily integrity;
- liberty;
- property;
- essential systems;
- environment/commons;
- other sufficiently serious protected interests.

---

## 4. Candidate base prohibition

> **A participant must not, without valid consent, lawful justification or other independently legitimate basis, intentionally, knowingly or recklessly create, materially increase or knowingly maintain a substantial and unjustified risk of serious prohibited harm to another participant or protected civil interest.**

Negligent endangerment may be separately recognised where a legitimate duty/standard exists.

---

## 5. Endangerment versus attempt

This distinction is fundamental.

### Attempt
The participant intends the prohibited result and enters direct execution.

### Endangerment
The participant need not desire the prohibited result but culpably creates or accepts a serious risk that it will occur.

> **Risking A Result != Necessarily Intending The Result.**

> **Reckless Endangerment != Failed Attempt.**

This prevents the attempt architecture from being distorted.

---

## 6. Endangerment versus completed harm

If harm occurs, the resulting harm offence may supersede or coexist with the endangerment state depending on legal implementation.

But:

> **No Injury != No Legally Relevant Danger.**

Good fortune should not erase culpable risk creation.

Equally:

> **Bad Outcome != Proof That Prior Risk Was Unjustifiable.**

Hindsight must not determine risk classification.

---

## 7. Risk dimensions

Risk should not be represented only as probability.

Relevant dimensions include:
- probability;
- severity;
- number of exposed participants;
- duration;
- reversibility;
- detectability;
- participant vulnerability;
- controllability;
- available safeguards;
- uncertainty.

A low-probability catastrophic risk may still be material.

> **Low Probability != Automatically Low Legal Significance.**

---

## 8. Risk state

A candidate conceptual model:

**Risk Significance = Probability × Severity × Exposure**, adjusted by context, uncertainty, safeguards and reversibility.

This is not a mandatory numerical formula.

> **Risk Model != Automatic Liability Formula.**

The purpose is disciplined reasoning, not false precision.

---

## 9. Foreseeability

Risk must be assessed from the information available at the relevant time.

> **Outcome Knowledge Must Not Be Back-Propagated Into Prior Foreseeability.**

This imports the existing Mental State invariant.

A freak outcome does not prove recklessness merely because it happened.

---

## 10. Knowledge

A participant may know that a serious risk exists and proceed.

Knowledge of risk alone does not automatically establish unlawfulness.

Examples:
- surgery;
- firefighting;
- hazardous engineering;
- rescue;
- regulated transport;
- boxing.

> **Known Risk != Unjustified Risk.**

Purpose, necessity, consent, safeguards and proportionality matter.

---

## 11. Recklessness

Candidate reckless endangerment requires:
1. awareness of a substantial risk;
2. conscious decision to proceed or maintain the risk;
3. absence of sufficient justification;
4. legally material exposure.

> **Risk Awareness != Automatic Recklessness.**

A participant may knowingly take a justified risk.

---

## 12. Negligence

Negligent endangerment may arise where:
- a legitimate duty/standard exists;
- a material risk was reasonably foreseeable;
- the participant failed to meet the required standard;
- the failure created or materially increased serious danger.

> **Could Have Imagined Risk != Automatically Negligent.**

The standard must remain realistic and role/context specific.

---

## 13. Accident

Unexpected risk creation without legally sufficient culpability should not be converted into criminal liability.

> **Danger Created != Automatic Culpability.**

Investigation, correction and system learning may still be required.

---

## 14. Consent and authored risk

Participants may knowingly accept risk.

The Authored Risk Acceptance and Consent architectures apply.

> **Consent To Risk != Consent To Unlimited Harm.**

> **Authored Risk != Authority To Export Risk To Non-Participants.**

Example:
Two participants may consent to boxing risks; spectators or neighbours have not thereby consented to unrelated danger.

---

## 15. Boxing and bounded dangerous contexts

Boxing provides a useful Concord example.

Inside a legitimate contextual wrapper:
- physical contact is expected;
- defined injury risks are accepted;
- rules constrain conduct;
- officials/safeguards exist;
- withdrawal/termination mechanisms exist.

> **Dangerous Activity != Automatically Endangerment.**

But conduct outside the wrapper can change state.

Example:
Continuing to attack after the bout is stopped is not justified by prior risk acceptance.

---

## 16. Driving and transport

Transport inherently creates risk.

Ordinary compliant driving is not endangerment merely because collision is possible.

Candidate distinction:

**Necessary/Ordinary Risk + Competent Safeguards**  
!=  
**Substantial Unjustified Risk + Culpable Disregard**

Examples of potentially relevant factors:
- impairment;
- extreme speed relative to context;
- known mechanical failure;
- deliberate safety-system disabling;
- hazardous manoeuvres.

Exact transport rules belong in specialised law/regulation.

---

## 17. Medicine

Medical treatment may intentionally expose a participant to substantial risk for legitimate therapeutic purpose.

> **Medical Risk != Automatic Endangerment.**

Relevant factors:
- informed consent;
- clinical necessity;
- alternatives;
- competence;
- standard of care;
- monitoring;
- emergency context.

Experimental treatment may carry greater uncertainty without automatically becoming unlawful where authorship and safeguards are legitimate.

---

## 18. Employment

Work may involve hazardous activity.

> **Employment Consent != Unlimited Risk Acceptance.**

Employers/operators may have duties concerning:
- equipment;
- training;
- protective systems;
- known hazards;
- emergency procedures.

Economic dependency should not be used to manufacture unlimited consent to danger.

---

## 19. Commons and environmental risk

Risk can be distributed rather than individually targeted.

Examples:
- contamination;
- infrastructure failure;
- hazardous emissions;
- ecological damage;
- systemic resource risks.

> **No Identified Individual Victim != No Protected Interest.**

SR-BL01 provides standing for foreseeably affected participants.

Environmental law may implement specialised thresholds.

---

## 20. Infrastructure and systemic risk

A participant may create risk to many others by compromising an essential system.

Potential protected interests include:
- power;
- water;
- communications;
- transport;
- health systems;
- continuity systems.

> **Indirect Risk != Insignificant Risk.**

Scale may aggravate the seriousness even where no harm ultimately occurs.

---

## 21. Digital systems

Digital actions can create physical or continuity risk.

Examples may include unsafe control changes, disabling safeguards or exposing critical systems.

This document remains mechanism-neutral.

> **Digital Mechanism != Non-Physical Consequence.**

Technical detail belongs in specialised operational law/security architecture.

---

## 22. Risk to self

Self-regarding risk is primarily governed by autonomy.

> **Risk To Self != Automatic Endangerment Offence.**

Protective authority may arise where capacity is materially impaired or risk is exported to others.

This follows existing Mental Health Exceptional Authority and self-ownership principles.

---

## 23. Risk to others through self-regarding conduct

An activity that appears self-regarding may impose risk externally.

Example:
A participant chooses extreme intoxication, then operates dangerous machinery around others.

The legal issue is not the intoxication alone but the exported risk.

> **Autonomy Over Self != Authority To Impose Unconsented Serious Risk On Others.**

---

## 24. Emergency and rescue

Rescue may require creating secondary risk.

> **Risk Creation In Rescue != Automatically Reckless Endangerment.**

BCA applies:
- legitimate protective function;
- necessity;
- proportionality;
- available alternatives;
- expected harm reduction.

Perfect safety is not required.

---

## 25. Authority

Official authority may permit risk imposition for legitimate functions, but only within scope.

Examples:
- emergency evacuation;
- infrastructure shutdown;
- controlled demolition;
- hazardous rescue.

> **Authority To Act != Unlimited Authority To Create Risk.**

MNC applies.

---

## 26. Warning

Warning others of danger can reduce imposed risk.

But:

> **Warning != Automatic Permission To Create Any Risk.**

A sign saying "danger" does not legitimise an otherwise unjustifiable hazard.

Likewise:

> **Notice != Consent.**

---

## 27. Safeguards

Safeguards matter to both risk magnitude and culpability.

Relevant questions:
- Were known safeguards available?
- Were they proportionate?
- Were they deliberately disabled?
- Were they maintained?
- Was monitoring adequate?

> **Presence Of Safeguard != Automatic Safety.**

> **Absence Of Perfect Safeguard != Automatic Recklessness.**

---

## 28. Risk uncertainty

Novel systems may contain uncertain risks.

Uncertainty should not be treated as either zero risk or infinite risk.

> **Unknown Risk != No Risk.**

> **Uncertain Risk != Automatic Prohibition.**

BL10 supports monitoring, correction and adaptive safeguards.

---

## 29. Near misses

A near miss can be important evidence of risk.

> **Near Miss != No Event.**

But:

> **Near Miss != Automatic Proof Of Criminal Endangerment.**

It may reveal:
- hidden system weakness;
- underestimated probability;
- operator error;
- need for correction.

Historical/Research feedback should preserve learning.

---

## 30. Repeated low-level risk

Repeated individually modest risks may create a serious cumulative state.

This interfaces with the pattern-level architecture developed for harassment.

> **Individually Small Risks May Form A Material Cumulative Risk.**

Examples may include repeated safety bypasses or chronic exposure.

---

## 31. Risk transferred across boundaries

A participant may reduce risk inside their own context by exporting it elsewhere.

> **Risk Reduction Here != Legitimate Risk Transfer There.**

This interfaces with CBERRM and cross-boundary externality architecture.

---

## 32. Attempt

Endangerment is generally a completed risk offence once the legally sufficient danger is created or maintained.

Attempted endangerment may be possible where direct execution begins but the danger is prevented before exposure occurs.

However:

> **Attempted Endangerment != Attempted Harm.**

The intended legal object differs.

This should be used cautiously to avoid excessive offence multiplication.

---

## 33. Participation

The Participation and Complicity architecture applies.

Responsibility can include:
- principal risk creator;
- director;
- knowing assistant;
- knowing facilitator;
- culpable omission where a duty exists.

> **Working Within Risky System != Automatic Complicity In Endangerment.**

Individual knowledge, role and contribution matter.

---

## 34. Organisations

Organisational risk can emerge from distributed decisions.

No single participant may have authored the entire unsafe state.

This requires:
- role mapping;
- information flow;
- authority;
- ignored warnings;
- responsibility boundaries;
- systemic causes.

> **System Failure != Automatic Individual Guilt.**

But:

> **Distributed Responsibility != No Responsibility.**

Enterprise/legal-person architecture may be relevant.

---

## 35. Risk and outcome separation

A robust legal record should preserve both.

Example:

**Risk State:** substantial unjustified H5 risk  
**Actual Result:** H0/no injury

This avoids outcome bias.

Conversely:

**Risk State:** low justified risk  
**Actual Result:** H5 freak outcome

The outcome alone should not rewrite the prior risk state.

---

## 36. Aggravation

Potential aggravators include:
- very high risk;
- catastrophic potential;
- many exposed participants;
- deliberate safeguard disabling;
- repeated prior warnings;
- vulnerable populations;
- abuse of authority;
- concealment of risk.

Aggravation must remain evidenced.

---

## 37. Mitigation

Potential mitigation may include:
- emergency necessity;
- incomplete but reasonable information;
- genuine unforeseeable system behaviour;
- coercion;
- diminished capacity/authorship;
- prompt corrective action.

Correction does not erase the historical risk but may affect responsibility and continuing protection.

---

## 38. Remedy and prevention

Where harm has not yet occurred, remedy is often preventative.

Potential responses:
- stop hazardous activity;
- repair system;
- restore safeguards;
- notify exposed participants;
- evacuate/protect where necessary;
- retrain;
- correct procedures;
- monitor;
- compensate established losses from protective disruption where appropriate.

> **Prevention Of Harm Is A Legitimate Legal Outcome Even When No Injury Has Yet Occurred.**

---

## 39. Protective authority

A serious current danger may justify temporary protective intervention.

But:

> **Risk Finding != Unlimited Control Authority.**

Authority must remain:
- necessary;
- proportionate;
- scoped;
- reviewable;
- time-bounded.

When risk falls below the relevant threshold, risk-derived authority ends or requires another basis.

---

## 40. Historical record and learning

Endangerment events and near misses can be valuable system evidence.

Historical should preserve:
- event;
- known state at time;
- risk assessment;
- outcome;
- corrections;
- later learning.

> **Later Knowledge != Historical Knowledge At Time Of Decision.**

This protects against hindsight distortion.

## 42. Worked examples

### A. Reckless lethal risk, no injury

A knowingly disables a critical safety mechanism and operates dangerous equipment around B despite recognising a substantial unjustified risk of death. A separate automatic shutdown prevents injury.

**Result:** no H5 harm and no attempted killing absent intent to kill; serious reckless endangerment may nevertheless be complete.

### B. Ordinary transport risk

A drives competently within applicable safety rules. An unforeseeable component failure creates a near miss.

**Result:** danger occurred, but culpable endangerment is not established merely from the near miss.

### C. Boxing

A and B knowingly enter a properly bounded boxing context and accept defined physical risks.

**Result:** known danger is not automatically unlawful endangerment; consent, wrapper rules and safeguards matter.

### D. Risk exported from boxing context

A deliberately throws a dangerous object from the ring into spectators.

**Result:** spectator risk was not authored by participation in the boxing wrapper; separate endangerment analysis applies.

### E. Emergency rescue

A firefighter takes a calculated hazardous action that creates some secondary risk but is reasonably necessary to rescue trapped participants.

**Result:** risk creation may be justified under BCA.

### F. Workplace hazard

An operator knows a machine guard is dangerously defective but orders workers to continue because shutdown would reduce output.

**Result:** economic purpose does not automatically justify imposed serious risk; employment does not create unlimited risk consent.

### G. Environmental exposure

An enterprise knowingly releases a substance creating a serious risk to a downstream population, though no individual injury is yet proven.

**Result:** absence of a presently identified injured participant does not eliminate the protected-interest/endangerment question.

## 43. Development result

Endangerment fills a genuine logical gap in the emerging Concord legal grammar.

The resulting distinction is:

**Intent + Execution + No Intended Result → Attempt**

**Culpable Serious Risk + No Required Intent To Cause Result → Endangerment**

**Culpable Conduct + Actual Harm → Completed Harm Offence**

**Justified/Authored Risk + Proper Safeguards → Lawful Risk-Bearing Context**

This architecture also establishes that **risk itself can be a legally relevant state**, provided it is serious, evidenced and tied to a protected interest.

A natural next derivation is **damage to or interference with essential systems / public safety**, but much of that may be better represented as specialised applications of property interference, endangerment, infrastructure duties and commons stewardship rather than another fundamental offence. Before proliferating offences, the Law domain should now begin testing whether the derived base laws form a sufficiently compact general code and identify the next genuinely distinct protected interest.
