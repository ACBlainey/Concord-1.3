**Project:** The Concord Framework

**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / WORKED LEGAL DERIVATION / PROVISIONAL / NON-CANONICAL

## 1. Purpose

This document derives a candidate fundamental law governing threats, coercion and extortion.

Fraud corrupts consequential authorship primarily through materially false information.

Coercion corrupts consequential authorship primarily through illegitimate pressure.

Candidate structure:

**Threat / Pressure**  
→ **Protected Interest Placed At Risk**  
→ **Material Constraint On Choice**  
→ **Demand / Intended Disposition Or Conduct**  
→ **Authorship Impairment**  
→ **Responsibility**

The architecture is mechanism-neutral.

**Autonomy + Safety + Dignity + Peaceful Coexistence**

→ **No Participant May Intentionally Use Unlawful Or Otherwise Illegitimate Threatened Consequences To Compel Another's Protected Choice, Transfer, Conduct Or Submission**

## 3. Protected interest

The protected interest is not freedom from all pressure.

Civil life contains:
- requests;
- negotiation;
- persuasion;
- incentives;
- warnings;
- lawful consequences;
- competition;
- refusal;
- boundaries.

The protected interest is freedom from **illegitimate coercive pressure that materially displaces or corrupts authorship**.

> **Pressure != Automatically Coercion.**

---

## 4. Threat

A threat is a communicated or intentionally conveyed prospect of adverse consequence that the actor intends, knows or represents will follow from another participant's conduct or refusal.

Threats may be:
- explicit;
- implicit;
- verbal;
- written;
- behavioural;
- technological;
- communicated through an intermediary.

But:

> **Prediction Of Harm != Automatically Threat Of Harm.**

"I think the storm will kill you if you go outside" is different from "If you go outside, I will kill you."

---

## 5. Candidate base prohibition — unlawful threat

> **A participant must not, without lawful justification, intentionally or knowingly threaten another participant with unlawful harm, unlawful interference or another prohibited consequence where the threat is sufficiently credible or consequential to constitute a legally material interference with protected safety or autonomy.**

The threatened harm need not actually occur.

---

## 6. Candidate base prohibition — coercion

> **A participant must not intentionally or knowingly use an unlawful or otherwise illegitimate threatened consequence to materially compel another participant to act, refrain, submit, transfer, consent or surrender a protected interest contrary to meaningful authorship.**

Coercion focuses on pressure upon choice.

---

## 7. Candidate base prohibition — extortion

> **A participant must not intentionally or knowingly use an unlawful or otherwise illegitimate threat to obtain property, value, labour, access, information, permission, service, advantage or another protected disposition from another participant.**

Extortion can therefore be represented as:

**Coercive Threat**  
+ **Demand / Acquisition Purpose**  
+ **Protected Disposition**

rather than as an unrelated ethical category.

---

## 8. Threat without demand

A threat can be unlawful even where no demand is made.

Example:
A credibly tells B, without justification, that A intends to seriously injure B tomorrow.

There may be no extortion, but the threat itself can create a protected safety/autonomy harm.

> **No Demand != No Threat Offence.**

---

## 9. Demand without threat

A demand is not automatically coercive.

"Pay me the £20 you owe me" is a demand.

Its legality depends upon how it is pursued.

> **Demand != Threat.**

> **Demand != Coercion.**

---

## 10. Lawful consequences

Communicating a lawful consequence is not automatically coercion.

Examples:
- "If you do not pay the valid invoice, I may bring a lawful civil claim."
- "If you continue violating this safe-space rule, your access may be withdrawn."
- "If you do not meet the stated accreditation standard, certification cannot be issued."

> **Notice Of Lawful Consequence != Automatically Unlawful Threat.**

However, the claimed consequence must actually be legitimate.

False claims of legal authority may engage fraud/deception.

---

## 11. Lawful leverage can become abusive

A consequence may be lawful in isolation yet illegitimate when deliberately used outside its proper function to compel an unrelated surrender.

Example:
An official has lawful authority to conduct inspection A but threatens to misuse that authority unless a participant grants unrelated personal benefit B.

> **Possession Of Lawful Authority != Permission To Use It As Personal Leverage.**

This may engage abuse of authority, coercion, corruption or extortion.

---

## 12. Unattractive alternatives

A participant may face a difficult choice without another participant coercing them.

> **Unattractive Alternative != Automatically Coercion.**

Examples:
- accepting a lower-priced offer or declining it;
- taking a difficult lawful job because alternatives are limited;
- choosing between competing legitimate services.

The legal question is whether another actor has illegitimately created, controlled or threatened a prohibited consequence to displace authorship.

---

## 13. Circumstantial pressure

Natural scarcity, illness, weather, market conditions or other circumstances may constrain choice.

> **Constraint By Circumstance != Automatically Coercion By Participant.**

A participant can nevertheless exploit a vulnerable circumstance through independently unlawful threats or conduct.

---

## 14. Economic pressure

Economic pressure is not automatically unlawful.

Legitimate bargaining can involve consequences such as:
- refusing a deal;
- ending a lawful contract;
- changing a lawful offer;
- competing for business.

But economic leverage may become coercive where tied to an unlawful threat or illegitimate abuse of authority/dependency.

> **Economic Leverage != Automatic Extortion.**

---

## 15. Employment

An employer may set legitimate job requirements and consequences within the employment relationship.

But:

> **Employment Authority != General Coercive Authority.**

Threatening unlawful violence, detention, exposure of protected information or other prohibited consequences to compel labour may constitute coercion/forced labour.

This interfaces with the Slavery and Forced Labour architecture.

---

## 16. Consent under threat

Consent obtained through sufficiently serious illegitimate coercion may not represent valid authorship.

> **Submission Under Coercion != Necessarily Valid Consent.**

The Consent and Withdrawal architecture applies.

The analysis should ask:
- what consequence was threatened;
- whether it was legitimate;
- severity/credibility;
- connection to the requested choice;
- whether meaningful refusal remained.

---

## 17. Credibility

A threat need not be certain to be legally significant.

Relevant factors may include:
- apparent capability;
- specificity;
- immediacy;
- prior conduct;
- context;
- communicated access;
- authority position;
- vulnerability;
- objective plausibility.

But impossible or obviously unserious statements should not automatically receive the same classification as credible threats.

> **Words Alone != Automatic Equal Threat Severity.**

---

## 18. Conditional threats

Many threats are conditional:

"If you do X, I will do Y."

Conditional form does not remove threat status.

The question is whether Y is an unlawful or illegitimate consequence and whether the communication materially interferes with protected autonomy/safety.

---

## 19. Threat to third parties

Coercion may operate through threatened harm to another participant.

Example:
A threatens to injure B's child unless B transfers property.

> **Threatened Harm Need Not Target The Person Being Coerced.**

The protected decision authorship of B and safety of the third party may both be implicated.

---

## 20. Threat to property

Threatened property destruction may support coercion/extortion.

Example:
"Pay me or I destroy your vehicle."

The property offence may remain attempted/conditional while extortion can be independently analysed.

---

## 21. Threat of information disclosure

Threatening to disclose information raises difficult boundaries.

Disclosure may itself be:
- lawful;
- unlawful;
- privacy-prohibited;
- contractually restricted;
- public-interest protected.

Even a potentially lawful disclosure may raise extortion concerns where used as illegitimate leverage to obtain an unrelated protected benefit.

This requires careful future testing against whistleblowing, journalism and legitimate reporting.

> **Right To Report Wrongdoing != Right To Sell Silence Through Coercion.**

Equally:

> **Request For Settlement != Automatically Extortion.**

Lawful dispute settlement must remain possible.

---

## 22. Official coercion

Officials can coerce through misuse of state/civil authority.

Examples:
- threatening unlawful arrest;
- threatening fabricated prosecution;
- threatening withdrawal of unrelated lawful service;
- threatening misuse of licensing power.

> **Official Authority != Personal Leverage.**

Where authority is genuinely available for a legitimate function, its use must remain inside the BCA.

---

## 23. Coercion by control of necessities

Control over housing, food, healthcare, compute, communication or other essential resources can create powerful leverage.

Control alone is not automatically coercion.

But deliberate threatened deprivation may become coercive where:
- the participant has a protected entitlement;
- the threat violates contract/law;
- authority is abused;
- dependence is intentionally exploited through prohibited pressure.

> **Resource Control != General Authority Over Dependent Participant.**

---

## 24. Duress and offender responsibility

A participant may commit an offence because another coerces them.

The general mitigation/defence architecture should distinguish:
- coercer's responsibility;
- coerced actor's authorship/capacity;
- severity/immediacy of threat;
- available alternatives;
- harm caused.

> **Coerced Participation != Automatically Full Voluntary Authorship.**

But coercion does not automatically erase all responsibility in every case.

---

## 25. Threat and attempt

Threatening future harm is not identical to attempting the threatened harm.

> **Threat To Commit Offence != Automatically Attempt To Commit That Offence.**

Example:
A threatens to strike B next week but has not entered direct execution of the assault.

The threat offence may be complete while attempted physical harm is not.

This preserves the existing Attempt architecture.

---

## 26. Extortion and attempt

**Attempt Liability: YES**

If A communicates an extortionate threat and demands property but B refuses and transfers nothing, completed acquisition has failed but attempted extortion/coercive acquisition may be established.

Depending on the formulation, the threat component itself may already be complete.

---

## 27. Participation

The general Participation and Complicity architecture applies.

Roles may include:
- threat author;
- director;
- messenger who knowingly communicates the coercive threat;
- facilitator;
- recipient/controller of demanded benefit where knowing participation is established.

> **Delivering A Message != Automatic Complicity Without Required Knowledge/Authorship.**

---

## 28. Mental state

Threat/coercion offences ordinarily require intentional or knowing conduct.

Relevant findings may distinguish:
- intent to communicate threat;
- knowledge of threatened consequence;
- intent to influence choice;
- intent to obtain disposition;
- intent/knowledge concerning unlawfulness or factual circumstances.

Careless words may still cause fear, but should not automatically be classified as intentional coercion.

---

## 29. Joke, hyperbole and context

Context matters.

A statement may be:
- joke;
- metaphor;
- hyperbole;
- quoted material;
- fictional performance;
- genuine threat.

> **Literal Violent Words != Automatically Genuine Threat.**

The law should evaluate contextual meaning without treating subjective denial as conclusive.

CWA/contextual wrapper principles may assist.

---

## 30. Protected speech and disagreement

Criticism, disagreement, boycott, protest, refusal to associate and peaceful social disapproval are not automatically coercion.

> **Disapproval != Threat.**

> **Refusal To Associate != Automatically Coercion.**

But these freedoms do not authorise independently unlawful threats.

---

## 31. Automated threats

A coercive system can operate automatically.

Example:
A deliberately configures a system to send victims a demand and threaten destructive action unless payment is made.

> **Automation != Absence Of Authorship.**

Responsibility traces to participants who intentionally design, deploy, direct or knowingly facilitate the coercive system, subject to the Participation architecture.

---

## 32. AI-mediated coercion

AI may generate or communicate pressure.

Relevant questions include:
- participant status of AI;
- who supplied objective/prompt;
- who deployed system;
- foreseeability;
- knowledge;
- control;
- independent AI authorship where applicable.

> **Use Of AI != Automatic Transfer Of Responsibility To AI.**

Nor should AI automatically be treated as a mere tool if participant status and independent authorship are established.

---

## 33. Harm

Potential harms include:
- fear;
- loss of autonomy;
- property/value loss;
- compelled labour;
- privacy loss;
- physical/psychological harm;
- loss of opportunity;
- institutional corruption.

Threat severity, actual harm and obtained benefit should remain separately represented.

> **Threat Severity != Actual Harm Severity.**

---

## 34. Remedy

Potential remedies include:
- termination of coercive condition;
- return of extorted property/value;
- invalidation of coerced disposition where appropriate;
- restoration of access/status;
- protective measures;
- compensation for established harm;
- correction of records;
- support for affected participants.

> **Compliance With Demand != Validation Of Coercive Transaction.**

---

## 35. Aggravation

Potential aggravating factors may include:
- threat of death/catastrophic harm;
- threat against children/dependants/third parties;
- exploitation of substantial vulnerability;
- official/fiduciary authority abuse;
- repeated/systematic extortion;
- organised operation;
- multiple victims;
- severe actual consequences;
- deliberate isolation of victim from help.

The general Aggravation and Mitigation architecture applies.

## 37. Worked examples

### A. Direct extortion

A tells B: "Transfer your property to me or I will seriously injure you." B transfers it because the threat is credible.

**Result:** completed coercive/extortionate acquisition; property and threat harms may additionally apply.

### B. Failed extortion

A makes the same demand. B refuses and immediately reports it.

**Result:** threat may be complete; attempted extortion may apply even though no transfer occurred.

### C. Lawful civil warning

A tells B: "You owe the invoiced amount. If we cannot resolve it, I will bring a lawful civil claim."

**Result:** communication of a legitimate legal route is not automatically unlawful coercion.

### D. Abuse of office

Official A tells B that A will fabricate grounds for detention unless B provides a personal benefit.

**Result:** claimed/actual official capability is being used outside legitimate function; coercion/extortion and authority abuse may apply.

### E. Circumstantial hardship

A offers B difficult but lawful work during an economic downturn. B accepts because alternatives are poor.

**Result:** constrained choice does not alone establish coercion by A.

### F. Threat to third party

A threatens to harm C unless B supplies confidential information.

**Result:** coercion of B may exist although threatened harm targets C.

### G. Threat versus attempt

A threatens to damage B's property tomorrow unless paid, but takes no executory step toward damaging it.

**Result:** threat/extortion analysis may apply; attempted property damage does not automatically follow.

## 38. Development result

Threats, coercion and extortion can be derived as protection of consequential authorship against **illegitimate pressure**.

This creates a useful complement:

**Fraud:** authorship corrupted through materially false information.  
**Coercion:** authorship corrupted through illegitimate threatened consequence.

The law remains narrow enough to preserve:
- persuasion;
- bargaining;
- lawful warnings;
- contract enforcement;
- peaceful disagreement;
- refusal to associate;
- legitimate authority.

The next fundamental derivation should examine **privacy, intrusion and unauthorised observation/access**, because Safe Spaces, autonomy and contextual wrappers already provide a strong upstream ethical and architectural basis for it.
