## 1. Purpose

Consent can alter the legal meaning of conduct that would otherwise constitute prohibited interference.

It should therefore be represented as a reusable legal classifier rather than independently reinvented inside every offence.

But consent is not a magic word or unlimited waiver.

Candidate structure:

**Protected Interest**  
→ **Proposed Interference / Risk**  
→ **Participant Authorship**  
→ **Consent Validity**  
→ **Scope**  
→ **Context**  
→ **Continuing Consent / Withdrawal**  
→ **Conduct Inside Or Outside Consent Envelope**

## 2. Ethical provenance

Primary sources:
- **BL2 — Respect Autonomy**
- **BL3 — Preserve Safe Spaces**
- **BL4 — Act Honestly**
- **BL5 — Act Fairly**
- **BL7 — Respect Life and Dignity**
- **BL10 — Remain Open to Correction**

Consent operationalises autonomy but is bounded by the conditions required for genuine authorship.

> **Consent Is An Expression Of Autonomy, Not A Mechanism For Erasing Autonomy.**

---

## 3. Consent as legal state

Consent should be treated as a contextual legal state concerning a defined act, risk, relationship or bounded activity.

It is not an unlimited property possessed by another participant.

> **Consent Given != Ownership Of Future Choice.**

> **Consent To A Participant != Consent To Everything That Participant May Do.**

---

## 4. Candidate validity requirements

Valid consent ordinarily requires sufficient:

1. **authorship** — the choice is genuinely attributable to the participant;
2. **capacity** — sufficient decision capacity for the relevant choice;
3. **information** — sufficient material understanding for the decision;
4. **voluntariness** — absence of coercion or other conditions defeating meaningful choice;
5. **specificity/scope** — the consent is connected to identifiable conduct, risk or context;
6. **continuity** — consent has not been withdrawn or materially invalidated by changed conditions.

These are not necessarily binary in every civil context, but consequential interference requires stronger confidence in them.

---

## 5. Information and understanding

Perfect information is impossible and should not be required.

The question is whether material information necessary for meaningful authorship was available and sufficiently understood.

> **Incomplete Knowledge != Automatically Invalid Consent.**

But:

> **Material Deception May Defeat Consent.**

> **Information Dump != Informed Consent.**

Disclosure should be usable rather than merely technically present.

Known material risks, constraints and relevant alternatives may need to be communicated according to context.

---

## 6. Voluntariness

Consent obtained through coercion is not genuine authorship.

Relevant pressures may include:
- threats;
- unlawful force;
- blackmail;
- abuse of dependency;
- abuse of entrusted authority;
- deliberately engineered inability to refuse;
- other coercive conditions.

Not every incentive or unequal circumstance destroys voluntariness.

> **Influence != Automatically Coercion.**

The question is whether the participant retained meaningful authorship of the choice.

---

## 7. Capacity is decision-specific

Capacity should not be treated as one permanent global attribute.

A participant may have capacity for one decision and lack it for another more complex or consequential decision.

> **Capacity For Decision A != Automatic Capacity For Decision B.**

Mental-health diagnosis alone does not determine consent capacity.

The existing capacity and Mental Health architectures remain applicable.

---

## 8. Scope

Consent applies only within its legitimate scope.

Example:

Consent to a medical examination does not automatically authorise unrelated procedures.

Consent to boxing does not authorise an attack after the bout.

Consent to enter a property does not necessarily authorise access to every nested space.

Therefore:

> **Consent To Context != Consent To Every Act Within Context.**

> **Consent To One Act != Consent To Another Act.**

> **Consent To Risk != Consent To Unlimited Harm.**

---

## 9. Contextual wrappers

Contextual Wrapper Architecture can represent the bounded environment in which consent operates.

Candidate:

**Participant Consent**  
+ **Wrapper Rules**  
+ **Temporal Boundary**  
+ **Spatial / Functional Boundary**  
+ **Known Risk Envelope**  
= **Consent Envelope Candidate**

But wrapper legibility does not itself establish consent.

> **Visible Rules != Consent.**

> **Entry Into Wrapper != Unlimited Waiver.**

The participant must still meaningfully author participation where consent is legally required.

---

## 10. Entry and continuation

A participant may validly choose to enter an activity, relationship or context and later choose not to continue.

> **Authorship Of Entry != Irrevocable Authorship Of Continuation.**

> **Past Consent != Continuing Consent.**

This applies particularly to continuing physical interaction, treatment, participation, access arrangements and other ongoing relationships.

A legal system should not convert an earlier autonomous choice into authority to override a later autonomous choice without an independent legal basis.

---

## 11. Withdrawal

Where consent is legally withdrawable, withdrawal changes the authority state prospectively.

Candidate transition:

**Valid Consent**  
→ **Permitted Conduct Within Envelope**  
→ **Consent Withdrawn**  
→ **Consent-Derived Permission Terminates**  
→ **Further Interference Requires Another Legitimate Basis**

Therefore:

> **Withdrawal Of Consent != Retroactive Invalidity Of Earlier Consented Conduct.**

and:

> **Earlier Consent != Authority For Later Conduct After Withdrawal.**

---

## 12. Communication of withdrawal

Withdrawal should not depend on ritual wording.

It may be communicated through words or sufficiently clear conduct where the other participant can reasonably recognise it.

The evidential question is whether withdrawal was communicated or otherwise reasonably knowable in the circumstances.

> **No Particular Phrase Is Required For Withdrawal.**

Where communication is impossible or impaired, context-specific safeguards may be required.

---

## 13. Opportunity to stop

Some conduct cannot cease instantaneously.

Where consent is withdrawn during an ongoing process, the actor should cease as soon as reasonably and safely possible.

> **Withdrawal Requires Cessation As Soon As Reasonably Practicable, Not Physical Impossibility.**

This prevents both abuse of delay and unrealistic standards.

---

## 14. Changed state and reauthorisation

A materially changed activity may exceed the original consent even without an explicit withdrawal.

> **Authorship Of State A != Automatic Authorship Of Materially Different State B.**

Examples:
- procedure changes materially;
- risk increases materially;
- participant changes;
- purpose changes;
- context changes;
- duration extends beyond agreed bounds.

Material change may require renewed consent.

---

## 15. Consent under deception

Deception should be analysed according to whether it concerns a fact material to authorship of the protected interest.

Not every lie invalidates every interaction.

Candidate test:

**Deception**  
→ **Material To Decision?**  
→ **Did It Prevent Meaningful Authorship Of The Relevant Consent?**

Therefore:

> **Deception != Automatic Invalidity Of Every Consent.**

But deliberate deception about the nature of the act, material risk, identity where legally material, or another foundational condition may defeat consent.

---

## 16. Consent and prohibited acts

Some legal protections may be non-waivable or only partly waivable.

A participant's willingness does not automatically legitimise every possible act.

Potential reasons include:
- inability to author the relevant consequence;
- effects on non-consenting third parties;
- externalities;
- commons harm;
- independently prohibited exploitation;
- legitimate protection of future opportunity;
- other Ethical Kernel constraints.

Therefore:

> **Consent Of Participants != Consent Of Non-Participants.**

> **Consent != Automatic Authority To Export Harm.**

The scope of non-waivable protections requires separate derivation rather than assumption.

---

## 17. Consent and dangerous activity

Concord already recognises that participants may autonomously accept bounded risk.

Examples include sport, dangerous employment and other authored-risk contexts.

The relevant question is not whether risk exists but whether it was legitimately authored within a bounded context.

> **Known Risk != Prohibited Risk.**

> **Voluntary Risk != Unlimited Risk Transfer.**

The Authored Risk Acceptance architecture should govern this interface.

---

## 18. Consent and employment

Employment or contract does not create general consent to harm, surveillance, control or risk.

> **Consent To Employment != General Waiver Of Rights.**

Specific occupational risks may be knowingly and voluntarily accepted within legitimate limits, but employer authority remains functionally and contextually bounded.

Economic dependency may be relevant when assessing whether apparent consent was genuinely voluntary.

---

## 19. Consent and medicine

Medical consent is a particularly consequential application.

It may require:
- decision-specific capacity;
- material information;
- treatment purpose;
- meaningful alternatives where available;
- known material risks;
- scope;
- ability to withdraw where practicable.

Emergency or diminished-capacity treatment without consent requires independent authority; absence of consent does not itself create that authority.

> **Need For Treatment != Consent.**

> **Need For Treatment != Automatic Authority.**

---

## 20. Consent and evidence

Consent may be explicit or, where legally appropriate, inferred from sufficiently clear conduct and context.

But inference must not be used to manufacture consent from ambiguity.

> **Silence != Automatic Consent.**

> **Failure To Resist != Automatic Consent.**

> **Prior Relationship != Automatic Consent.**

The more consequential the interference, the stronger the evidential confidence ordinarily required.

---

## 21. Consent and burden of proof

The evidential burden concerning consent may differ by legal context and remains a Judiciary/procedure question.

However no architecture should presume consent merely because proving internal state is difficult.

Records, wrappers, contemporaneous communications and conduct may provide evidence.

The legal system should preserve uncertainty rather than convert missing evidence into invented authorship.

---

## 22. Consent cannot create another participant's authority beyond scope

Consent may permit another participant to perform defined conduct.

It does not automatically grant them a general governance power over the consenting participant.

> **Permission != General Authority.**

> **Consent-Derived Authority Is Bounded By The Consent That Created It.**

If the consent terminates, consent-derived authority ordinarily terminates with it unless another independent authority exists.

---

## 23. Emergency transition

A consensual relationship may transition into an emergency state.

Example:
A participant consents to an activity, loses capacity, and develops an immediate serious threat to life.

The legal basis may transition from consent to emergency protective/medical authority.

The system should record that transition rather than pretending consent continued.

> **Change Of Authority Source Must Be Legible.**

---

## 24. Candidate consent states

A reusable schema may include:

**VALID / ACTIVE** — sufficiently authored consent currently applies.

**WITHDRAWN** — previously valid consent has been prospectively terminated.

**EXPIRED** — temporal/contextual scope ended.

**EXCEEDED** — conduct moved outside the consent envelope.

**INVALID** — required conditions for valid consent were absent.

**UNCERTAIN / CONTESTED** — evidence does not presently establish the state.

**NOT REQUIRED** — the relevant lawful conduct does not depend upon participant consent.

**OVERRIDDEN BY INDEPENDENT AUTHORITY** should be avoided as phrasing where possible: the cleaner representation is **CONSENT ABSENT/INAPPLICABLE + SEPARATE AUTHORITY SOURCE**, so the legal system does not imply that consent itself was transformed.

## 26. Development result

Consent is viable as a reusable legal state/classifier.

Its validity depends on sufficient authorship, capacity, information, voluntariness, scope and continuity.

Most importantly, consent remains dynamic:

**Consent can be given, bounded, exceeded, withdrawn, expire or require renewal after material state change.**

The architecture therefore preserves autonomy across time rather than treating one historical choice as permanent authority.

The next reusable Law layer should examine **aggravation and mitigation as general consequence modifiers**, while keeping complete defences, mental-health routing, actual harm and base offence classification separate.
