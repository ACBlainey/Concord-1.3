## 1. Purpose

This document derives candidate law protecting commons, ecological systems and shared future opportunity.

The problem cannot be reduced entirely to private property.

A river, atmosphere, habitat, aquifer, ecosystem, finite resource stock or other common system can be materially degraded even where:
- no single participant owns it;
- no single participant can yet prove individual injury;
- damage is distributed;
- consequences occur later;
- many individually small actions accumulate.

The protected interest is:

> **The Continuing Functional Integrity Of Legitimate Commons And The Fair Preservation Of Future Opportunity To Benefit From Them.**

## 3. Foundational distinction

> **Commons != Unowned Property Available For Unbounded Capture.**

A commons may be:
- publicly held;
- collectively held;
- distributed;
- unowned in ordinary property terms;
- managed by several jurisdictions;
- physically mobile;
- ecologically interconnected.

Its legal protection arises from stewardship and shared dependency, not merely title.

---

## 4. Protected interest

The protected interest has at least four dimensions:

1. **Functional integrity** — the system remains capable of performing relevant ecological/civil functions.
2. **Resource continuity** — use does not unjustifiably destroy future availability.
3. **Shared access/opportunity** — one participant's use does not illegitimately extinguish others' reasonable opportunity.
4. **Recovery capacity** — damage does not unnecessarily destroy resilience or regeneration.

> **Present Use != Ownership Of Future Availability.**

---

## 5. Candidate base prohibition

> **A participant must not, without lawful justification or other independently legitimate basis, intentionally, knowingly, recklessly, or where a legally specified stewardship duty applies negligently, materially degrade, contaminate, destroy, exhaust or otherwise impair a protected common resource or ecological system beyond the level legitimately permitted by its sustainable function, allocation and stewardship architecture.**

Exact environmental thresholds belong to specialised implementation.

---

## 6. No individually identified victim required

A commons offence may exist before direct personal injury is established.

> **No Identified Individual Victim != No Protected Legal Interest.**

Examples:
- poisoning an aquifer before anyone drinks from it;
- destroying a habitat before dependent participants experience downstream loss;
- exhausting a shared reserve;
- releasing persistent contamination.

Individual harm, if later established, may create additional offences/remedies.

---

## 7. Commons harm versus endangerment

Endangerment protects against serious imposed risk.

Commons harm protects the common system itself.

They can coexist.

Example:

**Toxic discharge**  
→ **Aquifer degradation** = Commons harm  
→ **Serious risk to users** = Endangerment  
→ **Actual illness** = Physical harm

> **One Event May Violate Several Distinct Protected Interests Without Those Interests Becoming Identical.**

---

## 8. Commons harm versus property harm

Property law protects legitimate property relations.

Commons law protects shared systems/opportunity.

> **Absence Of Private Ownership != Absence Of Legal Protection.**

Conversely:

> **Private Ownership != Unlimited Authority To Destroy Ecological Externalities.**

Property rights remain bounded by externalities and stewardship.

---

## 9. Use versus degradation

Use of a commons is not inherently harmful.

> **Resource Use != Resource Abuse.**

Legitimate use may include:
- water withdrawal;
- fishing;
- forestry;
- recreation;
- energy extraction;
- waste assimilation within safe limits;
- scientific use.

The question is whether use remains within legitimate regenerative, allocative and externality boundaries.

---

## 10. Sustainable yield

Renewable systems may support ongoing use below recovery/regeneration thresholds.

> **Renewable != Infinite.**

A mature implementation should use evidence-based limits reflecting:
- regeneration;
- uncertainty;
- seasonal variation;
- system interactions;
- cumulative use.

---

## 11. Finite resources

Non-renewable commons require different stewardship.

The aim cannot always be literal preservation forever.

Relevant considerations may include:
- necessity;
- alternatives;
- efficiency;
- recycling/recovery;
- future need;
- strategic reserves;
- fair allocation.

> **Stewardship != Prohibition On All Consumption.**

---

## 12. Future opportunity

BL8 protects more than current users.

> **Present Participants != Sole Beneficiaries Of Commons Stewardship.**

Future participants cannot presently consent to irreversible depletion.

This does not grant hypothetical future persons unlimited veto power.

It creates a stewardship duty to avoid unnecessary destruction of future option space.

---

## 13. Intergenerational fairness

Candidate principle:

> **Current Benefit Should Not Unnecessarily Externalise Irreversible Cost Onto Future Participants.**

This requires proportionality, not preservation of every present state unchanged.

---

## 14. Baselines

The Environment Domain already distinguishes baseline from ideal.

> **Environmental Baseline != Ethical Ideal.**

A degraded historical state does not become ethically preferred merely because it is old.

Likewise:

> **Change From Baseline != Automatically Harm.**

Some ecological change is natural or beneficial.

---

## 15. Measurement

Environmental legal states depend heavily on measurement.

Relevant architecture:
- baseline provenance;
- method consistency;
- uncertainty;
- missing data;
- sensor calibration;
- temporal comparability.

> **Measurement Change != Environmental Change.**

---

## 16. Uncertainty

Ecological systems contain uncertainty.

> **Uncertainty != No Risk.**

But:

> **Uncertainty != Automatic Prohibition.**

Decision architecture should consider:
- plausible severity;
- reversibility;
- evidence quality;
- monitoring;
- adaptive controls;
- recovery options.

---

## 17. Precaution

A bounded precautionary approach may be justified for high-severity irreversible risks.

But:

> **Precaution != Unlimited Authority To Ban Anything Uncertain.**

MNC and proportionality remain applicable.

---

## 18. Cumulative harm

Commons degradation often emerges from individually modest contributions.

> **Individually Small Contribution != No Cumulative Harm.**

This creates a difficult responsibility problem.

Legal architecture must distinguish:
- aggregate system threshold;
- individual contribution;
- allocated permission;
- knowledge;
- controllability;
- alternatives.

---

## 19. Contribution versus sole causation

> **Not Sole Cause != No Responsibility.**

But:

> **Small Contribution != Automatic Responsibility For Entire Aggregate Harm.**

Responsibility should track contribution, duty and mental state.

---

## 20. Distributed lawful use becoming aggregate harm

Many participants may each act within existing permissions while total use becomes unsustainable.

This may reveal a governance/allocation failure rather than individual criminality.

> **System-Level Overuse != Automatic Individual Guilt Where Participants Complied With Legitimate Known Limits.**

The correct response may be:
- update limits;
- revise allocation;
- monitor;
- transition;
- compensate reliance where appropriate.

---

## 21. Permit

A permit can authorise bounded use.

> **Permit != Ownership Of Commons.**

> **Permit != Permission To Exceed Its Scope.**

> **Permit != Proof That Underlying Rule Can Never Be Wrong.**

If new evidence shows serious danger, rules may need prospective correction subject to lawful transition.

---

## 22. Retroactivity

Participants should not be punished retrospectively because later science changes the understanding of previously permitted conduct.

> **Later Knowledge != Earlier Culpability.**

Historical state and knowledge-at-time must remain visible.

Continuing conduct after warning/reclassification can enter a new legal state.

---

## 23. Externalities

A participant may obtain private benefit while exporting ecological cost.

> **Private Benefit != Authority To Externalise Unconsented Commons Harm.**

CBERRM applies.

Externality recognition does not automatically create centralised authority; it creates standing and a need for legitimate resolution.

---

## 24. Cross-boundary systems

Air, water, species and ecological processes cross administrative boundaries.

> **Administrative Boundary != Ecological Boundary.**

Interaction may require interoperability, shared measurement and negotiated stewardship.

But:

> **Externality != Automatic Jurisdiction.**

Authority still requires legitimate derivation.

---

## 25. Pollution

Pollution should be defined functionally, not merely as presence of a substance.

Potential dimensions:
- concentration;
- persistence;
- toxicity;
- location;
- duration;
- cumulative load;
- ecological effect.

> **Substance Present != Automatically Pollution.**

Likewise naturally occurring material can become harmful through concentration/context.

---

## 26. Waste

Waste disposal is not inherently prohibited.

Stewardship should prefer:
- reuse;
- repair;
- disassembly;
- recovery;
- recycling;
- safe final containment.

This connects to Concord product-lifecycle architecture.

> **Post-Use Material != Automatically Valueless Waste.**

---

## 27. Product lifecycle

Manufacturers/designers may acquire stewardship duties where product design predictably determines downstream recoverability or hazardous waste.

Exact duties belong to Economy/Commerce/Standards.

> **Sale != Automatic End Of Every Stewardship Responsibility.**

But producer responsibility must remain bounded and promulgated.

---

## 28. Habitat

Habitat can have protected ecological function even where no participant owns it.

Relevant factors:
- biodiversity;
- system function;
- rarity;
- recovery time;
- dependency;
- alternatives.

> **No Human Occupant != No Protected Ecological Interest.**

---

## 29. Non-participant life

BL7 and BL8 may support protection of biological life/ecosystems without requiring every organism to have participant status.

> **Ecological Protection != Participant Status For Every Organism.**

This avoids collapsing environmental stewardship into personhood doctrine.

---

## 30. Species loss

Irreversible extinction can represent extreme loss of future ecological option space.

> **Irreversibility Can Increase Stewardship Significance.**

Exact protection levels require Environment/Research evidence.

---

## 31. Invasive species and ecological intervention

Preserving ecological integrity may sometimes require removing organisms or deliberately changing a system.

Therefore:

> **Environmental Protection != Never Intervene.**

The protected interest is system stewardship, not freezing every organism/state in place.

---

## 32. Restoration

Restoration can be legitimate but should not assume a single historical date is the only valid target.

> **Restoration != Automatic Return To Arbitrary Historical Snapshot.**

Desired state should derive from function, resilience, biodiversity, human/participant needs and evidence.

---

## 33. Climate and atmosphere

Atmospheric systems are paradigmatic commons.

Individual contributions can be globally distributed and cumulative.

This strongly supports:
- measurement;
- aggregate allocation;
- sector rules;
- technological transition;
- cross-boundary coordination.

It does not justify attributing entire system harm to every individual emitter.

---

## 34. Water

Water can simultaneously be:
- common resource;
- allocated resource;
- private possession after lawful extraction;
- essential civil need;
- ecological substrate.

> **Resource Classification Can Change With Context Without Erasing Source Stewardship.**

---

## 35. Land

Land may be privately owned while embedded in wider ecological systems.

Property rights can coexist with:
- water duties;
- pollution limits;
- habitat rules;
- externality constraints.

> **Ownership Of Land != Ownership Of Every External Consequence Originating From It.**

---

## 36. Resource allocation

Scarce commons require fair allocation.

Possible criteria:
- essential need;
- sustainable yield;
- prior legitimate reliance;
- efficiency;
- alternatives;
- public/common benefit.

> **First Capture != Automatic Permanent Priority.**

Allocation architecture should resist conversion of early access into permanent domination.

---

## 37. Economic value

Market price can inform but not exhaust commons value.

> **Market Price != Complete Measure Of Ecological Value.**

Relevant non-market values include:
- resilience;
- option value;
- system services;
- cultural value;
- future opportunity.

---

## 38. Stewardship and poverty

Environmental law should not externalise transition burdens arbitrarily onto participants with least capacity.

> **Stewardship != Permission For Unfair Burden Transfer.**

BL5 applies to transition design.

---

## 39. Emergency use

Emergency conditions may justify temporary extraordinary resource use.

BCA applies.

> **Emergency Need != Permanent Commons Claim.**

After emergency ends, emergency-derived authority ends or requires new basis.

---

## 40. Research

Research may require sampling/intervention.

Legitimate research does not automatically override ecological safeguards.

> **Research Value != Unlimited Stewardship Exemption.**

ESCP applies to discovery methods.

---

## 41. Cultural use

Commons may have cultural significance.

Culture is relevant but does not automatically authorise severe irreversible ecological harm.

> **Tradition != Automatic Stewardship Exemption.**

Nor should stewardship erase legitimate cultural use without proportional analysis.

---

## 42. Stewardship duty holders

Potential duty holders include:
- public institutions;
- commons managers;
- resource operators;
- enterprises;
- permit holders;
- infrastructure operators;
- participants who create specific dangers.

Not every participant bears identical operational duties.

> **Shared Benefit != Identical Stewardship Duty.**

Positive Duties/Omissions architecture determines scope.

---

## 43. Standing

Foreseeably affected participants should have standing to raise concerns.

But:

> **Standing != Authority To Decide.**

This preserves the general Concord distinction.

Future participants require institutional stewardship/representation mechanisms rather than fictional direct consent.

---

## 44. Representation of commons

A commons may require a guardian/steward function for legal proceedings.

Such guardian:

> **Represents Protected Stewardship Interest != Owns The Commons.**

Guardian authority must be bounded and reviewable.

---

## 45. Evidence

Evidence may include:
- sensor records;
- samples;
- ecological surveys;
- models;
- historical baselines;
- causal studies;
- operational records;
- permits;
- emissions/resource-use data.

Models should remain distinguishable from observations.

> **Model Output != Direct Observation.**

---

## 46. Causation

Environmental causation can be probabilistic/distributed.

The legal standard must avoid both:
- impossible demand for single-source certainty; and
- assigning aggregate harm without evidence of contribution.

> **Distributed Causation Requires Attribution Architecture, Not Abandonment Of Causation.**

---

## 47. Intentional degradation

Examples:
- deliberate dumping;
- deliberate habitat destruction;
- intentional disabling of pollution controls.

Mental state should remain explicit.

---

## 48. Knowing degradation

A participant may know their conduct will materially degrade a commons even if degradation is not their purpose.

Purpose and knowledge remain distinct.

---

## 49. Reckless degradation

Recklessness requires conscious disregard of substantial unjustified ecological/common risk.

> **Environmental Harm Result != Automatic Recklessness.**

---

## 50. Negligent stewardship

Negligent liability requires a legitimate duty/standard.

Examples:
- permit-holder monitoring;
- hazardous containment;
- infrastructure maintenance;
- professional environmental controls.

> **Environmental Responsibility != Unlimited Negligence Duty For Everyone.**

---

## 51. Accident

Accidental ecological damage can occur without culpability.

Emergency remediation may still be required.

> **Duty To Remediate != Automatic Admission Of Criminal Fault.**

Created-danger duties can arise even where initial event was non-culpable.

---

## 52. Attempt

**Attempt Liability: YES / CONDITIONAL.**

Direct execution intended to materially degrade a protected commons may constitute attempt if intervention prevents the degradation.

Mere planning remains preparation.

---

## 53. Endangerment

Where conduct creates substantial serious ecological risk but damage has not materialised, Endangerment may apply.

Specialised environmental precautionary rules may intervene earlier where justified.

---

## 54. Participation

General participation/complicity applies.

> **Employment By Polluting Enterprise != Automatic Personal Liability.**

Responsibility traces role, knowledge, authority and contribution.

---

## 55. Enterprise responsibility

Enterprises may be major commons actors.

Enterprise legal-person architecture should permit responsibility to attach to the organisation without automatically diffusing guilt to all members.

> **Organisational Scale != Responsibility Vacuum.**

---

## 56. Remedy hierarchy

Environmental remedy should prioritise where feasible:

1. stop ongoing harm;
2. contain;
3. restore function;
4. remediate;
5. replace/reconstruct where possible;
6. compensate unavoidable residual loss;
7. prevent recurrence;
8. monitor recovery.

> **Fine Paid != Commons Restored.**

Punishment and restoration remain distinct.

---

## 57. Irreversible harm

Some ecological loss cannot be repaired.

> **Irreversibility Increases Need For Prevention; It Does Not Create Unlimited Punitive Authority.**

Consequences remain proportionate and function-specific.

---

## 58. Offset

An offset may sometimes compensate equivalent ecological function, but should not become automatic permission to destroy unique systems.

> **Compensation Elsewhere != Automatic Equivalence.**

Equivalence must be demonstrated.

---

## 59. Financial compensation

Money may compensate participants but cannot literally recreate extinct species or lost ecological function.

> **Price != Restoration.**

Financial remedy is only one output.

---

## 60. Monitoring and sunset

Protective restrictions should be reviewed as evidence changes.

> **Past Environmental Risk != Permanent Authority Without Current Justification.**

BL10 requires correction in both directions:
- strengthen protection when risk was underestimated;
- relax restrictions when evidence no longer supports them.

## 62. Worked examples

### A. Deliberate toxic discharge

Enterprise A knowingly discharges a persistent toxin into a protected aquifer to avoid treatment costs.

**Result:** commons degradation may be complete even before a particular injured participant is identified. Endangerment/property/physical-harm states may additionally arise.

### B. Lawful use becomes unsustainable

Many users withdraw water within existing legitimate allocations, but new evidence shows the aggregate allocation exceeds recharge.

**Result:** system-level stewardship failure does not automatically create retrospective individual guilt. Allocation should be corrected prospectively with lawful transition.

### C. Private land pollution

A owns land but releases contamination that migrates into shared groundwater.

**Result:** land ownership does not create authority over exported ecological consequences.

### D. Accidental spill

A compliant operator suffers a genuinely unforeseeable equipment failure causing contamination and immediately activates containment/remediation.

**Result:** commons harm may exist while criminal culpability does not automatically follow. Remediation duty can still arise.

### E. Habitat destruction prevented

A intentionally begins direct execution of an unlawful plan to destroy a protected habitat, but intervention stops the machinery before damage occurs.

**Result:** attempted commons degradation may be established if the required intent/execution threshold is met.

### F. Restoration choice

A degraded wetland has changed substantially over centuries. Restoration planners choose a resilient functional state rather than replicate an arbitrary historical year.

**Result:** restoration need not mean freezing or reproducing one historical snapshot.

## 63. Development result

BL8 creates a genuinely distinct legal interest.

Commons protection cannot be fully reduced to:
- private property;
- individual physical harm;
- endangerment.

The protected object is:

# **Continuing Shared Ecological/Resource Function And Fair Future Opportunity.**

The resulting legal chain is:

**Commons / Ecological System**  
→ **Protected Function / Resource / Future Opportunity**  
→ **Baseline + Current State + Uncertainty**  
→ **Use / Contribution / Externality**  
→ **Permission / Allocation / Duty**  
→ **Mental State**  
→ **Degradation / Risk / Cumulative Effect**  
→ **Attribution**  
→ **Remediation + Restoration + Accountability + Review**

With this derivation, the three major protected-interest gaps identified in the Compact Basic Law Coverage Audit have now been addressed:
1. Entrusted/Fiduciary Integrity;
2. Fair Comparable Treatment;
3. Commons/Ecological Integrity.

The remaining high-value work is primarily **legal grammar and institutional process**, especially:
- preparatory participation (solicitation/incitement/conspiracy);
- legitimate civil/judicial process integrity;
- promulgation/versioning and non-retroactivity;
- mapping named conventional offences onto the compact derived code.
