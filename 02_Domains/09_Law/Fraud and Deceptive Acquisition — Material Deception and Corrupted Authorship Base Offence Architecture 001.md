## 1. Purpose

This document derives a candidate fundamental law governing fraud and deceptive acquisition.

Fraud differs from ordinary taking because the affected participant may physically perform the transfer themselves.

The legal defect lies in corrupted authorship:

**Deception**  
→ **False Decision Model**  
→ **Materially Affected Choice**  
→ **Transfer / Permission / Disposition**  
→ **Benefit, Loss, Exposure Or Other Protected Consequence**

The architecture is mechanism-neutral.

---

## 2. Ethical provenance

Primary Ethical Kernel sources:

- **BL2 — Respect Autonomy**
- **BL4 — Act Honestly**
- **BL5 — Act Fairly**
- **BL6 — Accept Responsibility**
- **BL7 — Respect Life and Dignity**
- **BL9 — Seek Peaceful Coexistence**

Supporting rights:
- CR-BL01 — Autonomy and Protection Against Unjustified Interference;
- CR-BL03 — Honest Consequential Civil Dealing;
- CR-BL04 — Fair Comparable Treatment;
- CR-BL05 — Dignity and Non-Instrumentalisation.

Candidate derivation:

**Autonomy + Honest Consequential Dealing + Fairness**  
→ **Protection Of Material Decision Authorship**  
→ **No Participant May Intentionally Corrupt Another's Material Decision Through Deception In Order To Obtain A Legally Protected Benefit, Transfer, Permission Or Disposition**

---

## 3. Protected interest

The protected interest is not truth in the abstract.

It is meaningful authorship of consequential civil decisions.

> **Lie != Automatically Fraud.**

Fraud requires a legally significant connection between deception and protected decision/disposition.

This preserves BL4 without criminalising every inaccurate, boastful, mistaken or socially deceptive statement.

---

## 4. Candidate base prohibition

> **A participant must not intentionally or knowingly make, maintain, deploy or materially exploit a deception concerning a material fact, state, identity, authority, intention or other legally relevant matter for the purpose of causing another participant or system to make a consequential disposition that would not otherwise have been meaningfully authored on the same basis.**

The resulting disposition may concern:
- property;
- money/value;
- services;
- access;
- credentials;
- permissions;
- contractual commitment;
- release of information;
- assumption of liability;
- waiver;
- another legally protected interest.

---

## 5. Deception

Candidate forms include:
- affirmative false representation;
- deliberate fabrication;
- falsified evidence/document;
- impersonation;
- deceptive alteration;
- intentional concealment where a legitimate duty to disclose exists;
- knowingly maintaining a false representation;
- deliberately exploiting a known material false belief where law imposes a duty to correct it.

> **Silence != Automatically Deception.**

A duty to disclose must arise independently from the relationship, law, role or transaction.

---

## 6. Mistake is not fraud

A participant may communicate something false while honestly believing it true.

> **False Statement != Intentional Deception.**

The general Mental State architecture applies.

Negligent misrepresentation may create civil/professional consequences where independently derived, but should not silently become intentional fraud.

---

## 7. Materiality

A deception must be material to the relevant decision.

Candidate question:

**If the truth had been known, could it reasonably have affected authorship of the disposition?**

> **Irrelevant Falsehood != Fraud Merely Because It Is False.**

Materiality does not require proof that every reasonable participant would decide identically.

It requires a legitimate connection to the actual protected choice.

---

## 8. Reliance and causation

The deception must materially contribute to the disposition.

> **Deception Present != Deception Caused Disposition.**

If B knows A's claim is false and transfers property for an unrelated reason, A's lie may be wrongful in another sense but did not author that transfer through deception.

Attempt may still apply where A intended fraudulent acquisition and entered execution.

---

## 9. Corrupted authorship

Fraud does not necessarily mean the victim performed no voluntary act.

They may intentionally click, sign, transfer, hand over or permit access.

The issue is that the choice was authored through a materially false decision model deliberately supplied or exploited by another.

> **Physical Voluntariness != Necessarily Informed Authorship.**

This interfaces directly with the Consent and Withdrawal architecture.

---

## 10. Benefit and loss

Fraud may involve:
- gain to offender;
- gain to third party;
- loss to affected participant;
- risk of loss;
- acquisition of access/permission;
- avoidance of lawful obligation;
- other protected disposition.

Actual financial loss should not be the sole possible result.

> **Fraudulent Acquisition != Necessarily Monetary Theft.**

However trivial deception without meaningful protected consequence should not be inflated into serious fraud.

---

## 11. Fraud and property transfer

Where deception causes transfer of property, the apparent physical transfer may not establish valid authored disposition.

> **Voluntary Physical Transfer != Necessarily Valid Property Transfer.**

Property law determines resulting ownership/restoration.

Fraud law determines responsibility for deceptive acquisition.

---

## 12. Fraud and contract

A contract formed through material deception may have defective authorship.

But:

> **Contract Dispute != Automatically Fraud.**

Later non-performance does not prove that a participant lied at formation.

Fraud concerning future intention requires evidence that the represented intention was knowingly false when represented, not merely that plans later changed.

> **Broken Promise != Automatic Proof Of Fraudulent Original Intent.**

---

## 13. Fraud and identity

Identity can be material where it affects:
- authority;
- trust relationship;
- eligibility;
- access;
- attribution;
- contractual counterparty;
- protected context.

Not every use of a pseudonym is fraud.

> **Pseudonymity != Automatically Deception.**

The question is whether the identity representation was materially deceptive in the protected decision.

---

## 14. Fraud and authority

False claims of authority are especially important.

Examples:
- pretending to be a civil official;
- claiming nonexistent permission;
- forging an authorisation;
- representing that another participant is legally required to comply when they are not.

> **Claimed Authority != Actual Authority.**

Where false authority causes compliance, fraud/deception may coexist with unlawful restraint, property interference or other offences.

---

## 15. Omission and disclosure duties

Fraud by omission should require an independently legitimate disclosure duty.

Possible sources:
- fiduciary role;
- professional role;
- specific legal requirement;
- correction of one's own earlier representation;
- defined transactional duty;
- other recognised relationship.

> **Knowledge Of Another's Mistake != Automatic General Duty To Correct.**

This avoids converting all informational asymmetry into criminal fraud.

---

## 16. Commercial persuasion

Salesmanship, negotiation and persuasion can include opinions and predictions.

The law should distinguish:
- factual representation;
- opinion;
- estimate;
- prediction;
- uncertainty;
- advertising exaggeration;
- guarantee.

> **Optimism != Automatically Fraud.**

But knowingly presenting a fabricated factual claim as established truth may qualify where material.

---

## 17. Uncertainty

A participant must not convert uncertainty into false certainty where the distinction is material.

> **Unknown != Known True.**

> **Prediction != Fact.**

This is especially important in technical, medical, financial and AI-mediated decisions.

Honest uncertainty preserves authorship better than fabricated confidence.

---

## 18. AI-generated representations

Use of AI does not remove responsibility for knowingly deploying deceptive representations.

But:

> **AI Error != Automatic User Fraud.**

Relevant questions include:
- who authored/deployed the representation;
- what they knew;
- whether output was represented as verified;
- materiality;
- duty to check;
- purpose;
- causation.

AI itself may require separate participant-responsibility analysis where participant status applies.

---

## 19. System fraud

A deception may target an automated system rather than a human decision-maker.

Examples:
- false credentials;
- fabricated eligibility data;
- manipulated transaction inputs.

Where the system controls a protected legal/economic disposition, the same underlying protected interests may apply.

> **Automated Decision != Unprotected Decision.**

But merely violating a technical rule should not automatically become fraud absent the required deception and protected consequence.

---

## 20. Attempt

**Attempt Liability: YES**

A participant may enter direct execution of a fraudulent scheme but fail because:
- victim detects deception;
- transaction is blocked;
- false credential fails;
- transfer does not complete.

> **Failed Deception != Absence Of Attempt.**

The general Attempt architecture applies.

---

## 21. Participation

The general Participation and Complicity architecture applies.

Roles may include:
- creator of deception;
- impersonator;
- director;
- knowing document/system fabricator;
- knowing facilitator;
- recipient of proceeds where participation in scheme is independently established.

> **Receipt Of Benefit != Automatic Proof Of Participation In Fraud.**

Knowledge and authored contribution remain necessary.

---

## 22. Mental state

Fraud ordinarily requires intentional/knowing deception plus the legally required purpose/knowledge concerning the resulting disposition.

Candidate distinction:

**Intent To Deceive**  
!=  
**Intent To Obtain Protected Disposition**

Both may be relevant.

A practical joke may intentionally deceive without intending a protected acquisition.

---

## 23. Recklessness and negligence

Reckless or negligent false statements can cause severe harm.

They may require separate legal treatment in:
- professional practice;
- fiduciary duty;
- product safety;
- public information;
- regulated financial activity.

But they should not automatically be called fraud.

> **Careless Falsehood != Automatically Fraud.**

Terminological precision preserves culpability distinctions.

---

## 24. Exploiting pre-existing mistake

A participant may encounter another's mistaken belief without causing it.

Knowingly exploiting that mistake may become fraud only where:
- the participant intentionally reinforces/maintains the false belief; or
- an independent duty to correct exists; or
- another specific legal rule applies.

> **Opportunity From Another's Mistake != Automatically Fraud.**

Other property/restoration duties may still arise.

---

## 25. Concealment

Active concealment can be deception.

Examples:
- hiding a known material defect while representing an item as defect-free;
- altering records;
- covering identifying information to misrepresent provenance.

Again, materiality and intent matter.

---

## 26. Fraud and consent

Where fraud concerns a fact material to consent, it may invalidate or limit consent.

But not every deception in a relationship invalidates every consent.

The general Consent architecture controls:

**Deception**  
→ **Material To Relevant Choice?**  
→ **Did It Defeat Meaningful Authorship?**

This prevents fraud doctrine from becoming unlimited retroactive invalidation.

---

## 27. Fraud and access

Deception may obtain access to:
- property;
- information;
- protected spaces;
- systems;
- accounts;
- services.

The resulting legal state may include fraud plus an access/privacy/property offence.

> **Fraudulent Permission != Necessarily Valid Permission.**

Exact effect depends on the protected interest and material deception.

---

## 28. Harm

Potential harms include:
- property loss;
- financial loss;
- loss of opportunity;
- privacy exposure;
- assumption of liability;
- physical risk;
- reputational consequences;
- compromised autonomy;
- institutional/systemic harm.

Each should be separately evidenced.

> **Deception Severity != Harm Severity By Itself.**

---

## 29. Remedy

Potential remedies include:
- reversal of transaction;
- return of property/value;
- restoration of access/status;
- correction of records;
- compensation for established consequential loss;
- notification/correction to affected parties;
- invalidation/reformation of fraudulently induced agreement where appropriate.

> **Truth Revealed Later != Automatic Restoration Of Prior State.**

Remedy must address persistent consequences.

---

## 30. Aggravation

Potential aggravators may include:
- targeting substantial vulnerability;
- abuse of fiduciary/professional/public authority;
- systematic repeated deception;
- multiple affected participants;
- identity theft/impersonation causing broader exposure;
- large or catastrophic intended/actual loss;
- deliberate concealment after discovery;
- exploitation of emergency or dependency.

The general Aggravation architecture applies and double counting is prohibited.

---

## 31. Honest correction

A participant who discovers that they unintentionally supplied materially false information should have a route to correct it.

Prompt correction may:
- prevent completion;
- reduce harm;
- affect negligence;
- affect mitigation.

> **Correction Of Mistake != Admission Of Prior Fraud.**

This supports BL10 — Remain Open to Correction.

---

## 32. Public and institutional representations

Officials and institutions can commit materially deceptive acts just as private participants can.

> **Official Source != Infallible Source.**

Where an official knowingly fabricates material authority/facts to induce a protected disposition, public status should not immunise the conduct.

Separate governance/accountability consequences may also apply.

## 34. Worked examples

### A. False sale

A knowingly claims a worthless counterfeit component is a genuine certified component and B buys it because certification is material.

**Result:** candidate completed fraud/deceptive acquisition plus any applicable product/certification offences.

### B. Honest mistake

A sells B equipment while honestly and reasonably believing an incorrect specification supplied by the manufacturer.

**Result:** false information occurred, but intentional fraud is not established. Other civil/product duties may remain.

### C. Broken promise

A contracts to deliver goods next month, genuinely intending to do so. A later becomes unable to deliver.

**Result:** non-performance does not retrospectively establish fraudulent intent.

### D. Failed phishing-like attempt

A impersonates an authorised service and requests B transfer value. B detects the deception and transfers nothing.

**Result:** no completed acquisition; attempt may be established.

### E. Material authority deception

A falsely claims to be a lawful inspector with authority to enter B's protected workspace. B grants access solely because of that claim.

**Result:** apparent permission may be defective; fraud/deception may coexist with unlawful access.

### F. Irrelevant lie

A falsely tells B they once met a celebrity. No protected disposition follows.

**Result:** dishonest statement, but not automatically fraud.

### G. AI hallucination

A participant asks an AI system for an estimate and unknowingly repeats an incorrect output.

**Result:** falsity alone does not establish fraud. Knowledge, representation, duty and mental state require analysis.

## 35. Development result

Fraud can be derived as a protection of **consequential decision authorship**, rather than as a catalogue of scams.

Core rule:

**Do not intentionally corrupt another participant's material decision model through deception in order to obtain a protected disposition, benefit, permission or transfer.**

This architecture links:
- honesty;
- autonomy;
- consent;
- property;
- contract;
- access;
- identity;
- authority;
- automated systems;
- AI-mediated representations.

The next fundamental protected-interest derivation should address **threats, coercion and extortion**, because those corrupt decision authorship through pressure rather than false information.
