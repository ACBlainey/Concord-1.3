## 1. Purpose

This companion adds a compact operational recovery and verification layer to the constitutional Continuity Protocol.

It does not replace the parent architecture.

It operationalises the question:

> **Can the declared valuable function actually survive or be recovered under the disruption being claimed, within the required conditions, without confusing preserved information with recoverable capability?**

## 2. Continuity object

Every consequential continuity assessment should declare what is intended to continue.

Possible objects include function, knowledge, service, responsibility, operational capability, infrastructure, provenance, institutional function, identity state or correction/review capability.

> **Current Implementation ≠ Necessarily the Continuity Object**

Where the legitimate function can survive through a different implementation, preservation should not silently become implementation immortality.

## 3. Operational continuity states

For a declared object and target:

- **CS1 RECORDED** — relevant information exists;
- **CS2 ACCESSIBLE** — intended future users/systems can retrieve it;
- **CS3 INTERPRETABLE** — meaning, context and provenance can be understood;
- **CS4 ACTIONABLE** — procedures/dependencies/permissions permit use;
- **CS5 RECOVERABLE** — sufficient enabling capability exists or can realistically be reconstructed to restore the target function;
- **CS6 OPERATIONAL** — the audited function is presently operating in the audited role.

These states are object-relative.

> **High Component State ≠ High System Recovery State**

A tested standby may demonstrate recoverability while remaining CS5 when it is not presently carrying the target function.

## 4. Disruption Envelope

A recovery claim must state the disruption boundary being tested.

Examples include component, building, site, credential/identity plane, supplier, staff, institution, region or long-duration technological decay.

> **Recovery Under One Disruption Envelope ≠ Recovery Under Every Disruption Envelope**

Wider threats may be recorded separately without invalidating a correctly bounded claim.

## 5. Recovery target

Where material, define:

### Recovery Time Objective (RTO)
Maximum acceptable time to reach the required continuity state.

### Recovery Point Objective (RPO)
Maximum acceptable loss of state/history between the last usable preserved point and disruption.

### Minimum Acceptable Function (MAF)
The complete minimum functional set that counts as acceptable degraded continuity.

MAF may include capacity, destination, quality, safety, provenance or other domain requirements.

RTO, RPO and MAF are external requirements supplied through legitimate domain authority or obligation.

> **Continuity Architecture Does Not Invent Its Own Acceptability Thresholds**

A target is satisfied only when all mandatory dimensions for that audit are satisfied.

## 6. Dependency evidence and criticality

For material dependencies, record evidence state where practical:

- **VERIFIED**
- **DOCUMENTED**
- **INFERRED**
- **UNRESOLVED**

Criticality is relative to the continuity object, disruption envelope, RTO/RPO, MAF and available recovery paths.

> **Critical to One Path ≠ Necessarily Critical to Every Recovery Path**

> **Identified Dependency ≠ Available Dependency**

> **Recovery Plan ≠ Recoverable State**

KCS may supply richer dependency/provenance state where available. This companion does not replace KCS.

## 7. Recovery basis

The recovery basis is the minimum evidenced set of preserved state and enabling capability required to reach the target after the declared disruption.

It may include artefacts, provenance, instructions, tools, credentials, prerequisite capability, successor/handoff information, verification tests and known failure records.

> **Minimum Recovery Basis ≠ Copy of Current System**

Legacy Ladder analysis may be required where recovery depends on prerequisite technological or skill chains.

## 8. Redundancy and correlated failure

Multiple copies or substitutes may share one failure domain.

> **Redundancy ≠ Independence**

Where consequence warrants it, test common physical, credential, supplier, software, knowledge, infrastructure and other correlated dependencies.

## 9. Current validity

Preserved artefacts may remain technically actionable after becoming obsolete, superseded, unauthorised or otherwise invalid for current restoration.

> **Technically Actionable ≠ Valid for Current Restoration**

Preserve historical/provenance value without silently restoring superseded authority or configuration.

## 10. Restoration and verification

A restoration process should identify:

**Trigger → Responsible Actor/System → Recovery Source → Required Dependencies → Restoration Sequence → Expected Output → Verification → Failure Route**

> **Restored ≠ Verified**

For high-consequence continuity, documentary assurance alone may be insufficient. Practical recovery exercises should be used where justified.

## 11. Succession

Where continuity proceeds through succession rather than restoration, distinguish:

- required function/responsibility;
- predecessor state;
- successor;
- knowledge/capability handoff;
- independent authority/permission;
- activation;
- verification;
- unresolved liabilities/dependencies.

> **Continuity ≠ Authority**

> **Succession ≠ Automatic Legitimacy**

> **Authority to Succeed ≠ Successful Handoff**

The parent Constitutional Succession architecture remains authoritative for Concord-specific succession.

## 12. Uncertainty and claim discipline

Unknown dependencies and unavailable evidence remain visible.

> **Continuity Claim Must Not Exceed Recovery Evidence**

Do not convert:
- named dependency into available dependency;
- archive existence into recoverability;
- one successful test into universal resilience;
- current accessibility into future interpretability;
- formal succession into successful handoff.

## 13. Recursive continuity review

Continuity mechanisms themselves require continuity and correction.

Review when material dependencies, formats, technology, responsible actors, threats, continuity objects or requirements change, or when recovery tests fail.

> **Continuity Mechanism ≠ Permanent Correctness**

Preserve useful superseded continuity mechanisms as provenance without leaving them operational merely because they are historical.

## 14. Relationship to Legacy Ladder

Legacy Ladder supplies deeper recovery analysis where a high-level artefact cannot be used without lower-level skills, tools, infrastructure or prerequisite technologies.

> **Preservation of Information ≠ Preservation of Recoverable Capability**

This companion consolidates that source-family relationship without absorbing the full Legacy Ladder.

## 15. Relationship to KCS

KCS owns richer knowledge/dependency/provenance/change state.

Continuity consumes that state where useful to determine whether the recovery basis remains current and evidenced.

> **KCS Dependency State ≠ Continuity Success**

and:

> **Continuity Need ≠ KCS Authority**

## 16. Relationship to State Map and Clock

Continuity state may contribute evidence to State and Maturity Mapping.

A continuity deficit may create a development/review candidate.

Time/state/event conditions may be represented through the Civilisation Clock/STRA.

These interfaces do not permit Continuity to assign civil priority, ownership or authority.

## 17. ESCP boundary

A continuity assessment operates within a bounded evaluation space.

> **No Additional Dependency Found ≠ No Additional Dependency Exists**

> **No Known Alternative Recovery Path ≠ Proof No Alternative Exists**

Where missing evidence could materially change the claim, preserve uncertainty or route further source/dependency resolution.

## 18. Minimal operational record

For consequential use, record where applicable:

1. continuity object;
2. legitimate value/function;
3. disruption envelope;
4. required state;
5. RTO;
6. RPO;
7. MAF;
8. critical dependencies;
9. evidence state;
10. recovery basis;
11. current continuity state;
12. restoration/succession route;
13. verification evidence;
14. uncertainty;
15. provenance;
16. review trigger.
