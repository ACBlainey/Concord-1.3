## 2. Portable core recovered

V1.2 gives the compact continuity principle:

> **Preserve what remains valuable; adapt what must change; retain enough knowledge, provenance and enabling capability to recover legitimate function after disruption.**

And two critical boundaries:

> **Continuity != Stasis**

> **Preservation Of Information != Preservation Of Recoverable Capability**

## 3. Continuity object

Continuity must first identify what is actually intended to survive.

Candidate objects include:
- function;
- knowledge;
- operational capability;
- responsibility;
- service;
- relationship;
- process;
- institution;
- identity state;
- infrastructure;
- provenance;
- correction/review capability.

> **Current Container != Necessarily The Valuable Continuity Object**

## 4. Operational chain

Recovered portable chain:

**Continuity Object → Value / Legitimate Function → Threat / Discontinuity → Critical Dependencies → Preservation State → Recovery Basis → Trigger → Restoration / Succession → Verification → Review / Adaptation**

This complements rather than replaces the constitutional Continuity Protocol.

## 5. Preservation states

V1.2 distinguishes a useful ladder:
- **CS1 Recorded** — information/state exists;
- **CS2 Accessible** — intended future user can retrieve it;
- **CS3 Interpretable** — meaning/context/provenance can be understood;
- **CS4 Actionable** — procedures and dependencies permit use;
- **CS5 Recoverable** — enabling capability exists to restore the target;
- **CS6 Operational** — function is presently available.

The central protection is:

> **Recorded != Recoverable != Operational**

A continuity claim should identify the state actually required.

## 6. Recovery targets

The portable development adds:
- **RTO** — recovery time objective;
- **RPO** — recovery point objective;
- **MAF** — minimum acceptable function.

A system can possess a recovery basis and still fail continuity requirements if it cannot recover quickly enough, loses too much state, or restores below minimum legitimate function.

## 7. Dependency evidence

V1.2 preserves evidence-state distinctions rather than pretending all dependencies are known.

Dependency criticality is relative to the declared target and available recovery paths.

> **Critical To One Path != Necessarily Critical To Every Recovery Path**

This prevents one implementation from being mistaken for the function itself.

## 8. Recovery basis

The recovery basis is the minimum preserved state and enabling dependencies needed to reach the target after the declared disruption.

It may include:
- artefacts;
- redundant copies;
- provenance;
- instructions;
- dependency maps;
- credential recovery;
- tools;
- prerequisite capabilities;
- successor/handoff information;
- validation tests;
- failure records;
- fallback implementations.

> **Minimum Recovery Basis != Copy Of Current System**

## 9. Redundancy

The blind-tested module explicitly establishes:

> **Redundancy != Independence**

Copies can share physical, credential, provider, format, knowledge or assumption failure modes.

Continuity must test correlated failure, not merely copy count.

## 10. Disruption envelope

Continuity claims are bounded by the disruption class they were designed/tested to survive.

> **Recovery Evidence Inside Declared Envelope != Universal Recoverability**

The system should state what disruptions its continuity claim covers and what remains unknown.

## 11. Restoration

Restoration should specify:
- trigger;
- responsible actor/system;
- recovery source;
- dependencies;
- sequence;
- expected output;
- verification;
- failure path.

> **Restored != Verified**

A recovered implementation should not regain trust merely because a restoration process completed.

## 12. Succession

Where the predecessor will not return, continuity may require succession.

V1.2 explicitly preserves:

> **Continuity != Authority**

> **Succession != Automatic Legitimacy**

A successor must independently possess whatever authority, permission or legitimacy the surrounding architecture requires.

Continuity preserves function/handoff; it does not manufacture sovereignty.

## 13. Degraded continuity

V1.2 treats continuity as more than normal/failure.

Relevant states can include:
- normal continuity;
- degraded continuity;
- interruption;
- recoverable loss;
- irrecoverable loss;
- restoration;
- succession/replacement;
- regeneration from a lower capability state.

This aligns strongly with the V1.3 Infrastructure service-state model.

## 14. Honest failure memory

Continuity should preserve useful unsuccessful states:
- failed approaches;
- invalid assumptions;
- failure causes;
- abandoned implementations;
- reasons for supersession.

> **Preserving Only Success Can Destroy Continuity Of Learning**

## 15. Legacy Ladder recovery

V1.2 Historical re-reading of the Legacy Ladder adds:

> **The Plans Are Not The Technology**

Capability may require:
**knowledge + people + skills + tools + machines + materials + infrastructure + supply chains + institutions + experience**

Therefore:

> **Historical Component Survival != Relationship Survival != Capability Survival**

and:

> **Historical Knowledge Validity != Current Capability Availability**

## 16. Time-varying capability graph

The Legacy Ladder is better formalised as a time-varying dependency graph than a strictly linear ladder.

Capabilities may have:
- branches;
- substitutes;
- shared dependencies;
- parallel lineages;
- convergence;
- keystone lower-level capabilities.

> **Historical Prominence != Dependency Importance**

An obscure calibration process can matter more to recoverability than a famous final artefact.

## 17. Recovery distance

Recoverability is not binary.

Relevant dimensions include:
- highest demonstrably surviving reproducible level;
- lowest missing keystone;
- recovery time;
- recovery cost;
- surviving alternate paths;
- current skills/tooling/material state;
- date/result of last recovery test.

> **Capability Restored Eventually != Continuity Was Preserved**

## 18. Recursive continuity

Continuity infrastructure itself requires continuity.

Archives depend on storage, formats, parsers, software, hardware/emulation, energy, institutions and expertise.

> **Historical Physical/Data Survival != Historical Interpretability**

> **Continuity Mechanism != Exemption From Continuity Review**
