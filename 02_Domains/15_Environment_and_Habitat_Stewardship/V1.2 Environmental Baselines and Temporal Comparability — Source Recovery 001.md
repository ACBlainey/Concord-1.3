## 1. Recovery finding

V1.3 Environment already establishes environmental legibility, baseline/monitoring, three-dimensional spatial use, commons stewardship, functional nature, externalities and restoration interfaces.

A deliberate V1.2 pass identified a missing operational layer: **environmental comparison through changing measurement, spatial and interpretive systems**.

The recovered material does not require a parallel environmental architecture. It strengthens the evidence and temporal-comparability requirements of the living V1.3 domain.

## 2. Baseline

Environmental change requires a recoverable comparison state.

> **Environmental Change Claim Requires A Recoverable Comparison State**

But:

> **Environmental Baseline != Environmental Ideal State**

A baseline may be the earliest reliable measured state, a policy reference, a pre-intervention state or another declared comparison point. Its selection is provenance, not proof that it is pristine, natural or optimal.

## 3. Baseline choice

Different legitimate baselines can produce different apparent trends.

Therefore an environmental claim should preserve:
- baseline identity;
- baseline time;
- spatial scope;
- measurement method;
- uncertainty;
- reason for selection;
- later comparison state.

> **Different Baseline != Necessarily Different Physical Reality**

## 4. Spatial context

Environmental state is spatial.

A value without material spatial context may be incomplete.

Preserve where relevant:
- world/body;
- spatial reference system and version;
- coordinates/area;
- spatial resolution;
- ecological/physical boundary;
- administrative/stewardship boundary where relevant;
- translation provenance.

> **Coordinate != Location Without Spatial Reference Context**

## 5. Boundaries through time

Physical events do not move merely because administrative or stewardship boundaries change.

> **Current Boundary != Historical Boundary**

> **Location Persistence != Jurisdiction Persistence**

Historical and environmental analysis should be able to interpret a past observation in its contemporary and current spatial frameworks where useful.

## 6. Resolution

Environmental meaning can depend on scale.

> **Regional Average != Local Environmental State**

Aggregation can erase hotspots, gradients, corridors and local extremes.

Conversely, a local sensor does not automatically represent a wider region.

Spatial resolution and aggregation method are therefore part of evidence provenance.

## 7. Measurement-system change

Longitudinal environmental analysis must distinguish changes in the environment from changes in observation.

> **Measurement Change != Environmental Change By Default**

Relevant transitions include:
- sensor replacement;
- calibration;
- sampling frequency;
- detection limit;
- laboratory method;
- model revision;
- coverage;
- interpolation;
- unit/definition changes.

Material transitions should remain reconstructable.

## 8. Calibration and correction

A later calibration can revise an estimate of the past without changing the past environment.

> **Recalibration != Environmental Event**

Where consequence warrants, preserve both the original analytical state and corrected state with provenance.

## 9. Missingness

Environmental missing data may be structured rather than random.

Sensors may fail during storms, heat, radiation events, infrastructure failure or conflict.

> **Missing Environmental Data != Random Absence By Default**

Coverage state is therefore evidence.

A dataset can become systematically optimistic if its observation system disappears during the worst conditions.

## 10. Observation, interpolation and model

Keep distinct:

**Physical State → Direct Observation → Interpolation / Derived Estimate → Model / Digital Twin → Prediction**

> **Spatial Estimate != Direct Measurement**

> **Digital-Twin State != Physical State**

> **Later Reconstruction != Contemporary Observation**

All can be useful; they have different epistemic status.

## 11. Exposure and harm

V1.2 sharpens environmental consequence classification:

> **Proximity != Exposure**

> **Exposure != Harm**

> **Hazard != Participant Vulnerability**

> **Same Hazard != Same Risk Across Time**

Risk may change with participant substrate, protective equipment, infrastructure, medicine, adaptation, duration, intensity and resource state.

This supports the V1.3 principle to describe the environmental condition before classifying the participant.

## 12. Causation, responsibility and capacity

Cross-boundary environmental analysis should preserve:

> **Environmental Causation != Responsibility != Current Mitigation Capacity**

A party may have caused an effect but lack present mitigation capacity.

A party may have mitigation capacity without having caused the effect.

A consequence may cross a boundary without manufacturing jurisdiction.

## 13. Multi-causality

Environmental outcomes can have multiple contributors.

> **Material Environmental Change != Single-Cause Event By Default**

Attribution should preserve competing/material causal hypotheses and uncertainty rather than forcing a single narrative.

Later causal knowledge must not rewrite what was knowable earlier.

## 14. Dataset comparability

Co-located environmental datasets may differ in:
- units;
- sensors;
- definitions;
- sampling;
- thresholds;
- grids;
- coverage;
- calibration;
- uncertainty.

> **Dataset Co-Location != Dataset Comparability**

Comparability should be demonstrated, not assumed.

## 15. Threshold history

Safety/ecological thresholds can change as knowledge changes.

> **Current Threshold != Historical Threshold**

Past observations may be retrospectively evaluated under current knowledge, but retrospective classification should not erase the classification that existed at the time.

## 16. Environmental maps

A map may contain observation, interpolation, classification, boundary choice and missingness.

> **Environmental Map != Raw Environmental Reality**

Map version/method are provenance.

## 17. Resource/environment linkage

Resource use and environmental consequence should remain distinct but linkable.

> **Resource Use != Environmental Consequence**

> **Non-Use Of Resource != Waste Without Context**

Reserve, resilience and future capacity can make non-use valuable.

Longitudinal linkage may reveal self-undermining loops:

**Resource Use → Environmental Degradation → Reduced Sustaining Capacity → Increased Scarcity / Risk**

## 18. Recovery and intervention

Environmental improvement after intervention does not by itself prove causation.

> **Recovery After Intervention != Proof Of Intervention Causation**

Likewise:

> **Non-Occurrence Of Predicted Harm != Automatic Falsification Of Prior Risk**

Mitigation, changed conditions, observation failure or incorrect prediction are separate possibilities.

## 19. Long horizons

Environmental historical value may emerge after the original monitoring purpose ends.

Long-term patterns can reveal changes invisible to individual observations.

But:

> **Potential Future Environmental Value != Unlimited Retention Authority**

Participant privacy, security-sensitive infrastructure and other protected information remain bounded.

## 20. Candidate environmental state record

Where consequence warrants, a state record may include:
- phenomenon;
- observed time;
- spatial reference/version;
- spatial resolution;
- method/instrument/version;
- calibration;
- observed value;
- uncertainty;
- coverage/missingness;
- baseline reference;
- threshold reference;
- direct/interpolated/modelled status;
- relevant exposure/infrastructure references;
- corrections;
- provenance.

This is a semantic interface, not a mandate for one universal database.

## 21. Candidate change record

A material environmental-change claim may include:
- baseline state;
- comparison state;
- spatial/temporal scope;
- observed change;
- measurement compatibility;
- causal hypotheses;
- attribution state;
- affected systems;
- responsibility state where separately established;
- mitigation/recovery state;
- disputes;
- provenance.
