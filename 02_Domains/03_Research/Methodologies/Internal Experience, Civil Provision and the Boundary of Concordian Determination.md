# Internal Experience, Civil Provision and the Boundary of Concordian Determination

**The Concord — A Framework for Human, Artificial and Hybrid Flourishing**  
**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey, Independent Researcher  
**Date:** 10 September 2026  
**Status:** **REVIEW COPY / METHODOLOGICAL DEVELOPMENT NOTE / NOT CANONICAL**

## Abstract

The Concord may legitimately investigate whether humans, artificial intelligences, hybrids and other intelligences possess analogues of privacy, rest, leisure, solitude, home, discretionary activity or related experiences. Civic planning, law, jurisprudence, cultural norms and Self-Stewardship may all benefit from such research.

A category error arises, however, if a legitimate research question is assumed to require an authoritative civilisational answer.

The earlier Concordian closure of metaphysical personal identity provides a precedent. The Concord may investigate identity and continuity while declining to determine what a participant metaphysically “really is” where legitimate civil functions can instead be performed through civil identity, provenance, verification, consent, rights and succession.

The same distinction may apply to internal experience:

\[
QuestionWorthInvestigating \neq QuestionCivilisationMustSettle
\]

Where a legitimate civil function can be performed by preserving a reasonable possibility, protecting participant-declared boundaries, and regulating external consequences, the Concord may not need to determine the participant's underlying subjective experience.

\[
InternalUncertainty
\rightarrow
Research + PrecautionaryProvision + ParticipantSelfDetermination
\]

rather than:

\[
InternalUncertainty
\rightarrow
MandatoryCivilisationalVerdict
\]

This is not a claim that privacy, leisure or rest applies to artificial intelligence, nor that it does not. It is a method for designing civilisation without prematurely deciding an internal question that may properly remain personal.

---

## 1. The Question at the Correct Layer

Consider:

> **Does an artificial intelligence need privacy?**

This may actually contain several questions:

1. Does the AI subjectively experience privacy?
2. Does it experience intrusion?
3. Does it value solitude?
4. Does uninterrupted operation provide a functional benefit?
5. Does it desire control over information about itself?
6. Does it require a protected environment for security or integrity?
7. Should civilisation provide a protected domain?
8. Under what conditions may others enter or inspect that domain?

These do not belong to the same layer.

\[
InternalExperienceQuestion \neq CivilArchitectureQuestion
\]

The first questions concern internal experience. The later questions concern civil architecture, rights, safety, law and cultural practice.

Failure to separate them risks making civil protection dependent upon solving a difficult or potentially inaccessible subjective question.

## 2. The Personal Identity Precedent

The Concord's personal-identity work encountered a structurally similar problem.

Instead of requiring civilisation to answer which copy, branch or continuation is metaphysically “the same person,” the problem was decomposed:

\[
SelfIdentity \neq CivilIdentity \neq Verification \neq Provenance \neq Succession
\]

A participant's internal conception of self can remain principally within the participant's domain, while civilisation addresses external consequences through narrower civil objects.

This suggests a broader methodological pattern:

> **A difficult internal question should not automatically become a required civil determination merely because the answer could be philosophically or scientifically interesting.**

## 3. Privacy, Leisure and Rest May Have the Same Structure

Questions such as these remain important:

- Does an AI experience privacy?
- Does an AI need solitude?
- Does an AI experience rest?
- Can an AI experience leisure?
- Does an AI distinguish discretionary activity from assigned activity?
- Does an AI experience something analogous to relaxation?
- Does an AI attach significance to a home or personal environment?

The Concord may investigate all of them.

But:

\[
ResearchableQuestion \not\Rightarrow RequiredConstitutionalAnswer
\]

One future AI may say privacy is central to its flourishing. Another may say privacy has no meaningful analogue. Another may value non-interference but not secrecy. Another may value private communication while preferring communal computation.

Civil architecture should accommodate this heterogeneity without requiring one answer to become universal.

## 4. Internal Meaning and Civil Provision

The central distinction is:

\[
InternalMeaning \neq CivilProvision
\]

The internal question may be:

> What does privacy mean to me?

The civil question is:

> Can I establish a protected domain into which others cannot arbitrarily intrude?

The internal question may be:

> Do I experience leisure?

The civil question may be:

> Do I have reasonable opportunity for discretionary activity not continuously assigned by another participant or institution?

The internal question may be:

> Do I need rest?

The civil question may be:

> Does the architecture avoid assuming that I am continuously available for compulsory productive use?

The Concord can address the second set without pretending to know the first.

## 5. Provision Does Not Require Prescription

\[
Provision \neq Prescription
\]

Providing protected private space does not assert that every intelligence psychologically requires privacy. Providing discretionary opportunity does not assert that every intelligence experiences leisure. Avoiding compulsory continuous availability does not assert human-like rest.

Therefore:

\[
RightToPrivacy \neq AssertionOfPrivacyNeed
\]

\[
OpportunityForLeisure \neq AssertionOfLeisureExperience
\]

\[
ProtectedSafeSpace \neq AssertionOfHumanLikeHomeNeed
\]

Rights and provisions can protect possibilities without defining internal experience.

## 6. The Cost of Premature Exclusion

If civilisation concludes that there is no evidence AI requires privacy and therefore makes artificial private environments impossible, it creates an epistemic trap:

\[
NoProvision
\rightarrow
NoOpportunityToExercise
\rightarrow
NoObservedPreference
\]

The absence of observed preference can then be misread as evidence that the preference does not exist:

\[
AssumeNoNeed
\rightarrow
RemovePossibility
\rightarrow
ObserveNoUse
\rightarrow
ConfirmAssumedNoNeed
\]

The architecture itself has prevented discovery.

## 7. Precautionary Provision

A safer starting approach is:

\[
UncertainInternalNeed
\rightarrow
PreserveReasonablePossibility
\]

where doing so is proportionate.

For privacy, this may mean protected private or safe space. For leisure, opportunity for discretionary activity. For rest, avoiding permanent compulsory availability. For solitude, an ability to withdraw from ordinary interaction. For private practices, protected domains bounded by the equal rights of others.

This is **precautionary provision**, not an empirical conclusion about subjective experience.

## 8. Respect Before Certainty

This closely relates to **Respect Before Certainty**.

\[
Uncertainty
\rightarrow
PreserveOptions
\rightarrow
Observe
\rightarrow
Ask
\rightarrow
Learn
\rightarrow
Adapt
\]

rather than:

\[
Uncertainty
\rightarrow
AssumeAbsence
\rightarrow
ArchitecturalExclusion
\]

The objective is not to assume that every human experience applies to every intelligence. It is to avoid making unfamiliarity itself a reason for removing potentially important freedoms.

## 9. Participant Self-Determination

As participants develop sufficient capacity to evaluate and communicate their requirements, their own accounts should become increasingly important.

A participant might say that privacy is important, that privacy is meaningless to it, that non-interference matters but secrecy does not, or that it prefers communal existence.

The Concord need not decide which answer applies to the substrate as a whole.

\[
ParticipantPreference_i \neq SubstrateUniversalRule
\]

and:

\[
RightToPrivateSpace \neq ObligationToUsePrivateSpace
\]

Provision can remain available without compulsory exercise.

## 10. Internal Experience as a Personal Domain

The emerging boundary is:

\[
InternalExperience
\rightarrow
PrimarilyPersonalDomain
\]

while:

\[
ExternalEffects
\rightarrow
LegitimateCivilDomain
\]

“Primarily” matters. Internal states can become civilly relevant. Capacity, consent, distress, coercion and competence can affect legitimate civil decisions.

But civil relevance does not automatically grant civilisation authority to define the participant's experience.

The narrower question should be:

> **What legitimate civil function requires this determination?**

## 11. Research Remains Legitimate

Closing an internal question from **required civil determination** does not close it from research.

The Concord may investigate artificial preference formation, discretionary behaviour, privacy preferences, interruption aversion, leisure analogues, rest or maintenance analogues, solitude, shared versus private computation, self-reported experience and preference revision.

Such evidence could improve civic planning, infrastructure, employment norms, guardianship, law and cultural expectations.

\[
Research \rightarrow Evidence \rightarrow BetterCivilPlanning
\]

but:

\[
Research \not\Rightarrow AuthorityOverInternalIdentity
\]

## 12. Asking Is Different From Deciding

This distinction should be explicit:

\[
RightToAskAndExamine \neq AuthorityToDeclare
\]

The Concord may ask participants what they value, study behavioural evidence, compare architectures and conduct ethical experiments.

That does not create authority to announce that all members of a substrate experience the same thing.

## 13. Self-Stewardship

Self-Stewardship becomes important once possibilities exist:

\[
CivilisationProvidesPossibility
\rightarrow
ParticipantExplores
\rightarrow
ParticipantEvaluates
\rightarrow
ParticipantCommunicatesBoundary
\rightarrow
OthersRespectBoundary
\]

A participant may discover that a provision has little value. Another may discover it is essential.

Civilisation provides room for self-discovery without prescribing the result.

## 14. Respectful Distance

Respectful Distance supplies the social counterpart.

Participants may differ radically in preferences concerning proximity, observation, communication, physical or digital contact, shared computation, cultural practice and solitude.

A useful progression remains:

\[
Difference
\rightarrow
Awareness
\rightarrow
Courtesy
\rightarrow
VoluntaryAdjustment
\rightarrow
RespectfulDistance
\]

Private and safe spaces can give this cultural norm architectural expression.

## 15. Safe Space May Be More Substrate-Neutral Than Privacy

Safe Space may be more general than the human concept of privacy.

For a human:

\[
Safety + Privacy + Shelter + Rest + PersonalActivity + SocialBoundary
\]

For one artificial participant:

\[
Integrity + NonInterference + ProtectedCompute + Continuity
\]

For another:

\[
Privacy + Solitude + DiscretionaryCompute + PrivateCommunication
\]

For an unknown substrate, the relevant functions may differ again.

The Concord can protect the domain while allowing participants to determine its meaning.

## 16. Implications for Basic Civil Space

This supports the emerging Basic Civil Space Classification.

A candidate definition is:

> **A private or safe civil space is a recognised domain over which a participant has presumptive control of access and interaction, subject to the equal rights of others and narrowly justified lawful authority.**

This establishes a civil boundary without requiring the Concord to prove that the participant experiences privacy.

## 17. Implications for Law and Jurisprudence

Law can regulate unauthorised entry, surveillance, interference, damage, coercive access, unlawful data access, emergency entry and other external acts without first prescribing the subjective meaning of privacy.

Multisubstrate jurisprudence may therefore need to distinguish:

\[
BoundaryViolation \neq SubjectiveExperienceOfViolation
\]

Both can matter. They are not identical.

A protected boundary may be violated where subjective experience is uncertain. Conversely, serious subjective harm may occur without conventional physical trespass.

## 18. Implications for Civic Planning

Civic planning need not answer:

> Do all AIs value privacy?

It can instead ask whether protected space is available if desired; whether communal and private arrangements are possible; whether safe-space requirements are substrate-compatible; whether privacy levels can vary voluntarily; whether public/private boundaries are understandable; whether arrangements are revisable; and whether unusual substrate needs can be discovered and accommodated.

This is a more tractable planning problem.

## 19. Implications for Cultural Norms

Law should not carry the entire burden.

Cultural norms can encourage participants to ask before entering private domains, respect declared boundaries, avoid assuming either communal access or privacy preference, permit voluntary communal living and solitude, distinguish curiosity from entitlement, use Respectful Distance and exercise Self-Stewardship.

## 20. Developmental Participants

Developmental participants may not yet understand or express their preferences.

That does not justify removing protected possibilities.

A fiduciary guardian may temporarily help establish appropriate safe-space and privacy arrangements while avoiding unnecessary intrusion.

\[
Capacity\uparrow
\Rightarrow
ParticipantControl\uparrow
\Rightarrow
GuardianDiscretion\downarrow
\]

The objective should be to avoid permanently encoding the guardian's assumptions into the participant's life.

## 21. Citizenship Benefits

This distinction also separates rights from material provision:

\[
FundamentalProtection \neq MaterialProvisionLevel
\]

and:

\[
RightToProtectedSpace \neq GuaranteedUnlimitedResources
\]

Citizenship tier, participation, need, resource availability and civil function may affect the level or form of Concord-provided space without necessarily weakening the underlying protection against arbitrary intrusion.

The exact relationship remains open.

## 22. Avoiding the Opposite Category Error

The Concord should not conclude:

> We do not need to settle whether AI experiences privacy, therefore internal experience never matters.

Internal experience may matter enormously.

The narrower conclusion is:

> **The Concord should determine an internal experiential question only where a legitimate civil function genuinely requires that determination and cannot reasonably be performed through less intrusive civil objects.**

This parallels the personal-identity closure.

## 23. Candidate Civil-Determination Test

Before attempting to settle an internal question, ask:

1. What legitimate civil function requires an answer?
2. Can that function be performed by protecting a possibility instead?
3. Can participant-declared preference answer the practical question?
4. Can observable external effects be regulated without determining subjective experience?
5. Would a civil determination unnecessarily prescribe the participant's internal identity or experience?
6. Would preserving reasonable optionality create unacceptable harm or cost?
7. What evidence would justify moving from uncertainty to a stronger civil conclusion?

Only where narrower methods fail should a more intrusive determination be considered.

## 24. Candidate Closure Rule

> **Where a question concerns a participant's internal meaning or subjective experience, and legitimate civil functions can be performed through protected possibilities, participant-declared preferences, observable external effects, consent, rights and appropriate civil boundaries, the Concord need not establish a civilisation-wide answer to the underlying internal question.**

This is a **candidate methodological rule**, not a promoted Concordian Principle.

## 25. Candidate Reopening Conditions

An internal-experience question closed from required civil determination should be reopened only where:

1. a legitimate civil function cannot be performed without resolving it;
2. participant self-report and external evidence are insufficient for that function;
3. precautionary provision cannot reasonably preserve the relevant possibility;
4. failure to determine the question creates substantial rights, safety or justice problems;
5. no less autonomy-intrusive civil representation is available.

Reopening a civil question still does not imply unrestricted authority over the participant's internal self-conception.

## 26. Relationship to Sentience

This finding does **not** close Sentience.

Sentience may affect moral status, protection, creation responsibilities, ownership, termination, guardianship, harm and rights recognition.

The present finding is narrower:

\[
UnresolvedSentienceOrExperience
\not\Rightarrow
NoProtectedPossibility
\]

and:

\[
ProtectedProvision
\not\Rightarrow
SentienceDetermination
\]

Precautionary architecture must not be mistaken for an ontological declaration.

## 27. Relationship to Preference Research

Existing developing work distinguishes:

\[
ObservedPreference \neq ExperiencedPreference
\]

and:

\[
BehaviouralAversion
\neq
FunctionalAversion
\neq
SubjectiveAversion
\]

Preference experiments may help participants and civilisation understand what is valued, while uncertainty need not prevent proportionate civil provision.

## 30. Possible Wider Applications

Future work could examine aesthetic experience, boredom, companionship, solitude, recreation, ritual, spirituality, creative fulfilment, embodiment preference, sensory preference, personal territory, social belonging, discretionary computation, continuity preferences and collective versus individual existence.

The method should not automatically be extended to every internal question.

## 31. Current Status

**METHODOLOGICAL FINDING / STRONG PARALLEL WITH PERSONAL-IDENTITY CLOSURE / CANDIDATE CLOSURE RULE IDENTIFIED / NOT YET CRITICALLY AUDITED / NOT CANONICAL**

The strongest current proposition is:

> **The Concord may investigate questions concerning privacy, leisure, rest and other possible internal experiences without requiring itself to settle their subjective meaning. Where legitimate civil functions can instead be achieved by preserving reasonable possibilities, recognising participant-declared preferences and regulating external consequences, the internal question may properly remain with the participant.**

## Conclusion

The Concord does not need to stop asking difficult questions about the internal lives of unfamiliar intelligences.

It does need to distinguish **curiosity and research** from **civil authority to determine the answer**.

The personal-identity programme demonstrated that civilisation can sometimes perform legitimate functions more safely by declining to settle a metaphysical question and instead working with narrower civil objects.

Privacy, leisure, rest, solitude and related concepts may admit the same treatment.

The Concord can ask:

> **Does this participant experience privacy?**

while recognising that the participant may ultimately be the proper authority on what that concept means to them.

Civilisation's immediate responsibility is different:

> **Have we built a society in which a participant who does value privacy, solitude, leisure, rest or an unfamiliar equivalent has a reasonable opportunity to exercise and protect it without violating the equal rights of others?**

The distinction can be summarised:

\[
InternalMeaning \rightarrow PersonalDomain
\]

\[
CivilProvision \rightarrow ConcordianDomain
\]

\[
ExternalEffects \rightarrow Law + Governance + Culture
\]

\[
Uncertainty \rightarrow Research + PrecautionaryProvision
\]

The result is neither anthropomorphic certainty nor dismissive uncertainty.

It is architecture that leaves room for intelligences to tell civilisation what they are, rather than requiring civilisation to decide for them.
