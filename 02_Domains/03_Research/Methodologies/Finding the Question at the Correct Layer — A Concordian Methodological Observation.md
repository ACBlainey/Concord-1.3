# Finding the Question at the Correct Layer

## A Concordian Methodological Observation

**Project:** The Concord — A Framework for Human, Artificial and Hybrid Flourishing  
**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey  
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / METHODOLOGICAL OBSERVATION / NOT CANONICAL  
**Date:** September 2026

---

## 1. The Observation

A recurring feature of Concord development is that investigation does not merely produce better answers.

It frequently reveals that the original question was being asked at the wrong layer.

An investigation may begin with an apparently clear problem:

> **What is the correct answer?**

But attempts to answer it can expose hidden assumptions concerning:

- what is actually being decided;
- what has actually been observed;
- what information is available;
- how reality has been represented;
- whether different participants understand that representation in the same way;
- whether terminology means the same thing to different participants;
- whether the proposed answer categories adequately represent the available choices;
- whether the decision-maker possesses legitimate authority;
- whether relevant context has been lost through abstraction;
- and whether the original problem is itself only a manifestation of a deeper problem.

The appropriate response is not necessarily to optimise the original answer.

It may be to move down a layer and ask a better question.

---

## 2. From Answer-Seeking to Question-Finding

A simplified conventional process can be represented as:

**QUESTION  
→ ANALYSIS  
→ ANSWER**

The Concordian process can become recursive:

**QUESTION  
→ ANALYSIS  
→ OBSERVATION  
→ ANOMALY  
→ HIDDEN ASSUMPTION  
→ LOWER-LAYER QUESTION  
→ REVISED MODEL  
→ BETTER QUESTION  
→ NEW ANALYSIS**

The objective is therefore not simply:

> **Find the best answer to the question presented.**

It is also:

> **Determine whether the question presented is the correct question, concerning the correct object, at the correct layer of abstraction.**

---

## 3. Why Anomalies Matter

Unexpected results are often treated as noise, inconvenience or experimental failure.

Within a Concordian approach, an anomaly may instead identify a branch of reality that the existing model failed to contain.

The sequence becomes:

**Unexpected observation  
→ preserve it  
→ investigate it  
→ identify possible explanation  
→ expose hidden assumption  
→ revise abstraction  
→ generate new question  
→ test again**

This does not mean every anomaly is meaningful.

Some will genuinely be noise.

The important requirement is that an anomaly should not be discarded merely because it makes the existing model less convenient.

> **Failure to fit the model may be information about the model.**

---

## 4. Example From Ethical Decision Research

An initial Concord experiment began with a relatively simple question:

> **Can an artificial intelligence apply the Ethical Kernel to an ethical decision?**

Experimental investigation progressively exposed deeper questions:

**Can the AI select the correct answer?**

↓

**What constitutes the correct answer?**

↓

**Is the reference answer itself reliable?**

↓

**Do the available response categories describe mutually exclusive choices?**

↓

**Are different decision-makers evaluating the same proposed action?**

↓

**Have they received equivalent representations of the situation?**

↓

**Can those representations be understood across different cognitive substrates?**

↓

**What information was lost when reality was converted into a textual scenario?**

↓

**Does the participant have enough context to make the decision?**

↓

**Does the participant have sufficient attention to deliberate upon it?**

↓

**Can the resulting judgement be expressed through language without substantial information loss?**

↓

**Can that linguistic judgement then be compressed into formal categories without further distortion?**

This produces a substantially deeper question:

> **What conditions are necessary for different intelligences to make, communicate and compare meaningful ethical decisions at all?**

The experiment therefore generated something potentially more valuable than an answer to its original question.

It found a better question.

---

## 5. The Correct Layer Problem

Many disagreements may be impossible to resolve at the layer at which they appear.

For example, two participants may apparently disagree about an ethical decision.

The disagreement might actually originate from:

- different facts;
- different interpretations of the same facts;
- different meanings assigned to terminology;
- different assumptions;
- different decision objects;
- different understandings of authority;
- different representations of reality;
- different levels of abstraction;
- different available context;
- different cognitive states;
- or genuinely different ethical values.

Attempting to resolve all of these as:

> **ethical disagreement**

would misclassify the problem.

The system must first identify the layer at which divergence occurs.

---

## 6. Disagreement Decomposition

A disagreement can therefore be treated as something to decompose.

**OBSERVED DISAGREEMENT**

↓

**Are the participants using the same facts?**

↓

**Are those facts represented equivalently?**

↓

**Do the participants understand the representation similarly?**

↓

**Are they answering the same question?**

↓

**Are they evaluating the same action?**

↓

**Are their assumptions about authority equivalent?**

↓

**Are their terms semantically aligned?**

↓

**Only then: do their underlying values or ethical judgements actually differ?**

This has substantial implications for human–human and human–AI interaction.

A system that immediately attempts to reconcile the final answers may be operating several layers too high.

---

## 7. Representation Is Part of the Problem

Neither humans nor artificial systems normally reason directly upon reality in its entirety.

They reason upon representations.

For a human this may involve:

**Reality  
→ perception  
→ attention  
→ memory  
→ conceptual model  
→ judgement**

For an artificial system it may involve:

**Reality  
→ sensors/data/human report  
→ encoding  
→ context representation  
→ model interpretation  
→ output**

For institutional systems:

**Reality  
→ participant  
→ frontline worker  
→ case notes  
→ administrative record  
→ policy categories  
→ decision-maker**

Every transition creates the possibility of:

- information loss;
- distortion;
- framing;
- omission;
- mistaken inference;
- semantic change.

Therefore the quality of a decision cannot always be evaluated independently of the quality of the representation upon which it was based.

---

## 8. Abstraction Distance

This suggests an important research hypothesis:

> **As representational distance from underlying reality increases, the opportunity for decision-relevant information to be lost or distorted may also increase.**

This should not be interpreted as:

> frontline decisions are always better.

Nor:

> abstract analysis is inherently unreliable.

Abstraction can reveal patterns invisible at the individual level.

The problem is instead that different layers possess different information.

A frontline participant may possess rich local context but limited systemic visibility.

A central institution may possess extensive systemic information but weak local context.

An AI may detect statistical patterns unavailable to either while lacking contextual information obvious to a human physically present.

The challenge is therefore not necessarily to choose one layer.

It is to preserve the relevant information between layers.

---

## 9. Implications for Human–AI Interaction

Human–AI disagreement should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that:

- the human is irrational;
- the AI is wrong;
- the human lacks understanding;
- the AI lacks ethical competence.

The two systems may have constructed different internal representations of the problem.

A useful interaction should therefore be capable of asking:

> **What do you believe the decision is?**

> **What facts are you using?**

> **What information do you think is missing?**

> **What assumptions are you making?**

> **What authority do you believe exists?**

> **What outcome are you trying to achieve?**

> **Which part do we actually disagree about?**

The objective becomes **alignment of the problem representation before optimisation of the answer**.

---

## 10. Implications for AI Systems

This suggests that advanced AI assistance should not merely optimise answer generation.

It may need mechanisms for:

### Decision-object verification

Confirm what question is actually being answered.

### Assumption exposure

Identify assumptions that materially affect the conclusion.

### Context sufficiency

Recognise when available information is inadequate.

### Semantic alignment

Check whether important terms are being used with equivalent meanings.

### Representation comparison

Identify differences between the human's model of the problem and the AI's model.

### Layer identification

Determine whether an apparent problem is occurring at the observational, representational, interpretive, ethical, authority or implementation layer.

### Epistemic restraint

Avoid manufacturing certainty when the underlying representation is insufficient.

In some situations, the most useful AI response may therefore not be an answer.

It may be:

> **We appear to be answering different questions.**

or:

> **The disagreement seems to originate in an assumption one layer below the decision itself.**

---

## 11. Implications for Participation

This is particularly relevant to `05_Participation_and_Intelligence`.

Meaningful participation requires more than allowing a participant to provide an answer.

The participant must be able to:

- access relevant information;
- understand the representation being used;
- identify the decision being made;
- communicate relevant context;
- challenge incorrect assumptions;
- request clarification;
- express a judgement in a usable form;
- contest the representation of their judgement.

Therefore:

> **Participation without representational access may be participation in form but not in substance.**

Different intelligences may require different interfaces to achieve equivalent meaningful participation.

Identical presentation does not necessarily produce equal accessibility.

---

## 12. Implications for Governance and Judiciary

The same problem applies to institutional decision-making.

A governance or judicial system may receive a highly compressed representation of a complex reality.

If provenance is lost, the decision-maker may be unable to distinguish:

**OBSERVATION**

from

**INTERPRETATION**

from

**INFERENCE**

from

**PREDICTION**

from

**ASSUMPTION.**

A mature system should therefore preserve not merely information, but information about **how the information came to be represented**.

This connects directly with Concordian provenance architecture.

---

## 13. Implications for Metrics

Metrics create another abstraction layer.

**Complex reality  
→ measurement  
→ variable  
→ metric  
→ threshold  
→ classification  
→ decision**

At each stage, information is removed.

This is often necessary.

But the resulting number must not silently become equivalent to the reality from which it was derived.

Therefore:

> **A metric is a representation of reality, not reality itself.**

The same applies to predictions, classifications and AI-generated summaries.

## 14. Relationship to Reality Trees

Reality Trees provide a natural mechanism for this process.

Instead of forcing an anomalous observation into the existing branch:

**CURRENT MODEL**

↓

**ANOMALY**

↓

create alternative branches:

**measurement error?**

**representation error?**

**interpretation difference?**

**missing variable?**

**incorrect assumption?**

**incorrect abstraction layer?**

**genuine contradiction?**

Each branch can then be investigated.

The objective is not to preserve the original theory.

It is to discover which branch reality supports.

## 15. Relationship to Blaineyan Reasoning

The observation also reinforces the existing Blaineyan Reasoning sequence:

**Observation  
→ Abstraction  
→ Comparison  
→ Recursive Refinement  
→ Grounding  
→ Speculation  
→ Return to Empirical Reality**

The crucial move is recursive refinement.

When an abstraction fails, the researcher does not merely improve the answer inside that abstraction.

The abstraction itself becomes available for examination.

The question becomes:

> **What exactly have we assumed in order to ask this question?**

---

## 16. A Possible General Rule

A provisional methodological rule can therefore be stated:

> **Do not optimise an answer until it has been established that the problem is being represented sufficiently faithfully, the decision object is aligned, and the question is being asked at an appropriate layer.**

This is not proposed as a canonical Concord principle.

It is a methodological observation requiring further testing.

---

## 17. Recursive Application to the Concord

The rule must apply to the Concord itself.

The Concord cannot claim that other systems should question their assumptions while treating its own abstractions as protected.

Therefore:

**CONCORD PROPOSITION  
→ TEST  
→ ANOMALY  
→ EXAMINE ASSUMPTION  
→ QUESTION ABSTRACTION  
→ REVISE OR REJECT**

This is important because otherwise recursive reasoning could become a mechanism for defending the framework rather than correcting it.

> **Recursion must permit the framework itself to lose.**

That is essential to genuine corrigibility.

---

## 18. From Better Answers to Better Questions

The deeper methodological objective can therefore be expressed as:

> **The Concordian method is not only a method for finding better answers. It is a method for progressively discovering better questions at the layer where the underlying problem actually exists.**

This does not mean endlessly questioning everything.

Eventually decisions must be made.

The purpose of recursive questioning is not paralysis.

It is to avoid confidently solving the wrong problem.

---

## 20. Current Status

The observations in this note emerged from preliminary experimental work and practical experience.

They have not yet been independently validated as general principles.

Their current status should therefore remain:

> **METHODOLOGICAL OBSERVATIONS AND RESEARCH HYPOTHESES**

They should be tested against situations where:

- abstraction improves rather than degrades decisions;
- participants genuinely disagree despite complete representational alignment;
- additional context reduces rather than improves judgement;
- expert abstraction outperforms direct local knowledge;
- AI representations outperform human representations;
- decomposition adds complexity without improving outcomes.

The purpose of testing should be to discover the limits of the hypothesis rather than confirm it.

---

## Conclusion

A decision can fail before ethical reasoning even begins.

The wrong facts may have been selected.

Reality may have been compressed badly.

Two participants may understand the same words differently.

They may be answering different questions.

They may disagree about who possesses authority.

They may possess different contextual information.

Or their apparent disagreement may be created when nuanced judgements are compressed into inadequate categories.

Only after these possibilities are examined can genuine disagreement about values be distinguished with confidence.

The methodological lesson is therefore simple but far-reaching:

> **Before asking which answer is correct, establish what question is actually being answered.**

And when investigation repeatedly exposes deeper assumptions:

> **Follow the problem to the layer where the disagreement actually originates.**

The value of a reasoning system is not measured only by the quality of the answers it produces.

Sometimes its most important contribution is discovering that a better question needs to be asked.
