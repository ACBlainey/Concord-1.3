## Executive Summary

Many ethical and governance analyses begin at the visible decision point.

A participant, operator, official, AI, institution or emergency responder is presented with constrained options and asked which option should be chosen.

That can be too late.

The decision environment may itself have been produced by earlier design choices, resource allocations, missing safeguards, institutional boundaries, maintenance failures, information loss or unresolved upstream dependencies.

This Development Note formalises **Architectural Ethics** as a Concordian method for tracing consequential outcomes both upward toward the architecture that shaped the decision environment and downward from architecture toward the lived consequences it tends to produce.

Recent Concord development makes the method more precise.

Layer-Zero work already provides a bottom-up vertical trace from lived reality toward systems and constitutional architecture. CDT independently demonstrated that a visible downstream deficit can be caused by an immature upstream owner and that repeatedly patching dependents may be inferior to developing the legitimate upstream source.

Those findings support the usefulness of upstream tracing, but they do not establish ethical or legal responsibility.

The central boundary is therefore:

> **Functional dependency ≠ causal contribution ≠ decision authority ≠ ethical responsibility ≠ legal liability.**

Architectural Ethics is intended to expose those relationships without collapsing them.

---

# 1. The Problem of Starting Too Late

A classic dilemma often presents the final actor with a small set of bad options.

The immediate question may be legitimate:

> What should this actor do now?

But a civilisational design method must also ask:

> Why did the system reach a state in which these were the available options?

A harmful outcome may be influenced by a chain such as:

**DESIGN → RESOURCING → IMPLEMENTATION → MAINTENANCE → INFORMATION FLOW → OVERSIGHT → LOCAL DECISION → OUTCOME**

The final actor may possess some responsibility.

So may upstream actors.

Or the apparent upstream connection may be merely structural rather than ethically culpable.

The purpose of Architectural Ethics is not to maximise blame. It is to locate preventable architecture.

---

# 2. Two Complementary Directions

## 2.1 Bottom-Up Trace

Layer-Zero work supplies a method beginning with observed reality:

**LIVED / OBSERVED OUTCOME**
→ **LOCAL FAILURE OR CONDITION**
→ **DECISION ENVIRONMENT**
→ **OPERATIONAL SYSTEM**
→ **COORDINATION / OVERSIGHT**
→ **GOVERNANCE**
→ **CONSTITUTIONAL / ARCHITECTURAL CONDITIONS**

Question:

> What is the highest relevant layer that materially helps explain the observed outcome?

The trace should stop when further abstraction ceases to add explanatory or corrective value.

## 2.2 Top-Down Trace

Architectural Ethics also runs in the opposite direction:

**ARCHITECTURAL RULE / DESIGN**
→ **INCENTIVES / RESOURCES / SAFEGUARDS / CONSTRAINTS**
→ **DECISION ENVIRONMENT**
→ **AVAILABLE ACTIONS**
→ **PARTICIPANT BEHAVIOUR**
→ **LIKELY CONSEQUENCES**

Question:

> What kinds of lived decision environments does this architecture tend to generate?

The two traces can meet.

A candidate upstream explanation becomes stronger when its predicted downstream effects correspond to observed reality.

---

# 3. Decision Points and Decision Lines

Complex systems often do not have one decisive moment.

A better representation may be a **decision line**:

**DESIGN DECISION**
→ **FUNDING / RESOURCE DECISION**
→ **POLICY DECISION**
→ **IMPLEMENTATION DECISION**
→ **SUPERVISORY DECISION**
→ **FRONT-LINE DECISION**
→ **PARTICIPANT OUTCOME**

At each position ask:

- What authority existed?
- What information was available?
- What duty or function applied?
- What alternatives were available?
- What constraints came from upstream?
- Was the consequence reasonably foreseeable?
- Could the actor materially reduce the risk?
- Could the actor escalate?
- Was a correction mechanism available?
- Had similar failures occurred previously?
- Was failure detected but uncorrected?

This converts a simplistic search for “who made the bad decision?” into a structured analysis of the environment that produced it.

# 4. Five Relations That Must Remain Separate

## 4.1 Functional Dependency

System A requires capability supplied by system B.

This is the principal relation represented by CDT dependency topology.

It says nothing by itself about blame.

## 4.2 Causal Contribution

An action, omission or condition materially contributes to an outcome.

Causation may be distributed, probabilistic or uncertain.

## 4.3 Decision Authority

An actor possessed legitimate or de facto power to choose, constrain, permit or prevent some action.

Authority may exist without causal contribution in a particular case.

## 4.4 Ethical Responsibility

Given capability, knowledge, duty, foreseeability, alternatives and context, an actor may bear ethical responsibility for action or omission.

This requires normative reasoning beyond dependency mapping.

## 4.5 Legal / Civil Liability

A legitimate legal architecture may assign liability under defined rules of standing, evidence, causation, jurisdiction, defences and remedy.

Architectural Ethics must not silently perform this adjudicative function.

Therefore:

> **Dependency evidence may trigger investigation. It does not itself assign liability.**

---

# 5. Relationship to CDT

CDT provides an important structural analogue.

Its upstream-bottleneck rule states, in effect:

> Where multiple downstream systems depend on a capability legitimately owned by an immature upstream system, develop the owner before independently patching each dependent.

Architectural Ethics asks a related but different question:

> Where multiple harmful outcomes or constrained decisions repeatedly arise, is there an upstream architectural condition that materially contributes to them?

The correspondence is useful:

**CDT:** downstream deficit → dependency trace → legitimate upstream owner → development.

**Architectural Ethics:** downstream consequence → causal/decision trace → relevant upstream conditions → prevention/correction analysis.

But the inference must stop before responsibility is assumed.

A system can be the correct functional owner of a capability without being morally or legally responsible for every downstream failure.

# 6. Relationship to Layer-Zero Methodology

Layer-Zero provides direct grounding.

Its Vertical Trace asks whether abstract Concord architecture reaches lived reality.

Architectural Ethics adds the reverse direction and responsibility distinctions.

Combined:

**OBSERVED REALITY**
↑
**BOTTOM-UP FAILURE TRACE**
↕
**DECISION ENVIRONMENT**
↕
**TOP-DOWN ARCHITECTURAL TRACE**
↓
**EXPECTED CONSEQUENCES**

A proposed architectural cause should survive both directions.

This gives a falsification opportunity:

If architecture X is claimed to explain outcome Y, but X does not plausibly generate Y under the relevant conditions, the causal hypothesis weakens.

# 7. Candidate Architectural Ethics Record

A future structured record could be:

**AER = <Outcome, Context, DecisionLine, Actors, Functions, Authority, Information, Constraints, Alternatives, Dependencies, CausalClaims, Foreseeability, Duties, CorrectionPaths, Evidence, Uncertainty, Review>**

For each decision-line node:

**DLN = <Actor/System, Function, Authority, Information, Action/Omission, UpstreamConstraints, Alternatives, Foreseeability, CorrectiveCapacity, EvidenceState>**

This is an analytical record.

It is not a liability verdict.

---

# 8. Evidence States

Causal and responsibility claims should preserve uncertainty.

Candidate states:

- OBSERVED
- CORROBORATED
- PLAUSIBLE
- MODEL-DEPENDENT
- CONTESTED
- INCOMPLETE
- UNKNOWN
- DISPUTED
- REFUTED
- SUPERSEDED

The architecture should be able to say:

> We have located a plausible upstream contribution but cannot yet establish responsibility.

That is preferable to either premature blame or premature exoneration.

---

# 9. The Highest Relevant Layer

The method must resist a failure mode in which every problem is traced indefinitely upward until “civilisation” becomes responsible for everything.

A trace should stop when one or more of the following apply:

1. further upstream relation is immaterial to the outcome;
2. no plausible corrective leverage is gained;
3. evidence becomes too weak to support the next step;
4. the relation becomes generic background rather than case-relevant cause;
5. legitimate responsibility clearly belongs at a lower bounded layer;
6. the next step would confuse historical condition with current authority.

Thus:

> **Trace to the highest relevant causal/design layer, not the highest imaginable layer.**

---

# 10. Distributed Responsibility

Some failures may genuinely have no single responsible node.

Example pattern:

**small design weakness**
+
**resource constraint**
+
**poor information transfer**
+
**local discretionary error**
+
**failed oversight**
→ **harm**

No component alone may be sufficient.

Architectural Ethics should therefore support:
- contributory responsibility;
- shared responsibility;
- uncertain responsibility;
- systemic coordination failure;
- and no established responsibility.

This parallels CBERRM's refusal to collapse consequence into responsibility or jurisdiction.

---

# 11. Upstream Liability Without Upstream Sovereignty

Identifying an upstream contributor must not grant that system broader authority.

For example:

- discovering a governance design defect does not authorise governance to absorb judiciary functions;
- discovering a KCS dependency does not make KCS sovereign over dependent systems;
- discovering a resource cause does not give the resource allocator authority over all participant decisions.

Therefore:

> **Upstream Relevance ≠ Upstream Sovereignty.**

and:

> **Corrective Leverage ≠ General Authority.**

---

# 12. Prevention Before Blame

The strongest civilisational use of the method is prospective.

After tracing a failure, ask:

> At which legitimate point could the smallest intervention most reliably prevent recurrence?

Possible responses include:
- redesign;
- better information;
- additional safeguard;
- resource correction;
- clearer interface;
- escalation route;
- review;
- redundancy;
- training;
- changed incentive;
- bounded authority correction.

This aligns with CDT's Minimum Necessary Intervention rule.

Preferred sequence:

**Understand → Localise → Source-Resolve → Correct Legitimate Owner/Interface → Retest**

rather than:

**Harm → Find nearest actor → Assign all blame.**

---

# 13. Interaction With Judicial Architecture

Architectural Ethics may supply evidence to a legitimate judicial or review process.

It should not replace that process.

Possible interface:

**Observed Harm**
→ **Architectural Ethics Trace**
→ **Candidate causal/responsibility relations**
→ **Evidence + uncertainty**
→ **Legitimate adjudicative process where required**
→ **Finding / remedy**
→ **Architectural correction**
→ **provenance / learning**

The judiciary remains responsible for legal determinations within legitimate jurisdiction.

---

# 14. Interaction With Empirical Validation Architecture

The newly developed Empirical Validation Architecture (EVA) gives Architectural Ethics a test discipline.

Claims such as:

> architecture X materially contributes to failure Y

should be classified and tested appropriately.

Possible test forms:
- historical replay;
- comparative cases;
- counterfactual simulation;
- intervention before/after evidence;
- repeated failure clustering;
- natural experiments;
- prospective prediction.

A decision-line model should therefore generate testable expectations rather than remain merely explanatory prose.

# 15. Failure Modes of Architectural Ethics

## F1 — Infinite Upstream Blame

Everything is attributed to civilisation-scale architecture.

**Control:** highest relevant layer / materiality stop.

## F2 — Dependency-to-Liability Collapse

A functional dependency is treated as proof of culpability.

**Control:** five-relation separation.

## F3 — Hindsight Bias

After harm, every upstream actor is assumed to have foreseen it.

**Control:** record information and foreseeability at the time.

## F4 — Authority Inflation

An actor's ability to prevent harm is converted into permanent authority.

**Control:** corrective leverage ≠ general authority.

## F5 — Individual Erasure

Systemic explanation is used to deny participant agency.

**Control:** preserve local decisions as part of the decision line.

## F6 — System Erasure

All responsibility is placed on the final actor despite constrained choices.

**Control:** mandatory upstream trace for significant repeated/systemic failures.

## F7 — Causal Storytelling

A plausible narrative is mistaken for demonstrated causation.

**Control:** evidence states + EVA testing.

## F8 — Blame Optimisation

The method becomes a mechanism for allocating punishment rather than preventing recurrence.

**Control:** prevention/correction objective; adjudication remains separate.

---

# 16. First Candidate Test

A suitable first test is not a new hypothetical trolley problem.

Use an already-developed Layer-Zero case where:
- lived outcome is documented;
- several system layers are represented;
- existing bottom-up trace exists;
- candidate interventions exist.

Run the Architectural Ethics trace top-down and compare it with the Layer-Zero bottom-up trace.

Freeze predictions before comparison:
1. which decision-line nodes should be material;
2. which upstream constraints should explain the observed local failure;
3. where the highest relevant layer should occur;
4. where corrective leverage should lie;
5. which apparent responsibility claims should remain unresolved.

If the two independently constructed traces converge materially, the method gains bounded support.

If they diverge, the disagreement may expose missing variables or a defect in one trace.

# 17. Development Status

Architectural Ethics has now crossed the sketch threshold because:
- the originating problem is clearly specified;
- Layer-Zero supplies an independent complementary method;
- CDT supplies a tested structural analogue for upstream dependency;
- CBERRM supplies useful separation among consequence, responsibility and jurisdiction;
- EVA supplies a way to test causal claims.

It is **not validated**.

The next legitimate step is a bounded retrospective/prospective test against an existing Layer-Zero case.

Do not expand it into a liability code before that test.

# Conclusion

Architectural Ethics asks civilisation to inspect not only the final decision but the architecture that made that decision necessary.

Its core contribution is a two-direction method:

**Lived Outcome → Upstream Trace**

and

**Architecture → Downstream Consequence Trace.**

Where the traces meet, the civilisation may find a better location for correction.

But the method is constrained by a critical separation:

> **Functional dependency ≠ causal contribution ≠ decision authority ≠ ethical responsibility ≠ legal liability.**

The objective is not to make responsibility diffuse until everyone is guilty.

It is to make architecture visible enough that preventable failure can be corrected at the right layer.
