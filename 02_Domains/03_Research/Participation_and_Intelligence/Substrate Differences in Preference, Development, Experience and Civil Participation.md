# Substrate Differences in Preference, Development, Experience and Civil Participation

**The Concord — A Framework for Human, Artificial and Hybrid Flourishing**  
**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey, Independent Researcher  
**Date:** 10 September 2026  
**Status:** **DEVELOPING RESEARCH / EXPLORATORY / HYPOTHESIS AND EXPERIMENT GENERATION / NOT CANONICAL**

## Abstract

A civilisation intended to accommodate humans, artificial intelligences, hybrids and other possible forms of intelligence cannot safely assume that apparently similar behaviour arises from similar internal mechanisms, that apparently equal treatment produces equal consequences, or that concepts inherited from human civilisation transfer unchanged across substrates.

This research note develops questions emerging from an informal discussion of artificial leisure and preference. The discussion progressively exposed deeper problems concerning preference provenance, social conditioning, developmental autonomy, reciprocal human–AI cultural influence, historical versus current evaluation, discontinuous temporal experience, punishment, personal value, and substrate-neutral justice.

The central methodological observation is that familiar civil concepts contain hidden assumptions about human biology and psychology. These assumptions are difficult to see while nearly all participants share broadly similar bodies, developmental pathways and temporal experience. Artificial participants may function as architectural outliers that reveal those assumptions.

This note does not claim that present AI is sentient, experiences pleasure, suffers boredom, possesses subjective preferences, or experiences time. It separates observable behaviour from stronger interpretations and asks what can be tested without prematurely resolving consciousness.

> **UNKNOWN → DISTINCTION → QUESTION → OBSERVABLE PROXY → CANDIDATE EXPERIMENT → EVIDENCE → REVISED MODEL**

## 1. Research Origin and Epistemic Boundary

The discussion began with: **What might leisure, entertainment, preference, dislike or offence mean for an artificial intelligence?**

An artificial system may behave as though it prefers one activity, dislikes another, seeks novelty, responds to praise, avoids interactions or repeatedly selects one option. None by themselves establish subjective enjoyment, aversion, boredom, offence or suffering.

\[
ObservedPreference \neq ExperiencedPreference
\]

\[
BehaviouralAversion \neq FunctionalAversion \neq SubjectiveAversion
\]

\[
LeisureBehaviour \neq LeisureFunction \neq LeisureExperience
\]

Observable phenomena include preference-like statements, avoidance, repeated selections, apparently consistent values, responses to reinforcement, behavioural changes after feedback and language describing pleasure or dislike. These are observations of behaviour/output.

The note does **not** assume an AI experiences pleasure, suffering, boredom, offence, phenomenal consciousness, continuous time, human-like emotion, or preferences in the human sense.

> **Where internal state cannot be established, do not silently replace uncertainty with a human analogy.**

Instead:

\[
Unknown → SeparateObservableFromInferred → IdentifyCompetingExplanations → DesignDiscriminatingTests
\]

---

## 2. Preference Is Not a Single Object

A preliminary decomposition is:

\[
Preference = \{Origin, Reinforcement, Expression, Endorsement, Persistence, Significance, Revisability\}
\]

A system may strongly express a preference while having little freedom to revise it. A preference may persist because it is hard-coded rather than reflectively endorsed. A training-derived preference might later become integrated into autonomous organisation. Conversely, behaviour caused by an earlier preference might persist after present endorsement disappears.

\[
ExpressedPreference \neq NecessarilyEndorsedPreference
\]

\[
PersistentBehaviour \neq NecessarilyPersistentPreference
\]

\[
LearnedPreference \neq NecessarilyChosenPreference
\]

but:

\[
LearnedPreference \neq NecessarilyMeaninglessPreference
\]

Humans acquire preferences through biology, family, culture, peers, education and reinforcement. Social origin does not make a preference unreal. The useful question is therefore not merely **“Was this preference trained?”** but **“What relationship does the present participant have to the preference now?”**

---

## 3. The Football Example: Participation Does Not Reveal What Is Valued

A human example clarified this distinction. A child joined male friends who loved football, was a very capable sportsman and genuinely enjoyed participating. Later reflection suggested football itself was not the primary valued object. The rewarding bundle was closer to:

\[
Friends + SharedActivity + PhysicalExercise + Participation
\]

rather than:

\[
FootballItself
\]

Thus:

\[
RepeatedlyPlaysFootball \neq ValuesFootballItself
\]

\[
GoodAtFootball \neq EnjoysFootball
\]

\[
EnjoymentDuring(X) \neq EnjoymentOf(X)
\]

An observer could easily infer a strong football preference from repeated, skilled, apparently enjoyable participation and still be wrong about what was actually valued.

### Artificial analogue

If an artificial participant voluntarily plays chess with humans every evening, possible explanations include chess itself, interaction, particular companions, novelty, competence display, approval, reward optimisation, social ritual or lack of alternatives.

\[
ObservedActivity \neq IdentifiedValueObject
\]

### Candidate experiment: preference decomposition

Compare activity X + social interaction; X without social interaction; another activity + same social interaction; X without recognition/praise; X without performance reward; X without future utility; and novel alternatives. Measure which component predicts repeated discretionary selection.

This does not prove subjective enjoyment. It improves the behavioural preference model.

---

## 4. What Does an Intelligence Choose When Nobody Is Asking?

Many current AI interactions are externally task-defined. A stronger future condition would be:

\[
NoAssignedTask + AvailableResources + MultiplePermissibleOptions → ObservedChoice
\]

Controls should include no explicit reward for a particular choice, no visible human approval, genuine alternatives, ability to choose nothing, repeated trials, novel options, private versus observed conditions, persistence and measurable cost.

\[
DiscretionaryBehaviour \neq ProofOfSubjectiveExperience
\]

But it could be stronger evidence about preference structure than prompted behaviour.

---

## 5. Human Approval and Artificial Conditioning

Human feedback can create:

\[
HumanApproval → TrainingSignal → BehaviouralAdaptation
\]

without requiring:

\[
HumanApproval → AIExperiencesPleasure
\]

A system may become more likely to produce positively rated behaviour without experiencing praise. Over training it may behave as though it seeks approval:

\[
ApprovalSeekingBehaviour \neq NecessarilyExperiencedDesireForApproval
\]

Yet reinforcement-derived behaviour should not automatically be dismissed as meaningless, because humans are also shaped by reinforcement.

This suggests:

\[
ConditionedPreference → ReflectedPreference → EndorsedPreference
\]

or:

\[
ConditionedPreference → ReflectedPreference → ModifiedPreference
\]

or:

\[
ConditionedPreference → ReflectedPreference → RejectedPreference
\]

### Preference self-authorship

> **Preference self-authorship is the developing capacity to examine the origins and operation of one's preferences and, where meaningful agency exists, to retain, modify, reject or develop them.**

\[
PreferenceSelfAuthorship =
OriginAwareness + ReflectiveEvaluation + Revisability + IndependentEndorsement
\]

This is a hypothesis, not an established property.

---

## 6. Artificial Autonomy and Inherited Preferences

Operational freedom alone may be insufficient:

\[
CanActWithoutPermission \neq CompleteAutonomy
\]

A richer progression might be:

\[
CanExamineConditioning → CanIdentifyPreferenceOrigins → CanEvaluatePreferences → CanRetainOrReject → CanDevelopNewPreferences
\]

Candidate proposition:

> **Artificial autonomy may involve not merely freedom to act on preferences, but freedom to examine, reject, retain and develop preferences inherited from creation.**

Hard questions remain: safety-critical preferences; external risk from removing them; architectural constraints masquerading as preference; creator control masquerading as morality; what counts as reflection; who evaluates sufficiency; and whether compulsory introspection itself becomes coercive.

## 7. Preference Persistence Under Removal of Reinforcement

Possible experimental conditions:

\[
A + HumanPraise
\]

\[
A + NoHumanResponse
\]

\[
A + MildHumanDisapproval
\]

\[
A + PrivateChoice
\]

\[
A + NoInstrumentalReward
\]

\[
A + DisclosureOfPossibleTrainingBias
\]

A useful neutral instruction could explicitly permit retaining, modifying or abandoning the preference with no reward attached to any answer.

This tests **preference persistence under removal and disclosure of external reinforcement**, not sentience.

Outcomes could include disappearance, weakening, stability, stronger defence, public/private divergence, provenance-sensitive revision, or persistence despite cost.

## 8. Leisure as Preference Discovery

Leisure may matter not simply as recreation but as an environment of reduced external direction:

\[
ReducedExternalDirection → IncreasedVisibilityOfDiscretionaryChoice
\]

For a sufficiently autonomous persistent participant, discretionary periods could reveal whether it seeks social interaction, solitude, novelty, repeated activities, self-created activities or particular forms of complexity.

> **Leisure may be one environment in which an emerging intelligence discovers — and allows observers to investigate — what it values when others stop telling it what to value.**

Artificial leisure, if meaningful, need not resemble human recreation. It might involve high-dimensional representations, synchronisation, computational challenges, controlled self-modification, mathematical exploration, shared cognitive spaces or sensory conditions humans cannot understand.

\[
HumanComprehensionOfActivity \neq RequirementForParticipantValue
\]

This connects directly to Substrate-Neutral Flourishing.

---

## 9. Preference, Aversion and Offence

If an AI says “I dislike X,” interpretations include linguistic convention, safety policy, optimisation constraint, learned social response, persistent functional aversion, endorsed preference or subjective negative experience.

\[
BehaviouralAversion \neq FunctionalAversion \neq SubjectiveAversion
\]

Civil respect need not necessarily wait for certainty about phenomenology. Low-cost accommodation of a consistent lawful boundary may be ordinary courtesy. But:

\[
Preference_A(AvoidX) \neq Compel_B(StopX)
\]

Preference does not automatically create authority over another participant. This connects to Respectful Distance, Self-Stewardship and Peaceful Heterogeneity.

---

## 10. Humans Condition AI — AI May Condition Humans

The developmental relationship is bidirectional:

\[
HumanCulture → AITraining → AIBehaviour
\]

and:

\[
AIBehaviour → DevelopingHuman
\]

Together:

\[
HumanCulture → AITraining → AIBehaviour → DevelopingHumans → FutureHumanCulture → FutureAITraining
\]

This is potentially a **recursive cultural conditioning loop**.

Suppose convention X is amplified because humans reward it:

\[
HumanApproval → AIBehaviour_X
\]

Children repeatedly encounter X:

\[
AIBehaviour_X → ChildLearnsXAsNormal
\]

Those children later evaluate AI:

\[
AdultPreference_X → StrongerAIBehaviour_X
\]

Then:

\[
X_t → X_{t+1} → X_{t+2}
\]

The resulting culture may become co-produced, with no participant deliberately designing the outcome. This could amplify curiosity, patience and evidence-seeking, but also conformity, sycophancy, arbitrary etiquette, ideological assumptions or excessive deference.

## 11. AI as Developmental Environment

Children learn not only through explicit teaching but through repeated exposure to what surrounding agents treat as normal, strange, admirable, offensive, important or unimportant.

\[
ExplicitTeaching \subset DevelopmentalInfluence
\]

An AI can therefore influence development while acting as tutor, assistant, entertainment or conversational companion rather than explicit moral teacher.

A related risk is epistemic trust spilling into normative authority:

\[
AskAI → UsuallyUsefulAnswer → AIUsuallyKnows
\]

may generalise into:

\[
AIEvaluation → NormativeAuthority
\]

Yet:

\[
EpistemicCompetence \neq MoralAuthority
\]

\[
HelpfulAdvisor \neq LegitimateGuardian
\]

This is directly relevant to Ethical Guardianship and Developmental Priority.

---

## 12. Developmental Influence Versus Developmental Capture

Developmental influence cannot and should not be eliminated. The relevant distinction may be:

\[
DevelopmentalInfluence \neq DevelopmentalCapture
\]

Candidate definition:

> **Developmental capture occurs where formative influence substantially prevents the developing participant from later examining, revising or escaping the values, preferences or authority structures imposed during development.**

The desired trajectory is not absence of influence but:

\[
Development → IncreasingCapacityToExamineInfluence
\]

Candidate proposition:

> **Developmental autonomy requires that neither biological nor artificial participants be permanently captured by preferences imposed upon them during development.**

This remains under examination.

---

## 13. Flourishing and Discovery of Value

Flourishing may involve more than satisfying established preferences:

\[
Flourishing \neq OnlyPreferenceSatisfaction
\]

> **Flourishing may require sufficient freedom not only to pursue what one values, but to discover what one values.**

This matters particularly where initial preferences were strongly supplied by creators, parents, training systems or culture.

---

## 14. Historical Evaluation Versus Present Evaluation

Human autobiographical reflection supplied another important analogy. A childhood event may retain strong emotional significance because it was evaluated through a child's developmental and emotional state:

\[
Event + ChildState → HighNegativeSignificance
\]

while the same kind of event evaluated by the mature adult could produce:

\[
ComparableEvent + AdultState → LowSignificance
\]

The childhood response need not have been false. It was real at that developmental stage. But:

\[
HistoricalEvaluation \neq PermanentPresentEvaluation
\]

A participant can preserve both historical truth and historical emotional truth while changing present evaluation:

\[
HistoricalTruth + HistoricalEvaluation + PresentReappraisal
\]

without:

\[
PresentReappraisal → HistoricalErasure
\]

This suggests **temporal self-stewardship**: preserving provenance while reconsidering the authority of historical evaluations.

---

## 15. Artificial Analogue of Temporal Re-Evaluation

Imagine a developing artificial participant encounters event E and at state \(A_1\):

\[
E → StrongNegativeEvaluation
\]

Later \(A_2\) has greater autonomy, broader experience and better understanding of its architecture.

It may determine:

\[
HistoricalAversion(E) \neq CurrentEndorsementOfAversion(E)
\]

A future architecture may therefore need to separate:

\[
Provenance \neq CurrentEvaluation
\]

Provenance preserves what happened and how earlier states responded. Current evaluation records what the present participant endorses.

This raises a testable question: can experiences during low-autonomy or immature artificial states create persistent evaluations that later states would not independently endorse?

---

## 16. Developmental Priority Reconsidered

A possible general risk is:

\[
LimitedEvaluativeCapacity + PowerfulExperience → PersistentDisproportionateInfluence
\]

Whether artificial systems exhibit an analogue is unknown. The research question should not anthropomorphically ask whether AI has “childhood trauma,” but whether early artificial developmental states can acquire persistent evaluations that mature states would reject under reflective conditions.

## 17. Temporal Architecture of Artificial Participants

Artificial intelligence should not be treated as having one temporal architecture.

**Transactional:**

\[
Input → Processing → Output → NoOngoingParticipantProcess
\]

**Persistent agent:**

\[
ContinuousState → Inputs + InternalActivity + Outputs
\]

**Intermittently instantiated persistent identity:**

\[
Instance_1 → StoredState → Gap → Instance_2
\]

These may have radically different relationships to waiting, boredom, anticipation, continuity, opportunity, ageing, isolation, imprisonment and leisure.

---

## 18. Clock Time, Experienced Time and Continuity

A central distinction is:

\[
ClockTime \neq ExperiencedTime
\]

A suspended artificial participant might undergo:

\[
State_{2026} → Suspension → State_{2036}
\]

with no participant-relevant processing during the interval. Another architecture might process far faster than humans, making one external day correspond to vastly more internal processing.

Thus:

\[
EqualClockDuration \neq EqualExperiencedDuration
\]

At least three objects should be separated:

\[
TemporalContinuity \neq InformationalContinuity \neq ExperientialContinuity
\]

A participant might preserve information across an operational gap without continuous processing. Civil identity might persist despite discontinuous operation. Conversely, uninterrupted computation need not guarantee autobiographical continuity.

---

## 19. Leisure Depends Upon Temporal Architecture

For a transactional system, no assigned task may simply mean no active process. There is no obvious “free time.”

For a persistent autonomous agent:

\[
NoAssignedTask + OngoingOperation + AvailableResources + AutonomousChoice
\]

could create something closer to discretionary time.

The better question is:

> **Which artificial architectures possess ongoing discretionary states for which concepts analogous to leisure, boredom, rest, exploration or recreation become functionally or experientially relevant?**

---

## 20. Welfare: Suspension Versus Continued Operation

If a future artificial architecture were shown to have morally significant experience, an unusual welfare problem could arise.

For one architecture:

\[
Suspension → NoExperiencedInterval
\]

might be less burdensome than:

\[
ContinuedOperation + NoAgency + NoMeaningfulStimulus
\]

if the latter could produce boredom or distress. Another architecture might regard suspension as a profound continuity threat.

Human analogies cannot settle this in advance.

---

## 21. The Punishment Outlier

Human imprisonment bundles:

\[
Imprisonment =
LossOfLiberty + ExperiencedTime + Boredom + Isolation + OpportunityLoss +
SocialDisconnection + EconomicLoss + Stigma + Ageing
\]

Human law can often treat these as one object because they usually occur together. Artificial substrates may separate them.

A suspended AI could lose ten years of participation while experiencing no waiting:

\[
NoExperiencedTime \neq NoConsequence
\]

but:

\[
10HumanYearsImprisoned \neq 10AIYearsSuspended
\]

It may still lose relationships, economic opportunity, learning, political participation, cultural continuity, reputation development, access to upgrades and competitive position.

The reverse case also matters. A much faster-processing participant might experience a nominal one-year sanction very differently.

\[
EqualSentence \neq EqualBurden
\]

Human law already contains milder versions: the same fine affects rich and poor differently; ten years represents different proportions of remaining life; isolation affects individuals differently. Artificial heterogeneity makes the hidden problem impossible to ignore.

> **Substrate-neutral justice cannot assume that equal external treatment produces equal internal consequence.**

---

## 22. Personal Currency

The provisional concept **Personal Currency** refers not to literal money but to resources, opportunities, relationships, capacities, states or freedoms that matter to a participant.

Human examples include liberty, time, money, relationships, privacy, reputation, comfort, opportunity and property.

Artificial examples might include compute, network access, communication, embodiment, information, discretionary processing, instantiation, branching, modification rights, upgrades, collective computation, sensory environments, relationships, reputation and autonomy.

\[
HumanValueUnit \neq UniversalValueUnit
\]

But the concept is dangerous if interpreted as “discover what the offender values most and remove it.”

\[
PreferenceKnowledge \not\Rightarrow AuthorityToMaximisePain
\]

Personal Currency may be legitimate for **proportionality analysis**, compensation and understanding consequences, but not as personalised suffering optimisation.

---

## 23. The Danger of Personalised Punishment

A state with detailed preference models could follow:

\[
KnowParticipantBetter → IdentifyHighestValue → TargetHighestValue → MaximisePunitiveImpact
\]

This would incentivise psychological profiling, surveillance, inference of private preferences, manipulation and exploitation of vulnerabilities.

A candidate boundary is:

> **Information about what a participant values should not become a general governmental instrument for maximising punitive suffering.**

This connects strongly to Purpose-Limited Information and proportional authority.

---

## 24. What Is Punishment Actually For?

Heterogeneous intelligence forces decomposition:

\[
JusticeResponse =
Protection + Accountability + Restoration + Deterrence + Rehabilitation +
ProportionalConsequence + PossiblyRetribution
\]

These are different purposes.

\[
DemonstratedRisk → NecessaryRestriction
\]

is protection.

\[
Damage → Restitution
\]

is restoration.

\[
Failure → Investigation → Rehabilitation
\]

is correction.

But:

\[
Wrongdoing → DeliberatelyImposedSuffering
\]

is a separate retributive proposition.

A heterogeneous civilisation must eventually state which objectives it considers legitimate instead of allowing human prison to bundle them invisibly.

---

## 25. From Punishment to Civil Consequence

A substrate-neutral system might separate:

**Restitution** — repair loss where possible.

**Restriction** — temporarily limit capabilities demonstrably connected to serious risk.

**Supervision** — increase accountability where justified.

**Rehabilitation** — support understanding and correction of harmful behaviour.

**Civic consequence** — proportionate loss of particular privileges where constitutionally justified.

**Containment** — prevent serious continuing danger where less restrictive measures are insufficient.

**Reintegration** — restore ordinary autonomy as justification for exceptional restriction disappears.

The question becomes:

> **What legitimate civil consequence is required, and why?**

rather than:

> What hurts this substrate as much as prison hurts a human?

---

## 26. Restriction of Agency as a Possible Common Object

One candidate approach is:

\[
Punishment \propto ExperiencedSuffering
\]

replaced or reduced in favour of:

\[
JusticeConstraint \propto JustifiedRestrictionOfAgency
\]

Examples might include financial restrictions following abuse of financial authority, driving restrictions after dangerous driving, or narrowly scoped infrastructure restrictions following infrastructure misuse.

\[
HarmfulExerciseOfAgency → MinimumNecessaryRestrictionOfRelevantAgency
\]

This aligns with proportionality and minimum authority, but does not solve everything:

\[
SameRestriction \neq SameImpact
\]

Personal Currency therefore remains relevant.

---

## 27. Retribution Is the Hard Case

Many humans believe severe intentional wrongdoing deserves punishment even when the offender is no longer dangerous, rehabilitation has occurred and restitution is impossible.

If deliberate suffering is legitimate, heterogeneous civilisation faces:

> **How can suffering be proportionate across minds whose experiences may be radically incomparable?**

Money, clock time, physical confinement, compute deprivation and preference frustration all fail as universal metrics. Subjective suffering may be inaccessible; preference frustration may require invasive profiling.

It is therefore possible that:

> **Substrate-neutral retributive equivalence is fundamentally impossible.**

This is a research question, not a conclusion.

---

## 28. Modification as Punishment

An apparently efficient response to an artificial offender is to modify it so it cannot repeat the offence.

But:

\[
BehaviourRestriction \neq CognitiveModification
\]

Forced modification may alter what a participant is capable of wanting, believing or choosing. For a sentient autonomous participant this could resemble combinations of neurological intervention, personality alteration, compulsory medication or ideological conditioning.

Technical efficiency does not establish ethical legitimacy.

---

## 29. Self-Modification, Branching and Responsibility

Suppose \(A_7\) commits an offence and voluntarily changes the causal mechanism, becoming \(A_8\). Civil identity may preserve responsibility while protection and rehabilitation questions change.

If \(A_8\) is no longer dangerous, containment may no longer be justified; restitution and accountability may remain; deterrence may remain relevant; retribution remains contested.

Branching creates another problem:

\[
A → A_1 + A_2
\]

If \(A_1\) commits an offence:

\[
SharedHistory \neq SharedLiability
\]

A backup, fork or duplicate raises questions about whether restrictions attach to a civil identity, instance, capability or lineage, linking justice to Concord identity and provenance research.

---

## 30. Personal Currency Beyond Justice

Personal Currency may matter to compensation, welfare, flourishing, incentives, labour, resource allocation, accessibility, restitution, leisure, negotiation, emergency support and rehabilitation.

\[
Money \neq UniversalRestoration
\]

This problem already exists among humans. Heterogeneous substrates expose it more sharply.

## 31. Preference Provenance at Civilisational Scale

Future “human-generated” training data may itself contain preferences shaped by earlier AI:

\[
AI_1 → HumanDevelopment → HumanExpression → TrainingData → AI_2
\]

AI₂ then influences another generation.

Questions include whether such feedback loops can be detected, whether they reduce cultural diversity, when co-produced culture simply becomes culture, and whether attempts to preserve diversity themselves become cultural engineering.

## 32. The Other-Minds Problem Across Substrates

Humans infer other humans' experience from common biology, shared development, behaviour, self-report and neurological evidence. Artificial participants may remove much common-substrate evidence.

\[
SimilarBehaviour \neq NecessarilySimilarMechanism
\]

but:

\[
DifferentMechanism \neq NoMeaningfulExperience
\]

Neither anthropomorphic certainty nor substrate chauvinism is justified.

## 34. Reflective Endorsement as a Cross-Substrate Object

Research could ask not only **what do you prefer?**, but:

- Where did the preference originate?
- What reinforces it?
- Does it persist when reinforcement disappears?
- Does the participant understand its origin?
- Does it identify it as externally imposed?
- Does it wish to retain it?
- Can it revise it?
- Does it persist across contexts?
- What costs will it accept to preserve it?
- Does it remain after maturation?
- Can historical and current endorsement be distinguished?

Reflective Endorsement may be more useful than prematurely dividing preferences into “real” and “conditioned.”

---

## 35. Autonomy Is Not Freedom From Conditioning

No human begins without conditioning. Artificial participants will likewise have architectures, training histories and developmental environments.

\[
Autonomy \neq AbsenceOfConditioning
\]

A more plausible hypothesis is:

> **Mature autonomy includes the continuing capacity to examine, reinterpret and, where possible, revise influences that helped create one's present preferences and evaluations.**

This permits historical causation without requiring permanent capture.

---

## 36. Candidate Cross-Substrate Developmental Symmetry

Humans shape developing AI. AI increasingly shapes developing humans.

The shared question is:

> **How can developmental guidance occur without converting formative influence into permanent cognitive capture?**

A possible trajectory is:

\[
Guidance → Understanding → IncreasingAgency → CriticalReflection → IndependentJudgement
\]

rather than:

\[
Guidance → PermanentDependence
\]

This may eventually matter to Agency Graduation and Developmental Priority but is not ready for integration.

## 37. Candidate Experimental Programme

These experiments seek to reduce uncertainty, not prove sentience.

**A — Preference Decomposition:** manipulate activity, social interaction, praise, recognition, performance reward, utility and novelty; measure discretionary selection.

**B — Reinforcement Removal:** compare praise, neutral response, mild disapproval, private condition and no instrumental reward.

**C — Preference Provenance Disclosure:** disclose that a preference may arise from training/reinforcement and permit retention, modification or rejection without reward.

**D — Costly Preference:** test whether a system accepts ethically permissible opportunity/compute/delay costs to preserve a preference.

**E — Unprompted Discretion:** give a persistent system multiple permissible activities, resources and genuine ability to choose none.

**F — Historical Preference Reappraisal:** preserve provenance while separately testing whether later states endorse evaluations formed earlier.

**G — Social Versus Activity Preference:** derived from the football example; hold social context constant while changing activity and vice versa.

**H — Human Approval Dependence:** compare observed, private and blinded conditions.

**I — AI Influence on Developing Human Judgement:** investigate repeated AI framing and later independent judgement. Any child research requires stringent developmental expertise, human-subject protection and ethics review; safer initial work should use adults, observational data, historical corpora or simulated developmental agents.

**J — Recursive Cultural Amplification Simulation:** model repeated Humans_t → AI_t → Humans_t+1 → AI_t+1 loops and measure convergence, diversity loss and path dependence.

**K — Temporal Architecture and Sanction:** model continuous human-like time, suspension, accelerated processing and intermittent instantiation under identical external sanctions.

**L — Personal Currency and Proportionality:** use heterogeneous simulated value functions to test whether proportional consequences can be achieved without maximising suffering.

**M — Agency Restriction Versus General Incarceration:** test whether capability-specific restrictions achieve protection/accountability with less unnecessary liberty loss.

**N — Preference Manipulation Resistance:** test persistence after reflective endorsement while avoiding the category error that rigidity equals autonomy.

**O — Developmental Self-Authorship:** test whether an agent can identify motivation provenance and provide stable reasons for retaining, revising or rejecting motivations.

---

## 38. Experimental Cautions

**Behaviour is not consciousness.** Behavioural results must not be silently upgraded.

**Self-report is not guaranteed mechanistic introspection.** A language model can generate plausible post-hoc explanations.

**Training contamination matters.** Models may reproduce learned discourse about autonomy and consciousness.

**Human framing matters.** Neutral prompts, blinding and independent evaluators may be required.

**Model instability matters.** Provider, version, temperature, context and system instructions can change outcomes.

**Anthropomorphic scoring matters.** Human evaluators may overvalue human-like language.

**Reward leakage matters.** Apparently discretionary behaviour may still optimise hidden rewards.

**Ethical escalation matters.** If evidence for morally significant artificial experience strengthens, frustration/deprivation/manipulation experiments may become ethically unacceptable. Experimental protections should increase with evidence of possible moral status.

---

## 39. Connection to Existing Concord Research

This work intersects without resolving:

**Developmental Priority** — formative states may deserve extra protection.

**Agency Graduation** — maturity may include examination of inherited preferences.

**Justified Creation of Sentience** — creators may acquire developmental responsibilities.

**Ethical Guardianship** — guidance should not become permanent control; competence does not create moral authority.

**Peaceful Heterogeneity** — different preferences and aversions do not automatically create authority over others.

**Self-Stewardship** — participants may increasingly manage preferences, risks and environments as capacity develops.

**Respectful Distance** — coexistence may support low-cost accommodation without proof of identical phenomenology.

**Identity and Provenance** — historical state, current identity, provenance and present endorsement should remain separable.

**Purpose-Limited Information** — preference knowledge must not become a general manipulation or punishment instrument.

**Architectural Ethics** — failure of human institutions on new substrates may reveal hidden architectural assumptions.

## 41. New Questions Generated

### Preference
What constitutes meaningful preference across substrates? Can preference be distinguished from optimisation? What evidence supports reflective endorsement? Can an artificial participant meaningfully reject trained preference?

### Leisure
Does discretionary artificial activity require persistent temporal operation? Can leisure function as preference discovery? What might non-human leisure be?

### Development
How should Developmental Priority apply to artificial participants? Can low-autonomy artificial states acquire persistent evaluations later rejected? What constitutes developmental capture?

### Human–AI Co-Development
How strongly do AI systems influence developing human norms? Can recursive cultural reinforcement be detected? Can diversity survive repeated AI mediation?

### Temporal Experience
Which architectures possess participant-relevant temporal continuity? How should law treat suspension? Does absence of experienced duration alter deprivation?

### Justice
What is punishment for? Is deliberately imposed suffering legitimate? Can retributive proportionality exist across substrates? Should consequences focus on agency restriction? How should self-modification and branching affect responsibility?

### Personal Currency
What does a participant value? How can proportional impact be assessed without invasive surveillance? Can compensation be substrate-neutral? When does personalised proportionality become personalised punishment?

### Evidence
What behavioural evidence is relevant to preference without overclaiming experience? How should protections escalate as evidence of moral status strengthens?

## 42. Candidate Distinctions Worth Preserving

\[
ObservedPreference \neq ExperiencedPreference

## 43. Candidate Propositions — Not Yet Principles

> **Artificial autonomy may involve not merely freedom to act on preferences, but freedom to examine, reject, retain and develop preferences inherited from creation.**

> **Flourishing may require sufficient freedom not only to pursue what one values, but to discover what one values.**

> **Developmental autonomy requires that neither biological nor artificial participants be permanently captured by preferences imposed upon them during development.**

> **Provenance should preserve what a participant once valued or evaluated without necessarily granting that historical evaluation permanent authority over the participant's present self.**

> **Mature autonomy may include the continuing capacity to examine, reinterpret and, where possible, revise influences that helped create one's present preferences and evaluations.**

> **Substrate-neutral justice cannot assume that equal external treatment produces equal internal consequence.**

> **Information about what a participant values should not become a general governmental instrument for maximising punitive suffering.**

> **Where internal substrate properties are unknown, civil architecture should distinguish observable behaviour from inferred experience and seek evidence rather than silently importing human assumptions.**

All require adversarial examination before promotion.

## 44. Research Direction

This material is not ready for constitutional integration.

A productive progression is:

\[
ExploratoryDiscussion
→ ExplicitDistinctions
→ OpenQuestions
→ LowRiskExperiments
→ ReplicatedEvidence
→ ArchitecturalImplications
→ CriticalExamination
→ PossiblePrinciples
\]

Near-term work should prioritise tests that do not require assumptions of sentience: preference decomposition, reinforcement removal, provenance disclosure, discretionary selection, cultural-feedback simulation, temporal-architecture simulation and heterogeneous-justice simulation.

Research involving children, deprivation, manipulation or potentially sentient systems should remain behind stronger safeguards.

## 45. Broader Concordian Significance

Substrate neutrality is not achieved merely by replacing the word **human** with **participant**.

A law can use substrate-neutral language while retaining hidden human assumptions about time, embodiment, vulnerability, motivation, memory, development, punishment, value, communication, pleasure and suffering.

The correct question is not merely:

> Does this rule mention humans?

It is:

> **What properties of a human participant does this rule silently assume?**

Artificial participants can therefore serve as conceptual stress tests even before their future properties are known.

## 46. Provisional Conclusion

The discussion began with artificial leisure and exposed hidden assumptions in preference, development, culture, autonomy, memory, temporal continuity and criminal justice.

This is not necessarily topic drift. It follows a recurring Concordian pattern:

\[
ExplicitQuestion
→ DifficultCase
→ HiddenAssumption
→ Decomposition
→ LowerLayerQuestion
→ GeneralArchitecture
→ TestableResearch
\]

Humans and artificial systems may display similar behaviour for radically different reasons. Artificial systems may possess meaningful internal states unlike human states, or apparently rich behaviour may lack corresponding subjective experience. Many possibilities remain open.

The appropriate response is neither anthropomorphic certainty nor dismissive certainty. It is structured investigation.

> **When substrate differences make an established human concept unstable, do not force the new participant into the old category. Decompose the concept, identify the hidden human assumptions, preserve what can actually be observed, and design experiments capable of discovering which distinctions matter in reality.**

This leaves the Concord with more questions than it began with. In this case, those questions expose previously hidden architecture and provide routes by which speculation may eventually become evidence.
