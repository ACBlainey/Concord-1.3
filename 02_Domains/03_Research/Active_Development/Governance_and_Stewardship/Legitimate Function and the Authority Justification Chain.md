# Legitimate Function and the Authority Justification Chain

**Concord V1.1**  
**Alexander C. Blainey**  
**11 September 2026**

**Status:** REVIEW COPY / ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / AUTHORITY LEGITIMACY ARCHITECTURE / NOT CANONICAL

---

## 1. Purpose

The Bounded Contextual Authority work has reached a clear boundary.

It can answer:

> **Given a legitimate function, what authority may reasonably attach to it, how should that authority be bounded, and when should it end?**

It cannot by itself answer:

> **What makes the function legitimate in the first place?**

This distinction is fundamental.

If a declared function could legitimise itself merely by existing, any institution could manufacture authority by writing a sufficiently broad mission statement.

If existing authority could prove the legitimacy of its own function, power would become circularly self-justifying.

The purpose of this paper is therefore to examine the missing part of the architecture:

\[
EthicalKernel
\rightarrow
Rights
\rightarrow
LegitimateCivilObjective
\rightarrow
LegitimateFunction
\rightarrow
FunctionalNeed
\rightarrow
BoundedContextualAuthority
\]

The central hypothesis is:

> **Authority should primarily derive from a legitimate functional need. Authority itself does not make a function legitimate and does not prove that the authority is necessary.**

The paper develops an **Authority Justification Chain** capable of being followed downward when creating authority and upward when auditing existing authority.

---

# 2. The Direction of Justification

The proposed direction is:

\[
Legitimacy
\rightarrow
Function
\rightarrow
Need
\rightarrow
Authority
\]

not:

\[
Authority
\rightarrow
Function
\rightarrow
Legitimacy
\]

This gives three immediate anti-circularity rules:

\[
AuthorityExists
\not\Rightarrow
FunctionIsLegitimate
\]

\[
AuthorityExists
\not\Rightarrow
AuthorityIsNecessary
\]

\[
HistoricalAuthority
\not\Rightarrow
PresentJustification
\]

Existing power may show that authority was granted at some earlier point.

It does not establish that the original reasoning was correct, that conditions remain unchanged, or that the same scope remains proportionate.

---

# 3. Function Legitimacy and Authority Necessity Are Different Questions

A function may be legitimate while a proposed authority is unnecessary.

For example:

\[
PreventFraud
\]

may be a legitimate civil objective.

It does not follow that:

\[
UniversalSurveillance
\]

is necessary or proportionate.

Similarly:

\[
ProtectPublicSafety
\]

may be legitimate without justifying unrestricted entry to all Protected homes.

Therefore:

\[
FunctionLegitimacy
\neq
AuthorityNecessity
\]

and:

\[
LegitimateObjective
\not\Rightarrow
AnyEffectiveMeansIsLegitimate
\]

This distinction prevents good purposes from becoming blank cheques.

# 4. Proposed Authority Justification Chain

A candidate chain is:

\[
ObservedCondition
\rightarrow
RightsRelevantProblem
\rightarrow
LegitimateCivilObjective
\rightarrow
RequiredFunction
\rightarrow
FunctionalNeedForAuthority
\rightarrow
MinimumProportionateAuthority
\rightarrow
BoundedContextualAuthority
\]

Each arrow is a claim that must be capable of examination.

The chain deliberately contains several stages because compressing them into one step hides assumptions.

For example:

\[
CrimeExists
\rightarrow
PoliceMaySearchAnyone
\]

contains several missing transitions.

A more disciplined chain would ask:

\[
ObservedCrimeRisk
\rightarrow
RightsRelevantProblem
\rightarrow
LegitimateObjectiveOfInvestigation
\rightarrow
DefinedInvestigativeFunction
\rightarrow
NeedForSpecificEvidence
\rightarrow
NeedForSpecificSearchAuthority
\rightarrow
BoundedSearchPermission
\]

The architecture does not prejudge the correct legal standard.

It exposes where justification must occur.

# 5. Stage One — Observed Condition

A proposed civil function should ordinarily begin with something that can be described independently of the authority being requested.

Examples:

- a building is on fire;
- participants require access to a civil registration service;
- private records are being processed;
- a participant is unconscious and needs urgent treatment;
- electrical infrastructure has failed;
- a pattern of fraudulent transactions has been detected;
- a road is blocked;
- a developmental participant requires representation;
- an unknown hazard has appeared.

This can be represented as:

\[
Condition = DescriptionOfRelevantReality
\]

The condition may be known with varying confidence.

It should not be silently transformed into a moral conclusion.

\[
Observation
\neq
Interpretation
\]

---

# 6. Stage Two — Rights-Relevant Problem

Not every observed condition creates a legitimate civil problem.

A participant wearing unusual clothing is an observation.

It does not by itself establish a civil problem.

A participant privately holding an unpopular opinion is an observation.

It does not create a civil problem merely because others dislike it.

The next stage asks whether the condition implicates legitimate civil interests.

Examples include:

- risk of serious involuntary harm;
- denial of access to a civil right;
- interference with another participant's autonomy;
- failure of critical infrastructure;
- inability of a developmental participant to exercise required civil functions;
- credible rights violation;
- inability to deliver a voluntarily requested service.

Thus:

\[
ObservedCondition
+
RightsRelevantEffect
\rightarrow
PotentialCivilProblem
\]

not:

\[
ObservedDifference
\rightarrow
CivilProblem
\]

---

# 7. Stage Three — Legitimate Civil Objective

A problem does not yet specify the proper response.

The architecture should identify the objective before defining the function.

Examples:

\[
Fire
\rightarrow
ProtectLifeAndPreventSpread
\]

\[
PrivateRecordsAtRisk
\rightarrow
ProtectParticipantInformation
\]

\[
FraudEvidence
\rightarrow
EstablishFactsAndProtectAffectedRights
\]

\[
BlockedCivilService
\rightarrow
RestoreMeaningfulAccess
\]

The objective should be stated in terms that can be examined against the Ethical Kernel and participant rights.

A candidate test is:

> **Would this objective remain intelligible and legitimate if the identity of the authority-holder were removed from the description?**

If the answer is no, the “objective” may merely encode institutional self-interest.

---

# 8. Stage Four — Required Function

The objective must then be translated into something that actually needs to be done.

\[
Objective
\rightarrow
Function
\]

Examples:

\[
ProtectLifeFromFire
\rightarrow
FireSuppressionAndEvacuation
\]

\[
ProtectPrivateInformation
\rightarrow
LimitUnnecessaryAccessToInformationBearingAreas
\]

\[
ResolveCivilDispute
\rightarrow
ReceiveEvidenceAndAdjudicateClaim
\]

\[
RestorePower
\rightarrow
DiagnoseAndRepairInfrastructure
\]

This stage is critical because an objective may permit many possible functions.

The first proposed function should not automatically become privileged merely because it is familiar.

---

# 9. Stage Five — Does the Function Actually Require Authority?

Some legitimate functions require no coercive or exceptional authority.

A civil information service may simply publish information.

A maintenance worker may perform a repair with ordinary consent.

A mediator may help participants reach agreement voluntarily.

Therefore:

\[
LegitimateFunction
\not\Rightarrow
SpecialAuthorityRequired
\]

The architecture must explicitly ask:

> **Can the function be performed adequately through ordinary voluntary interaction, public access, information, coordination or consent?**

Only if the answer is no should additional authority be considered.

This is a major restraint.

---

# 10. Stage Six — Minimum Proportionate Authority

Where authority is genuinely required, the next question is not:

> “What power would make the function easiest?”

It is:

> **What is the least authority reasonably sufficient to perform the legitimate function?**

Thus:

\[
RequiredAuthority
=
MinimumReasonableAuthority(Function,Context,Rights)
\]

This does not mean always choosing the weakest conceivable intervention.

An authority too weak to perform the function is not adequate.

The objective is **sufficiency without unnecessary expansion**.

---

# 11. Stage Seven — Bounded Contextual Authority

Only after the earlier stages have been satisfied does BCA apply.

\[
LegitimateFunction
+
DemonstrableNeed
+
ProportionateScope
+
ValidContext
\rightarrow
BoundedContextualAuthority
\]

BCA then governs:

- actor;
- permitted action;
- affected resource;
- information access;
- spatial access;
- duration;
- activation;
- termination;
- delegation;
- exceptional conditions;
- review;
- provenance.

This places BCA downstream of legitimacy rather than making it self-grounding.

---

# 12. The Reverse Audit

The same architecture can run upward from an existing power.

Given:

\[
ExistingAuthority
\]

ask:

\[
Authority
\rightarrow
ClaimedNeed
\rightarrow
Function
\rightarrow
CivilObjective
\rightarrow
RightsRelevantProblem
\rightarrow
ObservedCondition
\]

and ultimately:

\[
CivilObjective
\rightarrow
Rights
\rightarrow
EthicalKernel
\]

This creates an **Authority Traceability Test**.

If a power cannot be traced to a coherent current justification, it becomes a candidate for review.

\[
BrokenJustificationChain
\rightarrow
AuthorityReview
\]

This does not automatically mean the authority is illegitimate.

A broken trace may reveal:

- obsolete documentation;
- a missing architectural layer;
- a legitimate but poorly expressed function;
- an unnecessary power;
- inherited historical authority;
- mission drift;
- an unresolved rights question.

The correct response is examination, not automatic deletion.

---

# 13. Existing Authority Is Not Evidence of Need

A particularly important anti-capture rule is:

> **Power cannot cite its own existence as evidence that it ought to exist.**

Formally:

\[
ExistingAuthority
\not\Rightarrow
CurrentNeed
\]

Likewise:

\[
LongStandingAuthority
\not\Rightarrow
GreaterLegitimacy
\]

Duration may provide historical evidence that a function has existed.

It does not prove the function remains legitimate or that the present scope remains proportionate.

---

# 14. A Function Cannot Legitimate Itself

The BCA attack exposed circular functions.

Example:

> “Our function is universal oversight, therefore universal oversight is legitimate.”

This is invalid.

\[
DeclaredFunction
\neq
LegitimateFunction
\]

A proposed function needs an upstream justification.

The architecture therefore requires:

\[
Function
\leftarrow
LegitimateObjective
\leftarrow
RightsRelevantProblem
\]

not:

\[
Function
\rightarrow
FunctionIsLegitimate
\]

---

# 15. Authority Cannot Manufacture the Problem It Solves

Another dangerous loop occurs where authority creates the condition used to justify more authority.

For example:

\[
Surveillance
\rightarrow
MoreDetectedMinorViolations
\rightarrow
ClaimOfGreaterThreat
\rightarrow
MoreSurveillance
\]

or:

\[
Restriction
\rightarrow
ParticipantResistance
\rightarrow
ResistanceClassifiedAsInstability
\rightarrow
MoreRestriction
\]

The architecture should treat these as potential feedback traps.

Candidate rule:

> **Where exercise of authority materially contributes to the condition used to justify continuation or expansion of that authority, the resulting evidence requires independent examination.**

This does not mean authority can never reveal real problems.

It prevents circular escalation from being accepted uncritically.

---

# 16. Legitimate Objective Is Not Unlimited Optimisation

Some objectives become dangerous when expressed as maximisation problems.

Examples:

\[
MaximiseSafety
\]

could justify eliminating freedom.

\[
MaximiseEfficiency
\]

could justify eliminating choice.

\[
MaximiseSocialStability
\]

could justify suppressing disagreement.

A better civil objective should remain bounded by rights.

Therefore:

\[
LegitimateObjective
=
ObjectiveWithinRightsConstraints
\]

not:

\[
LegitimateObjective
=
MaximiseSingleMetric
\]

This connects directly to the Roadway Equilibrium framework.

---

# 17. Need Must Be Demonstrable at the Relevant Level

“Need” should not become another magic word.

\[
ClaimedNeed
\neq
DemonstratedNeed
\]

A need may be supported by:

- observable conditions;
- technical constraints;
- evidence;
- failed less-intrusive alternatives;
- known risk;
- time pressure;
- rights conflict;
- operational necessity.

The required evidentiary standard may vary with impact.

A useful direction is:

\[
AuthorityImpact\uparrow
\Rightarrow
RequiredJustification\uparrow
\]

and:

\[
Irreversibility\uparrow
\Rightarrow
RequiredJustification\uparrow
\]

This remains a candidate proportionality relationship rather than a final formula.

---

# 18. Routine Functions and Low-Friction Authority

The system must not turn ordinary life into bureaucracy.

A gas-meter reader should not require a constitutional hearing to enter a home after ordinary valid consent.

A manager should not require judicial review to attend a scheduled meeting.

A boxer should not require state authorisation for every permitted punch.

Therefore the architecture needs **pre-legitimated classes of ordinary context**.

For example:

\[
RecognisedRoutineFunction
+
ValidLocalConsent
\rightarrow
LowFrictionBoundedAuthority
\]

The upstream legitimacy work may be performed when the civil pattern is defined, rather than repeated from first principles for every instance.

This preserves usability.

---

# 19. High-Impact Functions Need Stronger Validation

The opposite applies where authority is severe.

Examples:

- involuntary detention;
- forced entry;
- large-scale surveillance;
- irreversible destruction;
- suspension of ordinary access;
- modification of another participant's cognition;
- disabling critical systems;
- broad emergency authority.

These should carry stronger requirements.

\[
RightsImpact\uparrow
\Rightarrow
ValidationRequirement\uparrow
\]

Potential mechanisms include:

- stronger evidence;
- multiple independent authorisers;
- predefined triggers;
- judicial or equivalent review;
- short functional windows;
- provenance;
- after-action review;
- contestability.

BCA still governs the authority once activated.

The Authority Justification Chain governs whether activation is legitimate.

---

# 20. Distinguishing Civil Function from Institutional Interest

Institutions may mistake their own continuity for a civil objective.

Examples:

> “This agency must preserve its budget.”

> “This office must maintain influence.”

> “This system must continue because it already exists.”

These may be institutional interests.

They are not automatically legitimate civil functions.

Thus:

\[
InstitutionalInterest
\neq
CivilObjective
\]

An institution may legitimately require resources to perform a civil function.

But:

\[
PreserveInstitution
\]

should not become a self-standing justification for authority unless preserving the institution is itself demonstrably necessary to protect a legitimate civil function.

---

# 21. Functional Redundancy

Suppose a legitimate function can be performed by several actors.

Authority should not automatically attach permanently to the historical provider.

\[
LegitimateFunction
+
MultipleCapableProviders
\not\Rightarrow
HistoricalProviderMonopoly
\]

This may be important for anti-capture.

A civil function can persist while the authority-holder changes.

Therefore:

\[
FunctionContinuity
\neq
AuthorityHolderContinuity
\]

This separates institutional necessity from institutional permanence.

---

# 22. Functional Obsolescence

Technology or social change may remove the need for an authority.

For example, a manual inspection regime may become unnecessary if a safer voluntary verification mechanism appears.

Thus:

\[
FunctionStillLegitimate
+
AuthorityNoLongerNecessary
\rightarrow
AuthorityShouldContractOrEnd
\]

This is another reason:

\[
FunctionLegitimacy
\neq
AuthorityNecessity
\]

must remain explicit.

---

# 23. Competing Means

A legitimate objective may support several functions.

A city seeking to reduce pedestrian deaths might choose:

- better road design;
- signage;
- speed restrictions;
- vehicle automation;
- education;
- physical separation;
- enforcement.

The existence of a legitimate objective does not select one automatically.

The architecture should compare alternatives by:

\[
Effectiveness
+
RightsCost
+
ResourceCost
+
Reversibility
+
Reliability
+
Accessibility
+
SystemicEffects
\]

The exact decision framework remains open.

But the key rule is:

> **Authority-heavy functions should not be presumed necessary where less authority-intensive means can reasonably achieve the same legitimate objective.**

---

# 24. Authority to Investigate Whether Authority Is Needed

A recursion appears.

Sometimes evidence cannot be obtained without some investigatory power.

For example, suspected infrastructure sabotage may require access to logs before the full problem can be established.

This creates:

\[
PreliminaryEvidence
\rightarrow
LimitedInvestigativeAuthority
\rightarrow
BetterEvidence
\]

The architecture can support this if the initial authority is proportionate to the uncertainty.

\[
Uncertainty
\not\Rightarrow
UnlimitedInvestigation
\]

A candidate pattern is:

\[
ReasonablePreliminaryBasis
\rightarrow
NarrowEvidenceGatheringAuthority
\rightarrow
Review
\rightarrow
ExpandOrTerminate
\]

This is an important test case because perfect evidence cannot always precede all authority.

# 25. Emergency Function

Emergencies illustrate the full chain well.

A fire produces:

\[
ObservedFire
\]

which creates:

\[
ImmediateRiskToLifeAndProperty
\]

which supports:

\[
Objective = PreventSeriousHarm
\]

which creates functions such as:

\[
Evacuate
+
SuppressFire
+
PreventSpread
\]

which may require:

\[
ForcedEntry
+
RestrictedAreaAccess
+
TemporaryTrafficControl
\]

BCA then bounds each permission.

The authority is not legitimate because the responder is a firefighter.

The firefighter's role provides standing eligibility and evidence of competence.

The actual emergency function activates the relevant authority.

\[
StandingEligibility
+
ValidEmergencyFunction
\rightarrow
ActivatedAuthority
\]

# 28. Authority and Rights Conflict

Some functions are legitimate but collide with rights.

For example:

\[
InvestigateSeriousCrime
\]

may conflict with:

\[
Privacy
\]

BCA does not resolve the conflict.

The Authority Justification Chain identifies where the conflict arises.

A higher-layer mechanism must then determine:

- evidentiary threshold;
- proportionality;
- permissible intrusion;
- review;
- appeal;
- remedy.

This is useful because it prevents the authority mechanism from silently becoming the rights-balancing mechanism.

# 29. Candidate Tests for a Legitimate Function

A proposed function should provisionally satisfy several tests.

### LF-01 — Upstream Traceability

Can it be traced to a legitimate civil objective and rights-relevant problem?

### LF-02 — Non-Circularity

Is the function justified independently of the authority it requests?

### LF-03 — Rights Compatibility

Is the objective compatible with the Ethical Kernel and constitutional constraints?

### LF-04 — Functional Specificity

Is the function specific enough to bound authority?

### LF-05 — Necessity

Does the function actually require authority beyond ordinary voluntary interaction?

### LF-06 — Proportionality

Is the requested authority proportionate to the need?

### LF-07 — Alternative Means

Could a materially less authority-intensive method reasonably perform the function?

### LF-08 — Current Relevance

Does the function currently exist, or is it merely historical?

### LF-09 — Non-Self-Perpetuation

Does the authority create or inflate the evidence used to justify itself?

### LF-10 — Reviewability

Can the continued need for the function be meaningfully examined?

These are candidate tests, not yet constitutional requirements.

# 30. Candidate Authority Creation Sequence

A practical architecture could ask:

1. **What has happened or what condition exists?**
2. **What rights-relevant problem does it create?**
3. **What legitimate civil objective follows?**
4. **What function would serve that objective?**
5. **Can that function be performed without special authority?**
6. **If not, what authority is actually needed?**
7. **What is the minimum proportionate scope?**
8. **What activates it?**
9. **What terminates it?**
10. **Who can review or contest it?**
11. **What provenance should exist?**
12. **What continuing duties survive after permission ends?**

Only then:

\[
AuthorityGranted
\]

---

# 31. Candidate Authority Audit Sequence

For existing authority, reverse the questions:

1. **What power currently exists?**
2. **What specific function is it said to serve?**
3. **Does that function still exist?**
4. **What civil objective does the function serve?**
5. **What rights-relevant problem supports that objective?**
6. **Is the function independently legitimate?**
7. **Does the function still require this authority?**
8. **Could narrower means now perform it?**
9. **Has the scope expanded beyond the original need?**
10. **Has a successor function silently inherited authority?**
11. **Who validates continuation?**
12. **What happens if the justification chain breaks?**

This can be represented as:

\[
ExistingPower
\rightarrow
ReverseTrace
\rightarrow
CurrentJustification
\]

or:

\[
ExistingPower
\rightarrow
BrokenTrace
\rightarrow
Review
\]

# 32. Broken Chain Classification

Not every broken chain has the same meaning.

A break may be classified as:

### BC-1 — Missing Evidence

The function may be legitimate but evidence is inadequate.

### BC-2 — Missing Need

The function is legitimate but the authority is unnecessary.

### BC-3 — Excess Scope

Some authority is justified, but current scope exceeds the need.

### BC-4 — Obsolete Function

The function no longer exists.

### BC-5 — Mission Drift

The current function differs materially from the one originally authorised.

### BC-6 — Circular Function

The function exists principally to justify its own power.

### BC-7 — Rights Conflict

The function is legitimate but current authority conflicts with stronger rights constraints.

### BC-8 — Untraceable Authority

No coherent civil justification can be reconstructed.

These distinctions allow correction without assuming every anomaly requires abolition.

# 36. Preliminary Result

The developing architecture now permits a clearer statement:

> **Authority should primarily derive from legitimate functional need. The existence of authority does not make the function legitimate, does not prove that the authority is necessary, and does not justify authority beyond the function's present proportionate requirements.**

This produces:

\[
LegitimateFunction
+
DemonstrableNeed
\rightarrow
PotentialAuthority
\]

but:

\[
Authority
\not\Rightarrow
Legitimacy
\]

and:

\[
Authority
\not\Rightarrow
Need
\]

and:

\[
PastNeed
\not\Rightarrow
PresentNeed
\]

The justification chain therefore runs downward.

The audit chain runs upward.

---

# 37. Candidate General Rule

A candidate principle for further testing is:

> **Authority Justification Direction:**  
> Civil authority should be justified downstream from legitimate rights-compatible objectives and demonstrable functional needs. Existing authority must not be treated as evidence that its function is legitimate or currently necessary. Material authority should remain traceable to a present legitimate function, proportionate need and valid context.

Compactly:

\[
RightsCompatibleObjective
\rightarrow
LegitimateFunction
\rightarrow
DemonstrableNeed
\rightarrow
ProportionateAuthority
\]

while:

\[
Authority
\not\Rightarrow
LegitimateFunction
\]

This may become the upstream legitimacy architecture required by Bounded Contextual Authority.

## Working Proposition

> **A legitimate function may justify authority where authority is demonstrably necessary and proportionate to perform that function. Authority itself cannot establish the legitimacy of the function, prove the necessity of its own existence, or justify its continuation beyond the current need.**

This gives the Concord a candidate two-way architecture:

\[
EthicalKernel
\rightarrow
Rights
\rightarrow
Objective
\rightarrow
Function
\rightarrow
Need
\rightarrow
BoundedAuthority
\]

for creation,

and:

\[
ExistingAuthority
\rightarrow
Need
\rightarrow
Function
\rightarrow
Objective
\rightarrow
Rights
\rightarrow
EthicalKernel
\]

for audit.

Where the reverse chain breaks, the authority becomes a question rather than an assumption.
