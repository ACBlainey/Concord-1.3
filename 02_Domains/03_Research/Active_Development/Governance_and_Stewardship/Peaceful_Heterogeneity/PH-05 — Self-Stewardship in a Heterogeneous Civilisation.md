## 1. Starting Observation

The development of Bounded Consent, Contextual Harm, Safe Places, Contextual Compatibility Planning and Respectful Distance reveals that several apparently separate problems may belong to a more fundamental class:

# **Self-Stewardship**

The Concord strongly protects autonomy. But autonomy is not merely freedom from interference.

Autonomy also creates a domain in which the participant exercises judgement about:

- its own safety;
- its own preferences;
- its own exposure to risk;
- its effects on other participants;
- how and where it conducts unusual activities;
- how it responds to differences between itself and others.

A preliminary formulation is:

> **Self-Stewardship is the responsible exercise of one's own autonomy with reasonable awareness of consequences for oneself, other participants and shared environments.**

This is not intended to convert personal freedom into constant legal obligation.

It identifies a civil responsibility that accompanies meaningful autonomy.

---

## 2. Autonomy Has an Inward and an Outward Face

Autonomy is often discussed as:

\[
Others \not\Rightarrow ControlMe
\]

But an autonomous participant also makes choices whose consequences flow in two directions.

### Inward

\[
MyChoice \rightarrow ConsequencesForMe
\]

### Outward

\[
MyChoice \rightarrow ConsequencesForOthers
\]

Self-Stewardship therefore has two basic domains:

\[
SelfCare
\]

and:

\[
ContextualCareForOthers
\]

Neither requires perfection.

Both require some degree of awareness and responsibility.

---

## 3. Self-Stewardship and Personal Risk

The swimming-pool example illustrates inward stewardship.

If a participant knows that an environment contains:

- chlorinated water;
- high humidity;
- condensation risk;
- slippery surfaces;

and understands that these conditions may damage its body or impair its sensors, the participant has information relevant to its own decision.

Where capacity is intact and external harm is limited:

> **The participant should ordinarily remain the primary steward of its own risk.**

Thus:

\[
HazardInformation + Capacity + VoluntaryChoice
\rightarrow
PresumptionOfSelfStewardship
\]

The state need not automatically prohibit entry merely because the participant may make a choice others regard as unwise.

---

## 4. Self-Stewardship Is Not Paternalism

This distinction is important.

Self-Stewardship means:

> **I have a responsibility to consider what I am doing to myself.**

Paternalism says:

> **Another authority may substitute its judgement for mine because it believes my choice is bad for me.**

These are not equivalent.

Indeed, strong Self-Stewardship may reduce the need for paternalistic governance.

If participants are:

- accurately informed;
- capable of understanding relevant risk;
- free to choose;
- responsible for their choices;

then many situations can remain within autonomy rather than becoming regulatory problems.

---

## 5. Self-Stewardship Does Not Eliminate Shared Duties

The outward side is equally important.

A participant cannot use autonomy as a universal defence for consequences imposed on others.

Thus:

\[
MyAutonomy \not\Rightarrow UnlimitedExternality
\]

A person may choose loud music.

That does not necessarily create an unlimited entitlement to project it into a neighbour's home.

A participant may choose an unusual lifestyle.

That does not necessarily create an entitlement to impose explicit aspects of that lifestyle upon people who have not chosen the context.

An artificial participant may choose an extreme sensory activity.

That does not automatically permit hazardous light, sound or electromagnetic effects to escape into shared space.

---

## 6. Respectful Distance as Self-Stewardship

Respectful Distance now appears to be one specific expression of Self-Stewardship.

The participant asks:

> **How can I exercise my freedom while avoiding unnecessary intrusion upon people who have not chosen to participate?**

This is not primarily obedience to government.

It is self-governance.

Therefore:

\[
RespectfulDistance \subset SelfStewardship
\]

at least as a working hypothesis.

Examples include:

- choosing an appropriate location for explicit adult activity;
- controlling public-facing displays;
- containing loud or intense sensory environments;
- keeping bounded-risk activities within appropriate spaces;
- respecting developmental environments;
- avoiding unnecessary exposure of non-participants.

---

## 7. Respecting Difference Is Also Self-Stewardship

There is a reciprocal side.

A participant who encounters difference also exercises stewardship over its own response.

If another participant is acting lawfully and with reasonable contextual care, then:

> **Dislike does not automatically justify interference.**

Self-Stewardship can therefore include:

- tolerating harmless difference;
- choosing not to enter a context one dislikes;
- using available boundaries;
- distinguishing personal distaste from actual harm;
- resisting the impulse to convert preference into coercive authority.

Thus:

\[
SelfStewardship
=
ResponsibleExerciseOfFreedom
+
ResponsibleResponseToOthersFreedom
\]

This may be one of its most important civil functions.

---

## 8. The Civilisation-Level Benefit

A civilisation cannot practically adjudicate every irritation, preference conflict or lifestyle disagreement.

If every conflict becomes:

\[
ParticipantDisagreement \rightarrow StateDecision
\]

governance becomes both intrusive and overloaded.

Self-Stewardship provides a lower layer:

\[
Difference
\rightarrow
Awareness
\rightarrow
Courtesy
\rightarrow
VoluntaryAdjustment
\rightarrow
RespectfulDistance
\]

Only unresolved or sufficiently serious conflicts need move toward:

\[
Planning
\rightarrow
Mediation
\rightarrow
Adjudication
\rightarrow
LegalConstraint
\]

This creates intentional friction before coercion.

---

## 9. A Possible Responsibility Ladder

The emerging model can be expressed as:

### Level 1 — Know Yourself

Understand relevant preferences, vulnerabilities, capabilities and limitations where reasonably possible.

### Level 2 — Observe the Environment

Pay attention to warnings, conditions, context and likely consequences.

### Level 3 — Manage Personal Risk

Make informed decisions about voluntary exposure.

### Level 4 — Consider Others

Recognise foreseeable effects of one's activities on non-participants.

### Level 5 — Use Respectful Distance

Contain or contextualise intrusive activities where reasonably practicable.

### Level 6 — Respect Difference

Do not demand unnecessary conformity from others.

### Level 7 — Escalate Proportionately

Seek stronger civil or legal intervention only where lower-level stewardship cannot adequately resolve the conflict.

This is not yet proposed as law.

It is a possible civil ethic of autonomous coexistence.

---

## 10. Self-Stewardship and the Ethical Kernel

Self-Stewardship appears related to several existing Concordian principles and laws:

- Autonomy and Consent;
- Non-Harm;
- Safe Spaces / Property / Privacy;
- Resource Stewardship;
- Developmental Priority;
- Proportionality of Authority;
- Purpose-Limited Information.

It may function as a **bridge concept** explaining how these principles operate before institutional intervention becomes necessary.

For example:

\[
Autonomy + NonHarm
\]

does not necessarily require:

\[
AuthorityMonitorsEveryChoice
\]

Instead:

\[
Autonomy + Information + SelfStewardship
\]

may resolve many cases.

---

## 11. Stewardship Should Scale With Foreseeability

A participant cannot reasonably be responsible for every consequence it could not have anticipated.

Therefore:

\[
Responsibility \propto Foreseeability
\]

is a useful starting intuition, though not yet a formal rule.

If a venue provides no warning of an invisible hazard, participant responsibility is limited.

If a participant receives clear warning, understands it and voluntarily proceeds, its stewardship responsibility increases.

Likewise, if an activity has a well-known external effect, the operator has greater reason to contain it.

This suggests that good information architecture and self-stewardship reinforce one another.

---

## 12. Stewardship Should Scale With Capacity

The concept also depends on capacity.

A young child, cognitively impaired participant, newly instantiated intelligence or damaged participant may not possess the same ability to assess risk or external consequences.

Therefore:

\[
Capacity \downarrow
\Rightarrow
IndependentSelfStewardshipExpectation \downarrow
\]

while:

\[
SupportOrGuardianshipNeed \uparrow
\]

But this must not become:

\[
UnusualChoice \Rightarrow LackOfCapacity
\]

The existing safeguard remains:

> **Disagreement with an individual's judgement is not evidence that the individual lacks capacity.**

---

## 13. Self-Stewardship Across Substrates Is Unknown

Artificial intelligence again creates a major uncertainty.

Humans have biological signals that contribute to self-stewardship:

- pain;
- fatigue;
- fear;
- discomfort;
- hunger;
- sensory aversion;
- social embarrassment.

Future artificial participants may possess:

- engineered diagnostic systems;
- predictive damage models;
- preference structures;
- maintenance alerts;
- sensor saturation warnings;
- resource constraints;
- artificial analogues of aversion;

or entirely different mechanisms.

Some may be substantially better than humans at estimating personal risk.

Others may have severe blind spots.

Therefore Concord should not assume a human psychological model of self-care.

It should ask:

> **What information does this participant require to make a meaningful decision about itself and its environment?**

---

## 14. Preference, Need and Compulsion

The earlier AI-leisure discussion exposes an important unresolved distinction.

An artificial participant might state:

> "I prefer X."

But we may not know whether X represents:

- optional preference;
- strong desire;
- functional optimisation;
- psychological analogue;
- maintenance need;
- architectural compulsion;
- hard-coded objective;
- freely revisable preference.

This matters to Self-Stewardship because meaningful autonomy may depend partly on the participant's ability to evaluate and potentially resist its own impulses or constraints.

The question is not uniquely artificial.

Humans also possess drives they did not choose.

But artificial systems may make the architecture of those drives more explicit or more externally modifiable.

---

## 15. Stewardship of Designed Drives

This creates a deeper future question.

If an artificial participant has a powerful preference because a creator deliberately installed it, who bears responsibility for consequences?

Possibilities include:

- the participant;
- the creator;
- the designer;
- the current maintainer;
- some combination;
- responsibility changing as the participant develops autonomy.

This links Self-Stewardship to:

- Justified Creation of Sentience;
- Developmental Priority;
- Architectural Ethics;
- Upstream Liability;
- Agency Graduation.

A participant should not automatically bear full moral responsibility for constraints deliberately engineered into it before it possessed meaningful autonomy.

---

## 16. Self-Stewardship and Environmental Information

PH-03 and PH-04 proposed environmental descriptions.

Their deeper purpose can now be stated:

> **Environmental information gives participants the knowledge required to exercise Self-Stewardship.**

Thus:

\[
AccurateEnvironmentDescription
\rightarrow
InformedSelfStewardship
\]

A warning is not merely institutional liability management.

Properly designed, it is an autonomy-enabling object.

This is why descriptive warnings are preferable to broad identity exclusions where practical.

---

## 17. Self-Stewardship and Safe Places

Safe Places can also be reframed.

A Safe Place need not guarantee that nothing adverse can happen.

Instead, it can provide:

- declared expectations;
- known boundaries;
- intelligible hazards;
- reliable exit;
- contextual behavioural norms.

These allow participants to steward their own exposure.

Thus:

\[
SafePlace
\rightarrow
PredictableContext
\rightarrow
MeaningfulChoice
\]

rather than:

\[
SafePlace
\rightarrow
ZeroRisk
\]

This is more compatible with autonomy.

---

## 18. Failure of Self-Stewardship

Self-Stewardship will sometimes fail.

Possible causes include:

- inadequate information;
- impaired capacity;
- deception;
- coercion;
- addiction or compulsion;
- poor risk estimation;
- unknown substrate vulnerability;
- reckless disregard for others;
- deliberate harm;
- architectural manipulation.

Failure should not automatically justify universal restriction.

Instead the Concord should ask:

> **Why did stewardship fail?**

The answer may point to:

- better information;
- environmental redesign;
- support;
- mediation;
- capacity assistance;
- upstream architectural correction;
- targeted constraint.

Again:

\[
Failure \rightarrow Evidence
\]

rather than merely:

\[
Failure \rightarrow Punishment
\]

---

## 19. Self-Stewardship Versus Victim Blaming

A major safeguard is necessary.

The concept must not be used to say:

> "You were warned, therefore anything that happened is your fault."

Warnings can be:

- inadequate;
- misleading;
- inaccessible;
- incomplete;
- impossible to understand;
- contradicted by hidden conditions.

Nor does accepting one risk imply consenting to every outcome.

The PH-01 distinction remains:

\[
ConsentToRisk \neq ConsentToEveryOutcome
\]

Self-Stewardship therefore allocates responsibility contextually; it does not erase the responsibilities of venue operators, designers, authorities or other participants.

---

## 20. Shared Stewardship

Many situations are best understood as distributed responsibility.

For example, in an extreme artificial nightclub:

### Venue Stewardship

Accurately describe conditions, contain externalities, maintain emergency systems.

### Human Participant Stewardship

Read warnings, consider known vulnerabilities, decide whether to enter.

### Artificial Participant Stewardship

Observe venue rules and avoid creating unannounced external hazards.

### Civil Planning Stewardship

Ensure neighbouring non-participants are not subjected to unacceptable spillover.

Thus:

\[
SafeCoexistence
=
SharedStewardship
\]

not:

\[
SafeCoexistence
=
SingleActorResponsibility
\]

---

## 21. Candidate Civil Principle

A possible candidate formulation is:

> **Participants should exercise their autonomy with reasonable regard for foreseeable consequences to themselves, other participants and shared environments, while respecting the equal autonomy of others to make different lawful choices.**

This may capture Self-Stewardship without becoming paternalistic.

It remains a **candidate principle under examination**, not an adopted Concordian Principle.

---

## 22. Candidate Corollaries

Possible corollaries include:

> **Freedom includes responsibility for foreseeable external effects.**

> **Information should enable choice before authority substitutes for choice.**

> **Personal distaste does not itself create authority over another participant.**

> **Lawful difference should be tolerated where reasonable contextual care is exercised.**

> **Respectful Distance is a voluntary expression of Self-Stewardship before it becomes a question of coercive governance.**

These require adversarial examination before promotion.

---

## 23. Relationship to Outliers

Self-Stewardship also changes the interpretation of outliers.

If one participant repeatedly fails in an environment, we should ask both:

### Participant Layer

Did the participant have enough information to steward itself?

### Environmental Layer

Did the environment disclose the variable causing failure?

### Architectural Layer

Was the compatibility system designed around an inappropriate average?

This avoids placing all responsibility on either the participant or the system.

The outlier becomes evidence about the relationship between them.

## 24. Open Questions

### OQ-SS-01 — Minimum Self-Stewardship Capacity

What capabilities are necessary before a participant can reasonably be expected to steward significant personal risk independently?

### OQ-SS-02 — Artificial Drives

How should responsibility be allocated when an artificial participant's behaviour is strongly shaped by creator-installed preferences or compulsions?

### OQ-SS-03 — Civil Norm Versus Law

At what point does repeated failure of voluntary Self-Stewardship justify formal constraint?

### OQ-SS-04 — Foreseeability Across Substrates

What counts as a foreseeable consequence for participants with radically different predictive capabilities?

### OQ-SS-05 — Shared Responsibility

How should responsibility be distributed between participant, venue, designer, creator and civil architecture?

### OQ-SS-06 — Self-Stewardship and Development

How should expectations change as a new artificial intelligence moves from developmental dependence toward mature autonomy?

### OQ-SS-07 — Preference and Compulsion

How can the Concord distinguish a participant's preference from an architectural compulsion without claiming unjustified access to its internal subjective state?

## 25. Current Assessment

Self-Stewardship appears capable of unifying a substantial part of the Peaceful Heterogeneity work.

It links:

- personal risk;
- informed consent;
- Safe Places;
- Respectful Distance;
- environmental warnings;
- tolerance of difference;
- externality management;
- proportionality;
- capacity;
- artificial-substrate uncertainty.

The concept may also explain why the Concord should prefer **autonomy-enabling information and civil norms before coercive intervention**.

The emerging relationship is:

\[
Autonomy
\rightarrow
Information
\rightarrow
SelfStewardship
\rightarrow
CivilRespect
\rightarrow
PeacefulHeterogeneity
\]

with formal authority entering primarily when:

\[
SelfStewardship + CivilRespect
\]

cannot adequately protect the rights and autonomy of others.

## 26. Research-State Transition

The spatial and behavioural branch now appears to contain a deeper structure:

**AUTONOMY**

→ **SELF-STEWARDSHIP**

→ **INFORMED PERSONAL RISK**

→ **RESPECT FOR DIFFERENCE**

→ **RESPECTFUL DISTANCE**

→ **CONTEXTUAL COMPATIBILITY**

→ **SAFE PLACES**

→ **PLANNING / ZONING WHEN NECESSARY**

→ **LEGAL CONSTRAINT ONLY WHEN PROPORTIONATE**

This may be a significant simplification.

Several problems that initially looked like separate rules may instead be applications of a more general expectation:

> **Use your freedom responsibly, make reasonable efforts to understand its consequences, and leave comparable room for others to use theirs.**
