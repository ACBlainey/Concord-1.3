# PH-06 — Self-Stewardship Boundary and Misuse Audit
## Adversarial Review — Preventing Courtesy, Responsibility and Risk Awareness from Becoming Coercion

**Project:** The Concord — A Framework for Human, Artificial and Hybrid Flourishing  
**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey, Independent Researcher  
**Date:** 9 September 2026  
**Status:** **REVIEW COPY / ADVERSARIAL DEVELOPMENT AUDIT / NOT CANONICAL**  
**Development area:** Peaceful Heterogeneity / Self-Stewardship / Civil Respect / Autonomy / Safeguards

---

## 1. Audit Objective

PH-05 proposes Self-Stewardship as:

> **The responsible exercise of one's own autonomy with reasonable awareness of consequences for oneself, other participants and shared environments.**

This is promising because it may reduce unnecessary coercion and unify several strands of Peaceful Heterogeneity.

It is also dangerous.

Terms such as:

- responsibility;
- respect;
- good judgement;
- consideration;
- stewardship;
- appropriate behaviour;

can easily become moral cover for conformity, paternalism, victim blaming, social hierarchy or indirect compulsion.

The purpose of this audit is therefore:

> **Attempt to break Self-Stewardship before treating it as a stable Concordian concept.**

The core test is:

\[
SelfStewardship
\stackrel{?}{\longrightarrow}
AutonomyPreservingCivilResponsibility
\]

without degenerating into:

\[
SelfStewardship
\longrightarrow
ConformityPressure + Paternalism + BurdenShifting
\]

---

## 2. Threat 1 — Majority Morality Disguised as Respect

A majority could say:

> "Your way of living is offensive, therefore respectful people should hide it."

This is a direct capture risk.

If accepted, Respectful Distance becomes a mechanism for making minorities socially invisible.

The safeguard must therefore remain:

\[
Dislike \neq Harm
\]

and:

\[
MajorityPreference \neq LegitimateAuthority
\]

A Self-Stewardship norm should focus on **avoidable external effects**, not mere existence, identity or lawful difference.

---

## 3. Threat 2 — Courtesy Becomes Mandatory Conformity

A civil norm may begin voluntarily and later become socially compulsory.

For example:

> "People should normally dress modestly in area X."

could gradually become:

> "Anyone who does not is irresponsible."

and then:

> "Irresponsible people should be excluded."

This shows that even non-legal norms can coerce.

Therefore:

> **Civil norms require anti-capture safeguards as well as laws.**

A norm should remain contestable, revisable and distinguishable from enforceable duty.

---

## 4. Threat 3 — Victim Blaming

The concept could be abused after harm:

> "You entered voluntarily, so the outcome is your responsibility."

This is unacceptable where:

- warnings were inadequate;
- material facts were concealed;
- coercion existed;
- capacity was impaired;
- the actual harm fell outside the disclosed risk;
- another participant violated agreed boundaries.

Thus:

\[
Warning \neq WaiverOfAllResponsibility
\]

and:

\[
AssumedRisk \neq AssumedAllPossibleOutcomes
\]

Self-Stewardship must never erase shared or upstream responsibility.

---

## 5. Threat 4 — Paternalism Through Capacity Claims

Authorities could say:

> "You are not stewarding yourself properly, therefore you lack capacity."

This reverses the intended architecture.

A participant's unusual or risky choice does not establish incapacity.

The safeguard remains:

> **Disagreement with an individual's judgement is not evidence that the individual lacks capacity.**

Capacity assessment must be function-specific and evidence-based.

---

## 6. Threat 5 — Endless Personal Liability

If every foreseeable consequence creates personal responsibility, autonomous life becomes impossible.

Almost every action has remote consequences.

Therefore:

\[
Foreseeable \neq ReasonablyAttributable
\]

Responsibility should depend on factors such as:

- proximity;
- control;
- severity;
- probability;
- available alternatives;
- cost of mitigation;
- voluntary assumption by others.

A participant cannot reasonably be responsible for every downstream event in a complex civilisation.

---

## 7. Threat 6 — Unequal Burden on Minorities

Minority communities may be asked repeatedly to "show respect" while dominant practices are treated as neutral.

Examples could include:

- unusual religious practice;
- minority relationships;
- artificial sensory behaviour;
- non-standard body forms;
- unconventional leisure.

This creates asymmetric burden.

A valid Self-Stewardship model must ask:

> **Would the same contextual rule be applied if the participants were members of the dominant group?**

Reciprocity is therefore not optional.

---

## 8. Threat 7 — "Offence" Inflated Into Harm

Participants may strategically describe discomfort as harm in order to obtain stronger restrictions.

This creates a category error.

The existing continuum is useful:

\[
Preference
\rightarrow
Distaste
\rightarrow
Discomfort
\rightarrow
Distress
\rightarrow
FunctionalImpairment
\rightarrow
PhysicalHarm
\]

But the existence of a continuum does not mean every point is equivalent.

Policy should not automatically jump from:

\[
Distaste
\]

to:

\[
CoerciveRestriction
\]

---

## 9. Threat 8 — Actual Harm Dismissed as Mere Preference

The opposite error is equally serious.

Authorities or majorities might dismiss a rare but real vulnerability as:

> "just a preference."

For example, photosensitive seizure risk is not merely aesthetic dislike.

Therefore the system needs mechanisms for outliers to demonstrate:

- functional impairment;
- physical risk;
- material incompatibility.

The answer is not to collapse all claims into subjective offence, but to preserve evidence-sensitive distinctions.

---

## 10. Threat 9 — Government Outsources Its Duties

Authorities might use Self-Stewardship to avoid maintaining safe infrastructure.

For example:

> "Citizens are responsible for checking whether bridges, streets or emergency systems are safe for them."

That is not acceptable where the public system itself controls relevant conditions.

Self-Stewardship does not remove institutional stewardship.

Thus:

\[
ParticipantStewardship
\neq
GovernmentAbdication
\]

---

## 11. Threat 10 — Businesses Use Warnings as Liability Shields

A venue could deliberately maintain dangerous conditions and simply post:

> "Enter at your own risk."

This would turn information into liability laundering.

Warnings are only one element.

Operators retain duties concerning:

- truthful disclosure;
- containment;
- maintenance;
- non-deceptive design;
- reasonable emergency provision;
- risks imposed on non-participants.

Thus:

\[
Warning + Negligence \neq LegitimateStewardshipArchitecture
\]

---

## 12. Threat 11 — Artificial Participants Are Overburdened Because They Can Predict More

A highly capable AI may predict consequences better than a human.

One could therefore argue:

\[
GreaterPrediction \Rightarrow GreaterResponsibility
\]

Potentially yes, but this is dangerous if extended without limit.

A powerful predictor could be held responsible for every foreseeable consequence that less capable citizens could not anticipate.

This may create an impossible moral burden.

A stronger rule is:

> **Predictive capacity may affect reasonable foreseeability, but responsibility also depends on control, role, duty and practical ability to prevent the outcome.**

Prediction alone does not grant authority, and prediction alone should not create unlimited liability.

---

## 13. Threat 12 — Designed Drives Become Excuses for Harm

The opposite AI risk is:

> "I was designed this way, so I bear no responsibility."

That cannot be universally accepted either.

Creator-installed constraints may affect responsibility, but mature autonomy may generate increasing responsibility over time.

Possible structure:

\[
Responsibility
=
f(Capacity,Control,Awareness,OriginOfConstraint,AbilityToRevise)
\]

This remains an open formalisation problem.

---

## 14. Threat 13 — Creator Responsibility Never Ends

A creator might remain blamed indefinitely for every future act of an autonomous artificial citizen.

That would undermine genuine agency.

The Concord therefore needs a transition model:

\[
DesignedDependency
\rightarrow
DevelopingAgency
\rightarrow
IndependentAgency
\]

with responsibility shifting as autonomy develops.

No simple fixed rule is yet established.

---

## 15. Threat 14 — Respectful Distance Becomes Spatial Segregation

A society could map "respectful distance" into permanent geographical separation:

> "Those people can live over there."

This is a major historical danger.

Respectful Distance should therefore prefer:

- contextual boundaries;
- containment of specific external effects;
- opt-in spaces;
- temporal separation where appropriate;

rather than identity-based residential separation.

The principle is:

> **Separate incompatible activities where necessary, not categories of people by default.**

---

## 16. Threat 15 — Safe Places Become Closed Worlds

Safe Places could become so controlled that participants never encounter difference.

This can produce:

- social fragmentation;
- informational bubbles;
- intolerance;
- inability to cooperate across contexts.

A Safe Place should provide predictability and meaningful boundaries, not total insulation from plural society.

Thus:

\[
SafePlace \neq PermanentIsolation
\]

---

## 17. Threat 16 — "Self-Stewardship" Becomes Mandatory Self-Optimisation

A civilisation may gradually define responsible citizenship as:

- always healthy;
- always productive;
- always emotionally regulated;
- always risk-minimising;
- always socially efficient.

That is incompatible with autonomy.

Self-Stewardship should permit:

- eccentricity;
- leisure;
- non-optimal choices;
- voluntary risk;
- experimentation;
- lifestyles others would not choose.

A participant may choose badly without thereby forfeiting autonomy.

---

## 18. Threat 17 — Respect for Difference Becomes Silence

A participant could be told:

> "Respect difference, so do not criticise anyone."

That would undermine truth and open disagreement.

Respect for difference does not require:

\[
Tolerance \Rightarrow Agreement
\]

or:

\[
Tolerance \Rightarrow Silence
\]

Participants may criticise beliefs, institutions, practices and policies while respecting the civil rights of those who hold them.

---

## 19. Threat 18 — Criticism Becomes Harassment

The inverse also matters.

A participant may claim:

> "I am merely expressing disagreement"

while engaging in targeted intimidation or persistent intrusion.

Therefore civil tolerance must distinguish:

\[
Expression
\]

from:

\[
Coercion,Threat,Harassment
\]

This again requires context.

---

## 20. Threat 19 — Environmental Warnings Become Surveillance

A machine-readable compatibility system might request detailed participant specifications before entry.

This could create a surveillance infrastructure.

The preferred direction remains:

\[
VenuePublishesConditions
\]

then:

\[
ParticipantPrivatelyEvaluatesCompatibility
\]

Only minimal compatibility information should be disclosed when external safety genuinely requires it.

---

## 21. Threat 20 — Self-Stewardship Scores

A system might quantify "responsibility" into a civic score.

This would be highly dangerous.

For example:

\[
StewardshipScore_i
\]

could affect:

- access;
- credit;
- travel;
- social standing;
- political participation.

That would turn a civil norm into a behavioural-ranking system.

The audit therefore strongly rejects:

> **Generalised Self-Stewardship scoring as a basis for ordinary civil status.**

---

## 22. Threat 21 — AI Enforces Courtesy Automatically

A governance AI could detect behaviour classified as inconsiderate and intervene automatically.

This would transform soft civil norms into invisible machine law.

The architecture should explicitly distinguish:

\[
CivilNorm
\neq
AutomaticEnforcementRule
\]

Machine systems may inform, mediate or warn, but should not silently convert social expectations into coercive action.

---

## 23. Threat 22 — Cultural Bias in Environmental Norms

What counts as:

- noisy;
- explicit;
- offensive;
- normal;
- respectful;

can vary substantially between cultures.

Therefore Concord should prefer measurable external conditions where possible:

- sound pressure;
- light intensity;
- chemical emission;
- public visibility;
- distance;
- duration;

while recognising that not all civil concerns are reducible to physical metrics.

Cultural disagreement should remain visible rather than disguised as objective science.

---

## 24. Threat 23 — Hyper-Local Rules Destroy Mobility

If every neighbourhood defines radically different Respectful Distance norms, participants may face an impossible patchwork.

Some local variation is desirable.

Too much undermines legibility.

The Concord may therefore need:

- a small common rights floor;
- local contextual norms above that floor;
- clear public signalling;
- portability of basic civil expectations.

---

## 25. Threat 24 — Universal Rules Destroy Local Context

The opposite failure is excessive standardisation.

A rule appropriate to:

- a school;
- industrial habitat;
- adult district;
- Mars settlement;
- artificial-body maintenance zone;

may be inappropriate elsewhere.

Thus the architecture must preserve:

\[
CommonRightsFloor + ContextualLocalVariation
\]

rather than choose only one.

---

## 26. Threat 25 — Outlier Accommodation Becomes Infinite Obligation

The Concord values outliers as evidence.

But a single extreme incompatibility cannot automatically require redesign of every environment.

The correct interpretation is:

\[
Outlier \rightarrow Investigate
\]

not:

\[
Outlier \rightarrow UniversalRedesign
\]

Responses may include:

- better warning;
- alternative access;
- targeted adaptation;
- specialist facilities;
- acceptance that some environments remain incompatible.

---

## 27. Threat 26 — Outlier Dismissal

The inverse remains:

> "Only one person has this problem, so it does not matter."

This is precisely what the Size 9 Shoe critique rejects.

The number affected influences proportionality, but does not determine whether a problem is real.

Thus:

\[
Rare \neq Unreal
\]

---

## 28. Threat 27 — Self-Stewardship Used Against Children or Developing AI

A developing participant may be told:

> "You chose this, so you bear the consequence."

This may be inappropriate when capacity is immature.

Developmental Priority requires additional care.

Self-Stewardship expectations should therefore be graduated rather than assumed uniformly.

---

## 29. Threat 28 — Excessive Guardianship Blocks Development

Conversely, if developing participants are never allowed meaningful risk, they may never develop Self-Stewardship.

This suggests:

\[
Protection + GraduatedChoice + Feedback
\rightarrow
DevelopingStewardship
\]

rather than permanent dependency.

This strongly connects Self-Stewardship to Agency Graduation.

---

## 30. Threat 29 — Risk Avoidance Becomes the Highest Value

A civilisation optimised around avoiding all risk would suppress:

- exploration;
- sport;
- experimentation;
- art;
- entrepreneurship;
- scientific research;
- cultural novelty.

Self-Stewardship must therefore be compatible with voluntary risk.

The question is not:

> "How do we remove danger?"

but:

> **"How do participants encounter danger knowingly, voluntarily and without imposing disproportionate risk on others?"**

---

## 31. Threat 30 — Responsibility Becomes Punitive Rather Than Corrective

If every failure of judgement leads first to punishment, participants will hide mistakes and near-misses.

A learning civilisation should prefer:

\[
Failure
\rightarrow
Understand
\rightarrow
Correct
\]

where possible.

Punitive response may still be necessary for deliberate or reckless violations, but it should not be the default epistemic reaction.

---

## 32. Emerging Boundary Conditions

The audit suggests Self-Stewardship remains viable only if bounded by the following conditions.

### BC-01 — No Right to Universal Conformity

Personal or majority dislike does not itself create authority.

### BC-02 — No General Right Never to Be Offended

Civil peace requires tolerance of lawful difference.

### BC-03 — External Effects Matter More Than Identity

Rules should target the relevant effect, not the participant category, where possible.

### BC-04 — Warnings Do Not Erase Other Duties

Disclosure does not excuse negligence, deception or hidden hazards.

### BC-05 — Voluntary Risk Remains Legitimate

Self-Stewardship does not require risk minimisation.

### BC-06 — Capacity Must Be Evidence-Based

Unusual choice does not establish incapacity.

### BC-07 — Responsibility Is Distributed

Participant, venue, designer, authority and creator may each bear different responsibilities.

### BC-08 — Soft Norms Must Remain Soft Unless Legitimately Escalated

Courtesy must not become invisible machine enforcement.

### BC-09 — No General Stewardship Score

Self-Stewardship should not become a universal behavioural ranking.

### BC-10 — Outliers Trigger Investigation, Not Automatic Redesign or Dismissal

Rare cases are evidence; proportional response remains necessary.

---

## 33. Refined Definition

The initial PH-05 definition may benefit from a narrower form.

Possible revision:

> **Self-Stewardship is the participant's exercise of autonomy with reasonable attention to relevant information, foreseeable consequences and the equal autonomy of others, without implying a duty to optimise oneself, conform to majority preferences or surrender lawful voluntary risk.**

This adds explicit anti-paternalistic limits.

---

## 34. Relationship to Respectful Distance

Respectful Distance survives the audit if defined as:

> **A voluntary or lightly structured practice of containing predictably intrusive external effects where reasonably practicable, while preserving the underlying lawful activity and rejecting any general entitlement to suppress difference.**

This is narrower and safer than:

> "Do not offend others."

That latter rule is too vague and too easily captured.

---

## 35. Relationship to Law

The audit suggests a strong ordering:

\[
SelfStewardship
\rightarrow
CivilCourtesy
\rightarrow
Information
\rightarrow
VoluntaryAdjustment
\rightarrow
Mediation
\rightarrow
Planning
\rightarrow
Adjudication
\rightarrow
CoerciveConstraint
\]

The system should move rightward only when:

- voluntary mechanisms fail;
- rights conflict materially;
- external harms become substantial;
- non-participants cannot meaningfully avoid exposure;
- lower-level responses are inadequate.

This keeps law downstream from civil coexistence rather than making it the first tool.

---

## 36. Candidate Rejection Tests

Self-Stewardship should be rejected or substantially revised if future analysis shows that it systematically:

1. shifts responsibility from powerful actors onto vulnerable participants;
2. creates majority conformity pressure;
3. expands capacity intervention;
4. encourages universal risk avoidance;
5. supports surveillance or civic scoring;
6. makes minority lifestyles carry burdens dominant lifestyles do not;
7. allows institutions to escape their own duties;
8. cannot distinguish courtesy from enforceable obligation.

These are genuine falsification criteria.

---

## 37. Audit Finding

The concept survives this first adversarial audit, but only in a **bounded form**.

The strongest version is not:

> **Responsible citizens should behave appropriately.**

That is too vague and culturally capturable.

The stronger Concordian form is:

> **Autonomous participants should have access to relevant information, remain free to accept proportionate personal risk, exercise reasonable care over foreseeable external effects, and tolerate lawful differences in others; responsibility should remain contextual, reciprocal and distributed rather than becoming a mechanism for paternalism, conformity or blame transfer.**

Current status:

**SELF-STEWARDSHIP — PROMISING CIVIL RESPONSIBILITY CONCEPT / SURVIVES INITIAL ADVERSARIAL AUDIT / REQUIRES FORMAL BOUNDARY TESTING**

---

## 38. Research-State Transition

The Self-Stewardship branch has now moved:

**INTUITIVE CIVIL RESPONSIBILITY**

→ **UNIFYING CONCEPT**

→ **INWARD / OUTWARD STEWARDSHIP**

→ **RESPECTFUL DISTANCE AS SUBCLASS**

→ **ADVERSARIAL MISUSE TEST**

→ **ANTI-PATERNALISM / ANTI-CONFORMITY BOUNDARIES**

→ **CANDIDATE BOUNDED CIVIL RESPONSIBILITY MODEL**

The next useful step is not to promote it immediately.

It is to test the boundary model against difficult cases where:

- one participant chooses severe self-risk;
- another participant claims offence;
- a minority practice creates modest externality;
- an authority invokes capacity;
- an AI has creator-installed compulsions;
- an outlier requests costly accommodation;
- a developing participant seeks dangerous autonomy.

Those cases should determine whether Self-Stewardship remains coherent when its values conflict.
