# PH-04 — Respectful Distance, Sensory Boundaries and Unknown Substrate Preferences
## Development Note — Civil Courtesy, Environmental Warning and Voluntary Exposure

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey, Independent Researcher  
**Date:** 9 September 2026  
**Status:** **REVIEW COPY / ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / NOT CANONICAL**

---

## 1. Starting Observation

PH-03 established that a lawful activity need not be appropriate in every location, and that civil planning can create boundaries allowing heterogeneous activities to coexist.

A further distinction is necessary.

Not every undesirable proximity should become a legal prohibition.

There is a large domain between:

\[
UnrestrictedBehaviour
\]

and:

\[
LegalProhibition
\]

that can be governed through **civil respect**.

A useful term for this domain is:

# **Respectful Distance**

The basic proposition is:

> **Participants engaging in lawful activities that they can reasonably anticipate may be strongly unwelcome, disturbing or offensive to others should exercise reasonable care over where and how those activities are exposed to non-participants.**

The purpose is not to establish a right never to be offended.

It is to reduce unnecessary involuntary exposure while preserving the freedom to conduct the underlying activity.

---

## 2. Respectful Distance Is Not a Right Against Disagreement

The concept must immediately be bounded.

A participant cannot reasonably demand that every disliked belief, identity, appearance, culture or lawful practice be removed from sight.

Therefore:

\[
Offence \neq Harm
\]

and:

\[
Dislike \neq AuthorityToExclude
\]

Respectful Distance is instead concerned with **avoidable exposure created by the manner or placement of an activity**.

A red-light district is a useful example.

Consenting adults may have substantial freedom to participate in lawful adult activity.

At the same time, participants and businesses may reasonably have a civil duty of care not to impose explicit displays or activity unnecessarily upon people outside that context.

This is better understood initially as **courtesy and coexistence**, rather than criminal law.

---

## 3. Civil Respect Before Legal Compulsion

The Concord should distinguish:

\[
CivilNorm
\]

from:

\[
LegalConstraint
\]

A civil norm can say:

> "This activity is legitimate, but considerate participants normally conduct it within an appropriate context."

That is materially different from:

> "The state prohibits this activity outside a prescribed zone."

The first encourages reciprocal accommodation.

The second invokes coercive authority.

Where civil respect is sufficient, law may be unnecessary.

This fits the Concord's proportionality architecture:

\[
Courtesy
<
Information
<
VoluntaryBoundary
<
PlanningConstraint
<
LegalRestriction
\]

The system should not jump unnecessarily to the right.

---

## 4. Reciprocal Duty

Respectful Distance should not become a one-sided obligation imposed only on minorities or unconventional communities.

Both sides have responsibilities.

Participants conducting an unusual or potentially disturbing activity should avoid unnecessary involuntary exposure.

Other participants should tolerate the existence of lawful difference where it is being conducted with reasonable contextual care.

Thus:

\[
FreedomToDiffer + DutyOfContextualCare
\]

is paired with:

\[
ToleranceOfDifference + NoRightToUniversalConformity
\]

This reciprocity is essential.

Otherwise "respect" can become a socially acceptable name for majority suppression.

---

## 5. No Universal Distance

The term **distance** should not be interpreted only in metres.

Respectful Distance may be created through:

- physical separation;
- walls;
- visual screening;
- acoustic isolation;
- restricted entry;
- signage;
- timing;
- separate digital spaces;
- opt-in access;
- environmental transition zones.

Therefore:

\[
RespectfulDistance = ContextualBoundary
\]

rather than necessarily:

\[
RespectfulDistance = X\ metres
\]

This avoids reproducing the arbitrary-distance problem identified in PH-03.

---

## 6. Preference Is Not Necessarily Vulnerability

The artificial-intelligence problem makes this much harder.

For humans, we know that people can experience:

- offence;
- disgust;
- embarrassment;
- distress;
- sensory discomfort;
- fear;
- trauma responses;
- aesthetic preference.

For future artificial intelligences, we do not know which analogous states, if any, will exist.

An AI might report:

> "I prefer not to encounter environment X."

But several interpretations remain possible.

The statement could represent:

- a genuine aversive experience;
- an operational preference;
- a learned policy;
- an optimisation constraint;
- a social convention;
- a safety response;
- a linguistic representation without corresponding subjective distress;
- something unlike any human category.

The Concord should not pretend this uncertainty has already been resolved.

---

## 7. Articulated Preference Still Matters

Metaphysical uncertainty does not mean expressed preference should be ignored.

If a recognised participant repeatedly and coherently expresses:

> "I do not want exposure to X"

that is relevant civil evidence.

Likewise demonstrated avoidance behaviour may be relevant.

A preliminary evidential hierarchy might include:

\[
DeclaredPreference
\]

\[
RepeatedPreference
\]

\[
ConsistentAvoidance
\]

\[
PerformanceOrWellbeingEffect
\]

\[
DemonstratedDamageOrImpairment
\]

These are not necessarily measurements of the same phenomenon.

But they provide evidence for planning without requiring civilisation first to solve whether an AI experiences "offence" in the human sense.

---

## 8. Preference Does Not Automatically Create Compulsion

The opposite error must also be avoided.

If participant A dislikes activity X:

\[
Preference_A(Avoid\ X)
\]

this does not automatically imply:

\[
Compel_B(Stop\ X)
\]

The response still depends on:

- severity;
- voluntariness of exposure;
- available alternatives;
- location;
- purpose;
- cost of accommodation;
- external effects;
- reciprocal rights;
- proportionality.

This is exactly why Respectful Distance may be useful.

It provides a middle layer between ignoring preference and legally suppressing the activity.

# PART II — SUBSTRATE-SPECIFIC LEISURE

## 9. Leisure May Be Radically Different Across Substrates

The swimming-pool example showed that a human leisure environment may be physically incompatible with some artificial bodies.

The inverse may also occur.

Future artificial citizens may develop leisure activities specifically suited to their sensory systems, processing architecture or bodies.

We presently do not know what these might be.

They could involve:

- extremely rapid visual modulation;
- unusual electromagnetic environments;
- acoustic frequencies or amplitudes;
- intense machine-readable signalling;
- rapid motion;
- high temperatures;
- unusual atmospheric conditions;
- network-state manipulation;
- sensory saturation;
- controlled overload or altered processing states.

These examples are speculative.

The important point is structural:

> **An environment designed as pleasurable, stimulating or challenging for one substrate may be unpleasant or dangerous to another.**

---

## 10. The Robot Nightclub Thought Experiment

Consider a hypothetical artificial-participant nightclub.

Its environment might deliberately use:

- extreme strobing;
- rapidly changing illumination;
- very high sound pressure;
- frequencies optimised for artificial sensors;
- patterns intended to produce unusual optical-processing states.

For compatible artificial participants this might be:

- entertainment;
- sensory novelty;
- competition;
- challenge;
- social bonding;
- an altered processing experience.

For humans the same environment could create risks such as:

- photosensitive seizures;
- hearing damage;
- disorientation;
- falls;
- sensory overload.

The correct sign would not be:

> **NO HUMANS**

A more substrate-neutral warning would describe the environment:

> **CAUTION — EXTREME SOUND LEVELS AND RAPID STROBING LIGHT. MAY CAUSE HEARING DAMAGE, PHOTOSENSITIVE SEIZURES, DISORIENTATION OR OTHER SENSORY EFFECTS.**

The human then has meaningful information.

---

## 11. Warning Does Not Mean Prohibition

Some humans might deliberately choose to enter precisely because the environment is extreme.

This matters.

A warning should not silently become paternalistic exclusion.

The same environment may be:

\[
DangerousForSome
\]

while:

\[
DesirableChallengeForOthers
\]

Humans already voluntarily engage in activities involving:

- extreme heat;
- cold;
- altitude;
- speed;
- loud sound;
- flashing lights;
- combat;
- endurance;
- underwater environments.

Therefore:

> **Hazard information supports autonomy; it does not automatically negate autonomy.**

Where the participant has relevant capacity and does not impose unacceptable risk on others, informed entry may remain legitimate.

---

## 12. Symmetry Across Substrates

This produces a useful symmetry.

### Human-designed pool

Potential artificial warning:

> chlorinated water, high humidity, condensation, wet surfaces.

### Artificial-designed sensory venue

Potential human warning:

> extreme sound, rapid strobing, unusual spectral output, possible seizure/hearing risk.

Neither requires:

> **NO ROBOTS**

or:

> **NO HUMANS**

by default.

Instead:

\[
Environment \rightarrow HazardDescription
\]

followed by:

\[
Participant \rightarrow InformedCompatibilityDecision
\]

This is much closer to substrate neutrality.

---

## 13. When Warning Is Not Enough

Warnings cannot solve every problem.

If an environment creates effects beyond its boundary — for example extreme sound affecting neighbouring homes — non-participants cannot meaningfully consent merely by seeing a warning.

Similarly, hazardous emissions, intense light, electromagnetic interference or other effects may escape the venue.

Then Respectful Distance becomes physical planning.

The operator has a duty to contain the externality.

Thus:

\[
ContainedHazard + InformedEntry
\]

is different from:

\[
UncontainedHazard \rightarrow InvoluntaryExposure
\]

The second creates a stronger basis for planning or legal intervention.

---

## 14. Internal Versus External Environment

A venue may therefore be evaluated through two boundaries.

### Internal

What happens to participants who voluntarily enter?

### External

What escapes to people who did not enter?

A high-risk environment can remain compatible with peaceful heterogeneity if:

1. entry is sufficiently informed and voluntary;
2. risks to non-participants are appropriately contained;
3. emergency consequences are manageable;
4. neighbouring environments remain usable.

This generalises well beyond leisure.

---

## 15. Respectful Distance as Externality Management

The concept can now be stated more precisely.

Respectful Distance is not primarily about suppressing offence.

It is about recognising that:

> **The freedom to conduct an activity does not necessarily include an unlimited entitlement to project its sensory, behavioural or environmental effects into spaces occupied by non-participants.**

This applies to:

- explicit visual material;
- extreme sound;
- intense lighting;
- smoke or odour;
- hazardous emissions;
- machine signalling;
- crowds;
- disruptive physical activity.

Some cases are matters of courtesy.

Others become material external harms.

The response should scale accordingly.

---

## 16. From Distaste to Harm

A useful continuum is:

\[
Preference
\rightarrow
Distaste
\rightarrow
Discomfort
\rightarrow
Distress
\rightarrow
FunctionalImpairment
\rightarrow
PhysicalHarm
\]

These categories should not automatically be treated identically.

At the lower end, civil respect and voluntary separation may be sufficient.

At the upper end, warnings, engineering controls, zoning or legal restrictions may become proportionate.

The difficulty is that the thresholds may vary enormously between participants.

This again makes outliers important.

---

## 17. Artificial Preference Remains an Open Empirical Question

The Concord should explicitly preserve the following uncertainty:

> **We do not presently know whether future artificial intelligences will possess subjective offence, aversion, desire or leisure needs comparable to humans, or whether superficially similar reported preferences will correspond to fundamentally different internal phenomena.**

This should remain an empirical and philosophical open question.

However, civil planning need not wait for a complete answer.

It can respond to observable evidence:

\[
Preference
\]

\[
Avoidance
\]

\[
Impairment
\]

\[
Damage
\]

\[
OperationalFailure
\]

while remaining agnostic about unsupported metaphysical claims.

---

## 18. Outliers Again Become Valuable

Imagine 10,000 artificial participants use a human entertainment district without difficulty, but one model consistently suffers severe sensor degradation from a particular lighting technology.

That participant is not merely an inconvenient exception.

It may reveal a hidden environmental variable.

Likewise, if almost all humans tolerate an artificial leisure venue but a small photosensitive population experiences serious neurological risk, the minority is planning evidence.

Thus:

> **Rare incompatibility is not evidence that the environment must necessarily close; it is evidence that the environment's description, warnings, containment or alternatives may be incomplete.**

This is the Size 9 Shoe Problem expressed spatially.

---

## 19. Respectful Distance and Safe Places

Safe places can now be understood as part of a larger spatial architecture.

Different spaces may intentionally support different expectations:

### General Shared Space

High expectation of broad compatibility and restrained externalities.

### Context-Specific Space

Activities or sensory conditions that are legitimate but not universally desired.

### Bounded-Risk Space

Participants knowingly enter environments with significant declared hazards.

### Protected / Developmental Space

Additional constraints because participants may have reduced ability to consent or avoid exposure.

### Specialist Substrate Environment

Conditions optimised for particular operational requirements.

These are functional categories, not social castes.

Movement between them should remain as open as compatibility permits.

---

## 20. Candidate Civil Norm — Respectful Distance

A candidate formulation is:

> **Participants should, where reasonably practicable, conduct lawful but predictably intrusive, explicit or strongly aversive activities with sufficient contextual separation that non-participants are not unnecessarily subjected to them.**

Important safeguards:

- "predictably" requires more than one person's arbitrary complaint;
- "reasonably practicable" preserves proportionality;
- "unnecessarily" recognises that some exposure is unavoidable in plural society;
- the norm does not create a general right not to be offended;
- the norm applies reciprocally rather than only to unpopular minorities.

This should remain a **candidate civil norm**, not yet a legal principle.

---

## 21. Candidate Environmental Rule — Describe Conditions, Not Identities

PH-03's candidate becomes even stronger:

> **Where practical, warnings and access information should describe the relevant environmental condition or hazard rather than naming broad classes of participants presumed to be incompatible.**

For example:

Weak:

> **NO HUMANS**

Stronger:

> **EXTREME ACOUSTIC AND STROBING ENVIRONMENT — HUMAN HEARING AND PHOTOSENSITIVITY RISKS.**

Weak:

> **NO ROBOTS**

Stronger:

> **CHLORINATED HIGH-HUMIDITY AQUATIC ENVIRONMENT — WATER, CORROSION AND CONDENSATION EXPOSURE.**

The latter statements remain useful even when an unexpected substrate appears.

---

## 22. Proportionality Architecture

The combined PH-03/PH-04 model now suggests:

\[
CivilCourtesy
<
EnvironmentalInformation
<
Warning
<
VoluntarySeparation
<
EngineeringContainment
<
Zoning
<
RestrictedAccess
<
Prohibition
\]

The intervention should move rightward only as:

- involuntary exposure increases;
- severity increases;
- external harm increases;
- participant capacity becomes less reliable;
- lower-intervention solutions fail.

This provides a potentially general civil-planning proportionality architecture.

## 24. Current Assessment

The term **Respectful Distance** appears useful because it identifies a layer of peaceful coexistence that sits below legal compulsion.

It captures a simple reciprocal idea:

> **You may live differently from me. I should tolerate that difference. Where your activity is predictably intrusive and can reasonably be contained, you should avoid unnecessarily imposing it upon me.**

The artificial-substrate examples strengthen rather than weaken the concept.

They show that the issue is not fundamentally morality or offence.

At the deepest level it is about **managing involuntary exposure between participants whose preferences, vulnerabilities and sensory systems may be radically different**.

That means the same architecture can address:

- red-light districts;
- schools;
- combat sports;
- swimming pools;
- industrial environments;
- human nightlife;
- hypothetical artificial nightlife;
- future environments we cannot presently imagine.

The robust strategy is not to predict every citizen.

It is to describe environments accurately, preserve voluntary choice, contain externalities and learn from the outliers who reveal what the planning system failed to anticipate.
