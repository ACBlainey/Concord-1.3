## 1. Purpose

This Development Note extends the sketch **Civilisation, Non-Conformity
and Peaceful Heterogeneity** by deliberately examining difficult outlier
cases.

The starting question is:

> **What minimum common constraints are actually necessary for
> heterogeneous participants and communities to coexist peacefully,
> while leaving everything outside those constraints free to vary?**

This leads naturally into consensual harm, autonomous self-regarding
harm, changing decision-making capacity, irreversible consequences and
voluntary termination. Questions of identity, continuity, reconstruction,
branching and succession that emerged at the outer boundary have been
extracted into a separate Development Note.

These cases are architectural stress tests rather than predictions. As
with the Layer-Zero homelessness investigation, outliers can expose
assumptions hidden by ordinary cases.

## 2. Peaceful Heterogeneity

A heterogeneous civilisation may contain participants with radically
different values, cultures, embodiments, cognitive architectures, risk
tolerances, preferences, lifestyles and conceptions of flourishing.

A provisional design space is:

**CONSENT + AUTONOMY + SAFE SEPARATION + PROPORTIONALITY + PROTECTION
FROM INVOLUNTARY HARM**

The purpose is not unlimited permissiveness. It is to determine what
actually requires common constraint and what can safely remain plural.
This connects to the **Size 9 Shoe Problem**: pluralism may be an
architectural response to the failure of average-based governance.

## 3. Consensual Harm

Humans routinely choose activities involving foreseeable harm or
substantial risk. Therefore:

> **Harm cannot automatically establish lack of autonomy.**

But the opposite rule also fails:

> **Consent does not automatically make every harmful act ethically
> uncomplicated.**

Consent may be uninformed, coerced, transient, based on materially false
beliefs, affected by impaired decision-making capacity, or progressively
altered by the harmful process itself.

## 4. Dynamic Capacity and Preference Drift

A participant may initially make an informed autonomous choice while
repeated exposure subsequently changes cognition, preferences, judgement
or risk tolerance:

**INFORMED AUTONOMOUS CHOICE → CONSENSUAL RISK/HARM → REPEATED EXPOSURE
→ POSSIBLE COGNITIVE OR PREFERENCE CHANGE → RISK-TOLERANCE DRIFT →
POSSIBLE CAPACITY UNCERTAINTY → POTENTIALLY IRREVERSIBLE HARM**

Research question:

> **Can a voluntarily entered harmful process progressively alter a
> participant's capacity, preferences or perception of acceptable harm
> such that later consent becomes materially different from the consent
> that initiated the process?**

But:

**UNUSUAL PREFERENCE ≠ HARMFUL PREFERENCE ≠ IMPAIRED CAPACITY**

> **Disagreement with a participant's judgement is not evidence that the
> participant lacks capacity.**

Concern should require evidence relevant to the actual decision, not
cultural difference, unconventional values or refusal of authority.

## 5. Irreversibility and Consent Assurance

Irreversibility matters, but cannot by itself establish authority to
intervene.

A useful provisional relationship is:

**SEVERITY / IRREVERSIBILITY ↑ → CONSENT ASSURANCE ↑**

rather than:

**SEVERITY / IRREVERSIBILITY ↑ → EXTERNAL AUTHORITY ↑**

Increasing assurance might include better information, repeated
confirmation, reflection time where appropriate, independent advice,
reversible alternatives, voluntary support, participant-selected
safeguards and capacity assessment where actual evidence warrants it.

## 6. Progressive Edge Cases

The problem can be examined through increasingly difficult cases:

-   **A:** voluntary moderate self-regarding harm;
-   **B:** serious self-regarding harm;
-   **C:** irreversible harm;
-   **D:** repeated harm and preference drift;
-   **E:** material capacity uncertainty;
-   **F:** imminent severe irreversible harm under capacity uncertainty;
-   **G:** voluntary permanent termination.

At each stage ask whether the participant is choosing, informed,
persistent and free from coercion; whether others face involuntary harm;
how reversible the outcome is; whether relevant capacity is actually
impaired; who possesses legitimate authority; what the least
autonomy-reducing response is; and what upstream architecture produced
the decision environment.

## 7. Architectural Ethics

If civilisation eventually faces only "permit catastrophic harm" or
"forcibly intervene," the final decision may be too late a point to
begin analysis.

> **Why did the architecture allow the participant and system to reach a
> state in which only those choices remained?**

**BOTTOM-UP:** OBSERVED OUTCOME → IMMEDIATE DECISION → SUPPORT FAILURE →
SYSTEM CONDITIONS → GOVERNANCE → ARCHITECTURE

**TOP-DOWN:** ARCHITECTURE → RULES / RESOURCES / SAFEGUARDS → DECISION
ENVIRONMENT → AVAILABLE OPTIONS → PARTICIPANT ACTION → OUTCOME

This parallels the homelessness work:

**OUTLIER → TRACE FAILURE UPSTREAM → REDESIGN ARCHITECTURE → REDUCE
FUTURE NEED FOR COERCIVE LAST-MOMENT DECISIONS**

## 8. The Nth-Degree Case: Voluntary Termination

The limiting case of self-regarding harm is voluntary permanent termination.

This case should be used as an **ethical boundary test**, not as a starting assumption and not as a source of practical self-harm guidance. Its value is that it pushes the relationship between autonomy, consent assurance, capacity, irreversibility and external authority to an extreme.

At this boundary, the investigation should ask:

- Is the participant making a persistent and informed choice?
- Is there material evidence of impaired decision-making capacity relevant to this choice?
- Is the choice affected by coercion, misinformation or an avoidable lack of alternatives?
- Are other participants exposed to involuntary rights violations?
- What support or reversible alternatives remain available?
- What authority, if any, could legitimately override the participant?
- Has the surrounding architecture unnecessarily reduced the participant's viable options before the crisis point?

The investigation also revealed that "permanent termination" cannot be treated as conceptually simple across humans, artificial intelligences and hybrids. Questions of reconstruction, copying, restoration, substrate transfer and continuity rapidly become a separate research domain.

Those questions are therefore extracted from this document into the dedicated Development Note:

**Identity, Continuity, Provenance and Verification Across Substrates — Branching, Duplication, Merger and Succession**

The present investigation retains only the point necessary for Peaceful Heterogeneity:

> **The more irreversible the consequence, the stronger the required assurance of meaningful consent may become; irreversibility alone does not automatically create external authority.**

## 9. The Core Peaceful-Heterogeneity Problem

After extracting the identity problem, the central investigation can remain focused:

> **How far can civilisation respect a participant's radically different conception of their own good before respect for autonomy conflicts with protection from severe harm or the rights of others?**

This question should not be answered by assuming that majority preference defines rationality.

The relevant distinctions include:

**DIFFERENCE ≠ HARM**

**HARM ≠ INCAPACITY**

**RISK ≠ ABSENCE OF CONSENT**

**IRREVERSIBILITY ≠ AUTOMATIC AUTHORITY**

**DISAGREEMENT ≠ EVIDENCE OF INCAPACITY**

**SUPPORT ≠ CONTROL**

The task is to identify the minimum common boundary required for peaceful coexistence while preserving the largest defensible interior space for autonomous variation.

## 10. Provisional Hypotheses for Critical Examination

The following remain **research hypotheses, not Concordian Principles**:

> **H1 — Peaceful heterogeneity should begin by identifying minimum common constraints rather than treating conformity as the default condition.**

> **H2 — Harm cannot automatically establish lack of autonomy.**

> **H3 — Consent does not automatically make every harmful act ethically uncomplicated.**

> **H4 — A voluntarily entered harmful process may alter later preferences or decision-making capacity, requiring dynamic rather than purely static examination.**

> **H5 — Unusual preference, harmful preference and impaired capacity are distinct states.**

> **H6 — Increasing severity and irreversibility may justify increasing consent assurance without automatically increasing external authority.**

> **H7 — Earlier voluntary support and better architecture may reduce the need for coercive last-moment intervention.**

> **H8 — Outlier cases can reveal hidden assumptions in governance architectures designed around average participants.**

## 11. Immediate Research Questions

The next stage should concentrate on questions that can be investigated without first resolving cross-substrate identity:

- What is the minimum common boundary necessary for peaceful heterogeneity?
- What kinds of self-regarding harm remain primarily within participant autonomy?
- When do externalities create legitimate civil concern?
- How should consent assurance scale with severity and irreversibility?
- How can dynamic capacity be evaluated without converting unconventional judgement into presumed incapacity?
- What evidence should be required before moving upward through the Support Gradient?
- What role should participant-selected safeguards, trusted contacts and advance plans play?
- When does intervention protect autonomy, and when does it replace autonomy?
- How should Safe Spaces, subsidiarity, practical exit and GTP support communities with materially different values?
- Which constraints must remain common across communities despite local variation?
- Can a civilisation permit radically different conceptions of flourishing without allowing one community's rules to become involuntary domination of another participant?

## 12. Development Path

Recommended next work:

**1. Define the Minimum Common Boundary**  
Separate harms that remain primarily self-regarding from harms that create involuntary external effects or rights conflicts.

**2. Formalise the Consent-Assurance Gradient**  
Model how information, persistence, severity, reversibility, coercion and capacity uncertainty affect required assurance.

**3. Build Edge-Case Scenarios**  
Progress from ordinary non-conformity through consensual risk, serious harm, dynamic capacity and the termination boundary.

**4. Apply Architectural Ethics**  
For every difficult case, trace both the immediate decision and the upstream architecture that produced the available choices.

**5. Test Against Existing Concord Architecture**  
Autonomy, Non-Harm, Safe Spaces, Privacy/Property, Developmental Priority, Recursive Oversight, Layer Zero/−0, GTP, Governance and Judiciary.

**6. Adversarial Examination**  
Attempt to break the hypotheses with cases involving coercion, manipulation, dependency, cultural conflict, harmful communities, deceptive consent, impaired capacity and irreversible outcomes.

## 13. Closing Observation

The identity investigation was generated by pushing the Peaceful Heterogeneity edge case to its limiting conditions. Extracting it does not weaken this work; it clarifies the boundary between two developing research programmes.

The Peaceful Heterogeneity problem remains:

**NON-CONFORMITY**  
→ **AUTONOMY**  
→ **CONSENSUAL RISK / HARM**  
→ **IRREVERSIBILITY**  
→ **DYNAMIC CAPACITY**  
→ **CONSENT ASSURANCE**  
→ **LEGITIMATE SUPPORT / AUTHORITY BOUNDARY**

Its central design objective is not maximum conformity or maximum safety.

It is to discover the minimum common architecture under which genuinely different participants can remain different while coexisting without involuntary domination or unjustified harm.
