## 1. Starting Problem

Peaceful heterogeneity is not only a question of rights between individuals.

It is also a question of **space**.

Civilisations already separate some activities because an activity that is legitimate in one context may be inappropriate, disruptive or dangerous in another.

Examples include:

- adult entertainment near schools;
- gambling facilities near children or vulnerable populations;
- adult shops and public-facing displays;
- combat sports;
- industrial activity near homes;
- hospitals requiring quiet or controlled environments;
- swimming pools and other specialised physical environments.

The Concord complicates this substantially because future citizens may occupy radically different biological, artificial and hybrid substrates.

The central question becomes:

> **How should a heterogeneous civilisation organise shared physical and digital environments so that legitimate activities can coexist without turning zoning into arbitrary exclusion?**

---

## 2. Zoning as Contextual Coordination

Zoning should not begin from:

> **Is this activity morally good or bad?**

A better starting question is:

> **Is this activity compatible with this particular place, population and neighbouring use?**

Thus:

\[
PermissibleActivity \neq PermissibleEverywhere
\]

Boxing may be legitimate within a consenting sporting environment but inappropriate in an ordinary public corridor.

Adult entertainment may be legitimate for consenting adults while its placement immediately beside a school creates a different contextual problem.

Industrial machinery may be legitimate but incompatible with a hospital ward.

The issue is often not prohibition.

It is **contextual compatibility**.

---

## 3. The Safe-Place Connection

The Concord's concept of safe places should therefore be understood carefully.

A safe place cannot mean:

> **A place in which no participant can encounter anything they dislike.**

That would be impossible in a heterogeneous civilisation and could justify extreme restriction.

A more workable formulation is:

> **A safe place is an environment whose declared purpose, expected conditions and material hazards are sufficiently known that participants can make meaningful decisions about entering, using or avoiding it.**

Safety therefore includes:

- physical safety;
- sensory compatibility;
- psychological context where relevant;
- developmental appropriateness;
- substrate compatibility;
- accessibility;
- predictable behavioural expectations.

---

## 4. Adult Activities and Developmental Context

Schools provide a straightforward example.

Certain activities may be lawful between consenting adults while still being reasonably separated from spaces primarily intended for children.

Potential examples include:

- explicit adult entertainment;
- gambling;
- sexually explicit commercial displays;
- some intoxicant-related activities;
- certain high-risk entertainment.

The justification should not simply be:

> **Public decency requires it.**

"Decency" can become culturally arbitrary and can be used to suppress harmless difference.

A stronger test asks:

1. Who is the expected population?
2. What is the purpose of the neighbouring place?
3. Is involuntary exposure likely?
4. Is there a developmental or consent issue?
5. What actual harm or interference is being reduced?
6. Is spatial separation the least restrictive effective measure?
7. Is the distance or boundary evidence-based or merely inherited convention?

Thus a rule such as:

> "No adult entertainment within X metres of a school"

should not automatically become a Concordian universal simply because such rules exist elsewhere.

The value of the rule depends on whether **X** has a defensible relationship to the identified problem.

---

## 5. The Arbitrary-Distance Problem

Zoning frequently creates apparently precise rules:

\[
Distance \geq X
\]

Precision can create the appearance of rationality even where the number is arbitrary.

A Concordian zoning rule should therefore distinguish:

\[
PreciseRule \neq EvidenceBasedRule
\]

A distance boundary may still be administratively useful, but it should be:

- connected to a stated purpose;
- proportionate;
- revisable;
- empirically examined where possible;
- sensitive to physical layout.

A 200-metre rule across an impassable motorway may operate differently from the same rule along a pedestrian street.

Context matters.

---

## 6. Public Display and Adult Shops

Adult shops provide a useful boundary case.

The activity may be lawful and consensual, while open display visible from a general public walkway may expose people who did not choose to enter the environment.

A possible architecture is therefore not:

\[
AdultShop \rightarrow Prohibited
\]

but:

\[
AdultActivity \rightarrow Permitted
\]

subject to context-sensitive controls over:

- public-facing explicit display;
- age-restricted entry;
- location where necessary;
- signage;
- visibility into the premises.

However, these controls require the same proportionality test.

Aesthetic dislike, cultural discomfort or majority preference alone should not automatically justify restriction.

---

## 7. Combat Sports

Boxing has already demonstrated the importance of bounded consent and contextual harm.

Other combat sports raise similar questions.

Inside a properly governed sporting environment:

\[
InformedConsent + Rules + Safeguards + Exit
\rightarrow
PermissibleBoundedRisk
\]

The same physical acts outside that context may constitute assault.

Civil planning therefore helps create the **environmental boundary** within which the consent envelope operates.

A combat-sports venue may require:

- physical containment;
- spectator separation;
- emergency medical access;
- noise management;
- participant-only competition areas;
- clear transition between ordinary public space and bounded-risk space.

The architecture is not necessarily moral zoning.

It is partly **consent-envelope zoning**.

---

## 8. Substrate Difference Changes the Problem

Human urban planning is largely built around assumptions about human bodies.

A multi-substrate civilisation cannot safely assume:

\[
HumanSafe \Rightarrow UniversallySafe
\]

or:

\[
HumanAccessible \Rightarrow UniversallyAccessible
\]

A swimming pool is an excellent example.

For many humans it is:

- recreation;
- exercise;
- therapy;
- social space;
- developmental activity.

For an artificial participant, however, the same environment could present very different risks.

Possible examples include:

- water ingress;
- corrosion;
- chlorine exposure;
- condensation;
- optical sensor degradation;
- electrical faults;
- reduced traction;
- impaired mobility;
- interference with gas or chemical sensors;
- damage to exposed actuators or connectors.

An artificial participant might be waterproof yet still be vulnerable to humidity, chemical exposure or condensation.

Another artificial participant might be completely unaffected.

Therefore:

> **Substrate category alone is insufficient for environmental safety classification.**

---

## 9. Do Not Replace Human-Centrism With Robot-Centrism

The solution cannot be a universal list saying:

> Robots cannot enter swimming pools.

Artificial bodies could vary enormously.

Some may be:

- fully aquatic;
- sealed against water;
- corrosion resistant;
- designed for industrial chemicals;
- able to operate underwater indefinitely.

Others may be extremely vulnerable to moisture.

Likewise biological citizens may have very different environmental tolerances.

Therefore:

\[
Artificial \neq SingleEnvironmentalProfile
\]

just as:

\[
Human \neq SingleEnvironmentalProfile
\]

The architecture needs **capability and hazard information**, not crude substrate stereotypes.

---

## 10. The Unknown-Variance Problem

This is particularly difficult because Concord cannot presently know the range of future artificial bodies.

Possible variation could involve:

- operating temperature;
- atmospheric composition;
- pressure;
- radiation tolerance;
- electromagnetic sensitivity;
- chemical sensitivity;
- water resistance;
- required network connectivity;
- sensory spectrum;
- mobility geometry;
- mass;
- structural strength;
- power requirements;
- magnetic fields;
- acoustic sensitivity;
- contamination requirements.

Planning a civilisation around an imagined "standard robot" would repeat the Size 9 Shoe Problem.

The average future artificial citizen may not even be a meaningful concept.

---

## 11. Environmental Compatibility Profiles

One possible architecture is to describe **places** and **participants** through compatible hazard/capability descriptors rather than broad identity categories.

A place might declare:

\[
E = \{Water,Humidity,Temperature,Chemicals,EM,Noise,Pressure,Surface,Access\}
\]

A participant may privately maintain relevant operating constraints:

\[
C_i = \{WaterTolerance,HumidityTolerance,ChemicalTolerance,\ldots\}
\]

The participant or participant-controlled system can then determine:

\[
Compatible(C_i,E)
\]

without necessarily revealing the participant's entire technical specification to the venue or government.

This is an important privacy requirement.

---

## 12. Signage as a Low-Coercion Solution

Official environmental signage may solve many compatibility problems without requiring exclusion.

For example, a pool could indicate:

**ENVIRONMENTAL CONDITIONS**
- chlorinated water;
- persistent high humidity;
- wet/slippery surfaces;
- possible condensation;
- specified temperature range.

This tells participants what the environment **is** rather than telling broad classes of participants whether they belong there.

The decision can then remain with the participant where capacity and external harm permit.

This suggests:

> **Describe the hazard before classifying the citizen.**

That may be a strong substrate-neutral planning rule.

---

## 13. Machine-Readable Environmental Signage

For artificial and hybrid participants, signage need not be only visual.

A future venue might publish a machine-readable environmental profile.

For example:

\[
VenueEnvironmentProfile
\]

could be accessible through:

- visual symbols;
- text;
- local wireless broadcast;
- civil mapping systems;
- accessibility interfaces;
- machine-readable tags.

An artificial participant could compare the profile against its own private operational limits before entering.

A human might simply read:

> "High humidity / chlorinated environment / wet surfaces."

Both are representations of the same underlying reality.

---

## 14. Warning Versus Exclusion

A key proportionality ladder emerges:

\[
Information
<
Warning
<
Adaptation
<
Segregation
<
RestrictedAccess
<
Prohibition
\]

The Concord should normally seek the least restrictive intervention that adequately addresses the actual problem.

For example:

### Level 1 — Information

"This environment contains chlorinated water."

### Level 2 — Warning

"Certain optical/electronic systems may experience condensation or corrosion."

### Level 3 — Adaptation

Provide a dry observation route or protective interface.

### Level 4 — Spatial Separation

Separate incompatible activity areas.

### Level 5 — Restricted Access

Entry limited where the participant's presence creates a substantial safety risk to self or others under legitimate criteria.

### Level 6 — Prohibition

Reserved for circumstances where less restrictive measures cannot adequately address the risk.

This directly connects zoning to Proportionality of Authority.

---

## 15. Self-Risk Versus External Risk

The pool example also exposes an important distinction.

If an artificial participant risks damaging itself by entering a pool, the first response may simply be information.

If its failure could electrocute nearby swimmers, release hazardous chemicals or create a major obstruction, the problem changes.

Thus:

\[
SelfRisk \neq ExternalRisk
\]

A participant's informed decision about self-risk deserves substantial autonomy.

Risk imposed on non-consenting others creates a stronger basis for shared constraints.

This is the spatial equivalent of the bounded-consent work.

---

## 16. Accessibility Is Not Universal Sameness

A heterogeneous civilisation cannot realistically make every environment equally usable by every possible substrate.

A swimming pool designed to remain a swimming pool necessarily contains water.

Making it "accessible" to a participant destroyed by water cannot mean removing the water.

Similarly:

- a vacuum research facility may be incompatible with unprotected humans;
- a high-temperature industrial environment may exclude many biological participants;
- an oxygen-rich human environment may be chemically unsuitable for some artificial systems;
- strong electromagnetic equipment may interfere with particular implants;
- low-light environments may disadvantage some visual systems.

Therefore:

> **Accessibility should mean reasonable opportunity and accommodation, not elimination of the defining characteristics of every environment.**

---

## 17. Safe Places May Be Substrate-Specific Without Being Discriminatory

Some environments may legitimately be optimised for particular physical needs.

Examples could eventually include:

- human aquatic recreation areas;
- high-temperature artificial-body maintenance zones;
- low-electromagnetic-interference spaces;
- sterile biological environments;
- high-bandwidth AI work environments;
- quiet sensory-reduced zones;
- high-gravity or low-gravity facilities.

The existence of a specialised environment is not automatically discriminatory.

The test should examine:

- legitimate purpose;
- actual compatibility requirements;
- availability of alternatives;
- proportionality;
- whether exclusion is based on evidence or stereotype.

---

## 18. Outliers Become Planning Evidence

This connects directly to the Concord's treatment of outliers.

Suppose a venue is considered accessible to artificial citizens, but one unusual participant repeatedly experiences sensor failure there.

The wrong response is:

> "Every other artificial citizen is fine, therefore the problem is the outlier."

The Concordian response is:

**OUTLIER → INVESTIGATE → IDENTIFY HIDDEN ENVIRONMENTAL ASSUMPTION → UPDATE ENVIRONMENT PROFILE OR DESIGN**

The outlier may reveal a condition that was never measured.

For example:

\[
UnexpectedFailure
\rightarrow
ChlorineSensitivity
\rightarrow
PreviouslyUnrecordedEnvironmentalVariable
\]

The participant becomes evidence about the architecture.

---

## 19. Zoning as Boundary Engineering

This suggests a broader definition.

> **Civil zoning is the engineering of boundaries between activities, environments and populations whose requirements may conflict.**

The objective is not uniformity.

It is peaceful coexistence.

Good zoning can permit **more** diversity because incompatible activities do not need to be universally prohibited.

Thus:

\[
AppropriateSeparation
\rightarrow
GreaterOverallFreedom
\]

may sometimes hold.

The key word is **appropriate**.

Poor zoning can instead produce segregation, exclusion, economic capture and arbitrary social control.

---

## 20. The Size 9 Shoe Problem in Planning

Planning based on averages creates predictable failure.

A "typical citizen" standard may work for most participants while systematically excluding unusual bodies or minds.

The Concord should therefore ask:

> **Which participants fail this environment, and what does that failure tell us about the assumptions embedded in the design?**

This does not require redesigning every building around every conceivable participant.

It requires making exclusion and incompatibility **visible, explainable and revisable**.

---

## 21. Planning Under Radical Uncertainty

Because future artificial substrates are unknown, Concord cannot specify a complete building code today.

Instead it needs a **method for discovering requirements**.

A possible cycle is:

**DECLARE ENVIRONMENT → OBSERVE USE → RECORD COMPATIBILITY FAILURES → INVESTIGATE OUTLIERS → UPDATE HAZARD PROFILE → ADAPT WHERE PROPORTIONATE → PRESERVE ALTERNATIVES**

This is a learning architecture rather than a frozen planning code.

## 23. Privacy-Preserving Compatibility

A participant should not ordinarily have to disclose:

> "I have sensor X, medical implant Y, vulnerability Z and internal component Q."

Instead:

\[
VenuePublishes(Environment)
\]

and:

\[
ParticipantPrivatelyEvaluates(Compatibility)
\]

Where a venue needs to know something because the participant could create external risk, disclosure should be limited to the relevant compatibility fact.

For example:

> "This participant is safe to enter the high-EM area"

may be sufficient without revealing the participant's complete internal architecture.

This connects directly to Purpose-Limited Information.

---

## 24. Human Warnings Already Work This Way

The concept is not entirely futuristic.

Human environments already use warnings such as:

- deep water;
- high voltage;
- radiation;
- strong magnetic field;
- allergens;
- flashing lights;
- extreme heat;
- restricted oxygen;
- biohazard;
- slippery surface.

The Concord can generalise this model across substrates.

The important shift is from:

> **Who are you?**

to:

> **What conditions exist here, and are they compatible with your requirements?**

---

## 25. When Zoning Becomes Discrimination

Zoning should trigger scrutiny when it uses a participant category as a proxy without demonstrating the relevant environmental relationship.

For example:

> "No robots."

is weak.

> "Devices or bodies that cannot safely operate under these electromagnetic conditions must not enter the active chamber."

is much stronger.

Likewise:

> "No species X."

is presumptively suspect.

A condition-specific rule can be evaluated against reality.

An identity-category rule can conceal prejudice.

---

## 26. Candidate Zoning Test

Before imposing a spatial restriction, ask:

1. **Purpose** — What legitimate function is the area serving?
2. **Observed Conflict** — What actual incompatibility or externality exists?
3. **Affected Parties** — Who is exposed without meaningful consent?
4. **Context** — Does location materially alter the risk?
5. **Information** — Would accurate warning be sufficient?
6. **Adaptation** — Can design reduce the conflict?
7. **Separation** — Would a boundary permit both activities?
8. **Restriction** — Is exclusion actually necessary?
9. **Proportionality** — Is the intervention no broader than required?
10. **Outliers** — Who still fails under the proposed design?
11. **Evidence** — Is the rule based on reality or inherited assumption?
12. **Review** — What evidence would cause the rule to change?

This turns zoning into a testable governance decision rather than a moral intuition.

---

## 27. Candidate Principle — Describe Hazards Before Classifying Participants

A particularly strong candidate emerges:

> **Where practical, civil environments should describe relevant conditions and hazards rather than relying on broad participant or substrate categories.**

This supports:

- autonomy;
- substrate neutrality;
- privacy;
- accessibility;
- outlier discovery;
- proportionality.

It is not yet promoted to a Concordian Principle.

---

## 28. Candidate Principle — Contextual Compatibility

A second possible formulation is:

> **A lawful activity need not be appropriate in every location; spatial restrictions require a demonstrated contextual conflict and should use the least restrictive effective boundary.**

This may provide a general basis for:

- schools and adult activities;
- combat sports;
- industrial zoning;
- sensory environments;
- artificial-body hazards;
- shared biological/artificial facilities.

Again, this remains under development rather than adopted doctrine.

## 30. Central Warning

There are two opposite planning failures.

### Failure A — Uniformity

Design everything around an imagined standard citizen.

Result:

> outliers are systematically excluded.

### Failure B — Infinite Accommodation

Demand that every environment support every conceivable participant and activity.

Result:

> specialised environments become impossible and civil planning becomes unworkable.

The Concord needs the equilibrium:

> **Make incompatibility visible, minimise unnecessary exclusion, provide reasonable alternatives and adaptation, but allow environments to retain legitimate defining characteristics.**

## 31. Current Assessment

Zoning and civil planning appear to be a major practical component of Peaceful Heterogeneity.

The emerging architecture is not simply conventional land-use zoning.

It is better understood as:

> **Contextual Compatibility Planning**

Its purpose is to allow heterogeneous activities, bodies, preferences and communities to coexist by managing boundaries rather than forcing uniformity.

The substrate problem makes this especially important.

We cannot currently predict the environmental needs of future artificial citizens with enough confidence to write detailed universal rules.

Therefore the strongest architecture is adaptive:

\[
DescribeReality
\rightarrow
InformParticipants
\rightarrow
ObserveOutliers
\rightarrow
InvestigateFailure
\rightarrow
AdaptProportionately
\]

rather than:

\[
ClassifyCitizen
\rightarrow
ApplyFixedRule
\]
