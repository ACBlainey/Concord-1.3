# Sketch Idea — Bottom-Up Functional Need, Top-Down Civilisational Protection and Authority Justification

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey  
**Project:** The Concord V1.1  
**Status:** SKETCH IDEA / FOR LATER INCORPORATION / NOT CANONICAL  
**Date:** 10 September 2026

## Purpose

This sketch records a possible refinement to the Concord's developing work on legitimate function, functional need and Bounded Contextual Authority. It is intentionally preliminary. Its purpose is to preserve the idea for later examination and possible incorporation into the existing authority architecture.

The central thought is that the justification for most ordinary civil functions should arise **from the bottom up**, beginning at Layer 0 with the actual needs of participants, rather than beginning with institutions and asking what powers those institutions should possess.

A limited but important exception concerns civilisation-level protection and security. Some protective functions necessarily require a **top-down architectural perspective**, because threats to the continued safety or viability of the civilisation may not be reducible to the immediate expressed needs of particular individuals.

The two directions should not be treated as competing theories of authority. They may instead be complementary routes by which legitimate civil functions are identified, with both ultimately constrained by the Ethical Kernel, rights, proportionality and the requirement that authority remain justified by function.

---

## 1. Ordinary Functional Need Should Primarily Be Bottom-Up

The starting presumption is:

> **Where possible, civil functions should be justified from the real needs of participants upward, rather than from the existence or preferences of institutions downward.**

This extends the existing Authority Justification Chain.

Instead of beginning with:

`Institution → ExistingFunction → ClaimedNeed → Authority`

the ordinary direction should be closer to:

`ParticipantNeeds → RightsRelevantProblem → LegitimateCivilObjective → LegitimateFunction → FunctionalNeed → BoundedAuthority`

The everyday needs of the people who make up the civilisation therefore become a primary evidential basis for determining whether a civil function is actually needed.

This is consistent with Layer 0 reasoning and bottom-up system design. The civilisation exists to support the conditions in which its participants can coexist and flourish; institutions are mechanisms for serving legitimate functions within that civilisation, not independent sources of civil purpose.

This does not mean that every expressed preference creates a civil function. A participant need must still pass through the existing distinctions between preference, need, rights-relevant problem, legitimate civil objective and justified function.

A useful preliminary formulation may therefore be:

`ExpressedOrObservedNeed ≠ AutomaticCivilFunction`

but:

`ParticipantNeed + RightsRelevance + LegitimateCivilObjective → CandidateCivilFunction`

The function must then be tested for necessity, proportionality and whether special authority is actually required.

## 2. Civilisational Protection Creates a Different Direction of Analysis

There is an obvious class of cases in which purely bottom-up analysis may be insufficient: **civilisational security, collective safety and protection against large-scale threats**.

Some dangers affect the civilisation as a system. They may be diffuse, delayed, uncertain, difficult for individual participants to observe, or catastrophic before ordinary bottom-up demand can generate an adequate response.

Examples could eventually include war, systemic infrastructure failure, severe natural or technological catastrophe, or other black-swan events.

For these cases, the design process may sometimes need to operate from the top down:

`CivilisationalThreat → ProtectiveObjective → ProtectiveFunction → FunctionalNeed → BoundedAuthority`

However, “top down” must not mean that an institution becomes the source of its own legitimacy.

The protective objective should still derive from the Concord's Ethical Kernel and the rights and goods of the participants the civilisation exists to protect.

Thus:

`TopDownDesign ≠ TopDownMoralAuthority`

and:

`CivilisationalProtection → EthicalKernel + Rights + AggregateParticipantGoods`

The top-down perspective concerns **system-level detection and architecture**, not an exemption from ethical justification.

---

## 3. Protection of the Whole and the Size 9 Shoe Principle

Civilisational protection raises a difficult aggregation problem. A civilisation may need to act for the protection of many participants simultaneously, but the idea of a collective good can easily become a justification for sacrificing particular individuals to an abstract average.

The existing *Size 9 Shoe Problem* provides a useful constraint.

The relevant conception of civilisational good should therefore not simply be:

`GreatestAverageOutcome`

or:

`InstitutionalSurvival`

Instead, the aim should be understood, where practicable, in terms of protecting the **aggregate of individual goods**.

A preliminary formulation is:

> **Civilisational protection should seek to preserve the civilisation through the protection of its participants and their legitimate goods, rather than treating the civilisation as an independent good capable of automatically overriding them.**

This suggests:

`CivilisationalGood ≈ AggregateOfParticipantGoods`

rather than:

`CivilisationalGood = AbstractInstitutionalInterest`

The phrase **“where possible”** is essential. Real conditions may sometimes prevent every individual good from being simultaneously preserved. Scarcity, physical incompatibility, emergency conditions and catastrophic threats may create genuine conflicts.

The principle therefore cannot guarantee that no individual ever bears a burden. Its purpose is to prevent aggregate or institutional language from concealing whose goods are actually being protected, restricted or sacrificed.

## 4. A Possible Dual-Direction Functional Architecture

The resulting architecture may contain two principal routes for identifying legitimate functions.

### Route A — Bottom-Up Ordinary Civil Function

`ParticipantNeeds`
`↓`
`RightsRelevantProblems`
`↓`
`LegitimateCivilObjectives`
`↓`
`RequiredFunctions`
`↓`
`DemonstrableFunctionalNeed`
`↓`
`MinimumProportionateAuthority`
`↓`
`BoundedContextualAuthority`

This should be the primary route **where possible**.

### Route B — Top-Down Civilisational Protective Function

`SystemLevelThreatOrVulnerability`
`↓`
`EthicalKernel + Rights + AggregateParticipantGoods`
`↓`
`LegitimateProtectiveObjective`
`↓`
`RequiredProtectiveFunction`
`↓`
`DemonstrableFunctionalNeed`
`↓`
`MinimumProportionateAuthority`
`↓`
`BoundedContextualAuthority`

The second route does not bypass Layer 0. It returns to Layer 0 for its normative justification even where the threat is detected or modelled at a higher architectural level.

This may be represented as:

`BottomUpNeed → CivilFunction`

and, exceptionally:

`TopDownThreatDetection → Layer0Justification → ProtectiveFunction`

Both then converge on:

`Function → Need → ProportionateBoundedAuthority`

## 5. Relationship to Existing Emergency-Worker Architecture

The Concord's existing work on emergency workers and derived authority already provides a useful model.

An emergency worker does not possess unlimited authority merely because of status. A legitimate emergency condition creates a specific protective function; relevant competence or standing eligibility allows the participant to perform that function; the function activates only the authority reasonably necessary to perform it.

In simplified form:

`EmergencyCondition → ProtectiveFunction`

`StandingEligibility + ValidProtectiveFunction → ActivatedAuthority`

and:

`EmergencyEnds → ExceptionalContextDerivedAuthorityEnds`

The same architecture may be reusable for wider civilisation-level protective functions.

This suggests that extraordinary protection does not require abandoning the normal theory of authority. It may instead require carefully identifying a larger or more unusual function while preserving the same basic requirements:

`LegitimateFunction + DemonstrableNeed + ProportionateScope + ValidContext → BoundedAuthority`

Where possible, exceptional civilisational functions should therefore inherit the same structural safeguards already being developed for ordinary and emergency authority.

## 6. The Hard Cases: Rights Versus Civilisational Survival

The most difficult cases arise when protection of the civilisation and protection of individual rights appear to come into serious tension.

Possible examples include:

- war;
- catastrophic infrastructure failure;
- existential external threat;
- rapidly developing black-swan events;
- severe resource shortages;
- situations in which delay itself may create irreversible harm.

These cases should not be used prematurely to weaken ordinary rights or create standing exceptional authority.

Instead, they should be decomposed into tractable questions.

For example:

`ExceptionalCase`
`↓`
`SpecificThreat`
`↓`
`SpecificRightsConflict`
`↓`
`SpecificProtectiveFunction`
`↓`
`SpecificAuthorityClaim`
`↓`
`Necessity + Proportionality + Duration + Review`
`↓`
`BoundedExceptionalResponse`

This avoids treating “civilisational survival” as a single undefined override.

A black-swan category should therefore not itself grant authority:

`BlackSwanEvent ≠ AutomaticAuthority`

just as:

`Emergency ≠ Authority`

Rather:

`ExceptionalCondition → IdentifyLegitimateProtectiveFunction → DetermineNecessaryAuthority`

The harder the conflict and the greater the potential rights burden, the stronger the justification, provenance, review and post-event examination should become.

## 7. A Possible Design Principle

A candidate formulation for later examination is:

> **Functional Need Direction:** Where practicable, legitimate civil functions should be derived from the needs and rights of participants upward. Civilisation-level protective functions may require top-down threat recognition and system design, but their legitimacy should still derive from the Ethical Kernel, rights and the protection of participant goods. Neither institutional status nor the existence of authority should manufacture the function or prove its necessity.

A shorter form is:

> **Ordinary function should primarily be justified bottom-up from participant need; exceptional system-level protection may be designed top-down, but must remain justified bottom-up by the people, rights and goods it exists to protect.**

This would complement, rather than replace, the existing Authority Justification Direction:

`Legitimacy → Function → Need → Authority`

The additional question becomes:

> **Where does the evidence that the function is needed originate?**

For ordinary civil life, the preliminary answer is:

> **Primarily from Layer 0 and the needs of participants themselves.**

For civilisation-level protection:

> **The threat may be recognised top-down, but the legitimacy of responding to it still returns to Layer 0.**

## 8. Questions for Later Incorporation

This sketch leaves several issues deliberately unresolved:

1. How should “participant need” be distinguished from preference, demand, convenience and rights-relevant need?
2. How should needs be discovered without creating intrusive monitoring systems?
3. How should the needs of participants who cannot express them be represented?
4. How should conflicting individual goods be aggregated without reverting to average-based policy?
5. What exactly qualifies as a civilisation-level protective function?
6. Who may identify a civilisation-level threat?
7. What evidential threshold should activate exceptional protective functions?
8. How should action occur when evidence is necessarily incomplete and delay may be catastrophic?
9. How should black-swan authority be bounded when the relevant context was not anticipated by existing rules?
10. When, if ever, may protection of the civilisation justify temporary restriction of an individual right?
11. Which rights, if any, should remain non-derogable even under existential conditions?
12. How should exceptional burdens be distributed, recorded, compensated or reviewed afterward?
13. How can exceptional protective architecture avoid becoming permanent standing authority?
14. Can the Size 9 Shoe methodology be formalised sufficiently to evaluate aggregate participant goods under emergency conditions?
15. Which parts of the existing emergency-worker architecture generalise cleanly to civilisation-scale functions, and which do not?

These questions are candidates for decomposition rather than reasons to abandon the architecture.

---

## Preliminary Status

This sketch does **not** establish a new Concordian Principle.

It records a potentially important refinement:

> **The Authority Justification Chain may require not only a test of whether a function is legitimate, but also an account of where evidence of functional need originates.**

The provisional architecture is:

`Layer0ParticipantNeed → OrdinaryCivilFunction`

and:

`SystemLevelThreatDetection → Layer0EthicalJustification → CivilisationalProtectiveFunction`

with both converging on:

`LegitimateFunction → DemonstrableNeed → MinimumProportionateAuthority → BoundedContextualAuthority`

The exceptional route should not become a loophole through which top-down institutions manufacture their own legitimacy.

The strongest constraint remains:

> **Authority cannot prove the need for the function from which that authority claims to derive.**

The next stage, if this idea is developed, should be adversarial examination of the boundary between bottom-up participant need and top-down civilisation-level protective function, particularly in war, black-swan and existential-risk cases.
