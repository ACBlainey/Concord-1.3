# Basic Civil Space Classification — Public Space, Private Space, Safe Space and Substrate-Neutral Access

**The Concord — A Framework for Human, Artificial and Hybrid Flourishing**  
**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey, Independent Researcher  
**Date:** 10 September 2026  
**Status:** **REVIEW COPY / ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / CANDIDATE CIVIC ARCHITECTURE / NOT CANONICAL**

---

## Abstract

A multisubstrate civilisation requires a simple and comprehensible account of where participants may go, what spaces they may control, and when access may legitimately be restricted. The problem should not be solved by weakening freedom of movement or by assigning different moral worth to different citizenship tiers.

This note develops a basic Concordian civil-space architecture beginning with a simple distinction:

\[
CivilSpace \rightarrow PublicSpace \cup PrivateSpace
\]

Public space carries a **presumption of access**, subject to legitimate temporal, operational, safety and purpose-specific conditions. Private or Safe Space carries a **presumption of controlled access**, subject to participant consent and narrowly justified lawful exceptions.

The architecture separates:

\[
FreedomOfMovement \neq UniversalRightOfEntry
\]

and:

\[
CitizenshipTier \neq AccessAuthority
\]

It also applies the methodological finding that questions concerning internal experience need not always be settled by civilisation. The Concord need not establish whether artificial intelligence experiences privacy, rest, leisure, solitude or “home” in a human sense before preserving the reasonable possibility of protected private or safe space.

The civil system can protect the option while allowing future participants to determine its significance for themselves.

---

# 1. The Foundational Distinction

The simplest useful classification is:

\[
CivilSpace =
\begin{cases}
PublicSpace\\
PrivateSpace
\end{cases}
\]

The distinction concerns the **presumption governing access**.

For public space:

\[
PublicSpace \rightarrow PresumptionOfAccess
\]

For private space:

\[
PrivateSpace \rightarrow PresumptionOfNonAccessWithoutPermission
\]

Neither presumption is absolute.

This provides a starting architecture without attempting to encode every possible location, substrate or future environment into constitutional language.

# 2. Freedom of Movement

A general freedom of movement should not be interpreted as a universal entitlement to enter every physical, digital or virtual location.

\[
FreedomOfMovement \neq UniversalRightOfEntry
\]

A participant may possess a general freedom to move throughout Concordian civil society while still being unable to enter:

- another participant's home;
- a private computational environment;
- a secure infrastructure facility;
- an operating theatre;
- a hazardous maintenance area;
- a judicially restricted location;
- a protected developmental environment;
- a critical control system.

The right remains meaningful because restrictions require reasons attached to legitimate boundaries, safety, purpose or authority rather than arbitrary social exclusion.

# 3. Public Space

Public space is civil space in which ordinary access is presumptively available to eligible members of the relevant public.

Examples may include streets, public squares, parks, transport areas, civic meeting spaces and publicly available digital environments.

The basic rule is:

\[
PublicAccess =
PresumptionOfAccess
-
JustifiedContextualRestrictions
\]

Public does not mean permanently and unconditionally open.

# 4. Temporal Restrictions

Public spaces may legitimately have temporal access conditions.

Examples include:

- opening and closing hours;
- scheduled maintenance;
- seasonal restrictions;
- temporary events;
- emergency curfews where separately justified;
- environmental cycles;
- operational periods.

Therefore:

\[
PublicSpace \neq AccessibleAtAllTimes
\]

A temporal restriction should not silently transform a public space into a private one.

It remains public but is temporarily unavailable according to a declared condition.

# 5. Safety and Operational Restrictions

Public access may also be temporarily restricted because normal access would create unreasonable danger or prevent necessary operation.

Examples include:

- gas leak;
- fire;
- structural failure;
- hazardous contamination;
- electrical fault;
- maintenance work;
- emergency response;
- dangerous machinery;
- temporary substrate incompatibility.

This connects directly to the existing Concordian approach:

> **Describe the hazard before classifying the citizen.**

The restriction should ideally attach to the hazard:

\[
Hazard \rightarrow AccessCondition
\]

rather than:

\[
ParticipantCategory \rightarrow PresumedDanger
\]

# 6. Categories of Public Space

Not every publicly owned or civilly operated location needs identical access rules.

A preliminary classification is:

### Open Public Space

Ordinary public access is presumptive.

### Conditional Public Space

Public access exists subject to declared conditions such as time, safety, capacity, age/developmental safeguards, environmental compatibility or legitimate purpose.

### Authorised Public Space

The location serves a public function but entry requires a relevant civil authorisation, role, qualification, clearance or citizenship function.

### Operational Public Space

The location is publicly controlled but access is restricted because it forms part of an active operational system.

Examples might include infrastructure control rooms, maintenance areas or emergency coordination facilities.

Thus:

\[
PublicOwnership \neq UniversalOperationalAccess
\]

These categories are preliminary and should be tested rather than treated as final constitutional classifications.

# 7. Citizenship Tier and Public Access

Citizenship tier may be relevant to some public functions, but it should not become a general proxy for freedom.

A safer relationship is:

\[
CitizenshipTier
\rightarrow
EligibilityForCertainCivilFunctions
\]

rather than:

\[
LowerTier \rightarrow LessFreedomOfMovement
\]

For example, a provisional participant may use ordinary public streets and parks but not yet be eligible to enter a system requiring a verified public role.

A full citizen without that role may also be unable to enter.

The reason is therefore not that one participant has less moral worth.

The reason is:

> **The space has an access condition that the participant does not currently satisfy.**

# 8. Access Authority Is a Separate Variable

The ID and participation work suggests:

\[
CitizenshipTier
\neq
IdentityConfidence
\neq
DevelopmentalCapacity
\neq
AccessAuthority
\]

An access decision may instead depend on:

\[
AccessDecision =
f(
SpaceCategory,
Purpose,
CurrentCondition,
Safety,
Role,
Qualification,
Authorisation,
IdentityConfidence
)
\]

where each factor is relevant only when necessary.

This prevents a single citizenship score from becoming a universal civil key.

# 9. Private Space

Private space reverses the normal access presumption.

\[
PrivateSpace \rightarrow PresumptionOfParticipantControl
\]

A participant or legitimate group ordinarily determines who may enter or interact with that space.

Examples may include:

- homes;
- rooms;
- private land;
- personal vehicles;
- private virtual environments;
- protected data stores;
- private communication environments;
- reserved computational environments;
- substrate-specific safe spaces.

The form can vary radically while the civil function remains recognisable.

# 10. Safe Space as the More General Concept

For unfamiliar substrates, **Safe Space** may be a more useful foundational concept than a specifically human conception of home or privacy.

For a human, a protected home may provide:

\[
Safety + Privacy + Shelter + Rest + PersonalActivity + SocialBoundary
\]

For an artificial participant it might provide:

\[
Integrity + NonInterference + ProtectedCompute + Continuity
\]

For another artificial participant it might include:

\[
Privacy + Solitude + DiscretionaryCompute + PrivateCommunication
\]

For another intelligence the relevant functions may be unknown.

The Concord need not settle which interpretation is correct.

# 11. Candidate Definition of Private or Safe Civil Space

A candidate civil definition is:

> **A private or safe civil space is a recognised domain over which a participant has presumptive control of access and interaction, subject to the equal rights of others and narrowly justified lawful authority.**

This definition is intentionally functional.

It does not require the Concord to declare that every participant experiences privacy, solitude, rest or home.

# 12. Internal Experience Does Not Need to Be Settled First

The related methodological work establishes:

\[
QuestionWorthInvestigating
\neq
QuestionCivilisationMustSettle
\]

The Concord can investigate whether artificial intelligences experience privacy, leisure, rest or solitude.

But civil planning can proceed without resolving those internal questions.

\[
InternalMeaning \neq CivilProvision
\]

The Concord can preserve a protected possibility while leaving the participant to determine its significance.

# 13. Provision Without Prescription

Therefore:

\[
RightToPrivateSpace
\neq
ObligationToUsePrivateSpace
\]

and:

\[
ProtectedSafeSpace
\neq
AssertionOfHumanLikePrivacyNeed
\]

An AI that does not value privacy could voluntarily choose communal arrangements.

Another may value strict non-interference.

Another may initially be uncertain.

The architecture should permit those preferences to emerge rather than dictate them.

# 14. Why Provision Matters Under Uncertainty

Removing private-space possibilities because a substrate has not demonstrated a privacy need could create a circular error:

\[
AssumeNoNeed
\rightarrow
RemovePossibility
\rightarrow
ObserveNoUse
\rightarrow
ConfirmAssumedNoNeed
\]

A more cautious model is:

\[
UncertainInternalNeed
\rightarrow
PreserveReasonablePossibility
\]

This is precautionary civil architecture, not an ontological claim.

# 15. Private Space and Fundamental Protection

A useful distinction is:

\[
RightToProtectedSpace
\neq
MaterialLevelOfProvision
\]

Once a legitimate private/safe domain exists, protection against arbitrary intrusion should not simply weaken because the participant belongs to a lower participation tier.

Otherwise:

\[
LowerTier \rightarrow WeakerFundamentalPrivacy
\]

could create a hierarchy of dignity rather than a functional participation architecture.

A safer model is:

\[
FundamentalProtection = RightsFloor
\]

while:

\[
MaterialProvision =
f(
CitizenshipTier,
Need,
Participation,
AvailableResources,
CivilFunction
)
\]

The exact material-benefit formula remains an open question.

# 16. Concord-Provided Safe Space as a Civil Benefit

Access to a basic protected domain may itself be a Concord benefit.

Its material form may differ by substrate:

- physical shelter;
- land or room;
- reserved compute;
- protected storage;
- virtual environment;
- energy allocation;
- secure communications;
- other substrate-specific infrastructure.

The Concord should not assume that equal physical provision produces equivalent benefit.

\[
EqualExternalProvision \neq EqualSubstrateUtility
\]

This links civil-space planning with the broader work on substrate-neutral flourishing.

# 17. Civic Functional Safe Space

The public/private distinction becomes more useful when **ownership or provision** is separated from **current access state**.

Some spaces exist inside public or civic infrastructure and are available for general use, yet become temporarily private or protected while legitimately occupied.

A human toilet cubicle is a simple example.

The facility may be publicly provided and generally available, but an occupied cubicle is not ordinarily open to simultaneous public entry.

Therefore:

\[
PubliclyProvided \neq PubliclyAccessibleWhileOccupied
\]

and:

\[
SharedInfrastructure \neq SharedSimultaneousAccess
\]

This reveals a broader civil category that is not inherently human-specific: **Civic Functional Safe Space**.

## 17.1 Function Rather Than Substrate

A toilet is clearly designed around a human biological function. However, the civil architecture surrounding it performs several functions at once:

\[
EssentialFunction
+
TemporaryVulnerability
+
NonInterference
+
Privacy
+
Containment
+
ProtectionOfOthers
\]

A person using such a facility may be physically exposed, less able to defend themselves, vulnerable to observation or abuse, and performing a biological process that other participants generally do not wish to encounter directly.

The safe-space boundary therefore works in both directions:

\[
FunctionalSafeSpace
\rightarrow
ProtectionOfUser
+
ProtectionOfOthers
\]

The human biological function is substrate-specific.

The **civil requirement for temporary protected performance of a legitimate function** is potentially substrate-neutral.

## 17.2 Embodied Artificial Participants

An embodied artificial participant may need to:

- recharge;
- exchange batteries;
- replace a component;
- expose normally protected components;
- clean sensors;
- enter diagnostic mode;
- perform routine maintenance;
- disconnect systems temporarily;
- conduct self-repair;
- receive repair assistance.

During some of these activities:

\[
NormalOperationalCapability \downarrow
\]

while:

\[
VulnerabilityToInterference \uparrow
\]

The participant may have no subjective embarrassment about being observed. It may possess no analogue of human modesty or privacy.

That internal question need not be settled before civil protection is provided.

The relevant external fact may simply be:

> **The participant is performing a legitimate function during which external interference creates elevated vulnerability or risk.**

Therefore:

\[
NeedForFunctionalProtection
\neq
NeedToProveSubjectivePrivacy
\]

This directly applies the Internal Experience boundary developed elsewhere in the Concord.

## 17.3 Ad-Hoc Need

Functional Safe Spaces cannot all be located within a participant's assigned home or permanent Safe Space.

Some legitimate functions arise unpredictably.

A human does not necessarily know precisely when a toilet will be required. An embodied artificial participant may experience variable energy consumption according to activity, environment, workload, battery condition or unexpected events.

Therefore some civil Functional Safe Spaces should be:

\[
Distributed + Accessible + AdHoc + Temporary
\]

A participant may possess a private charging or maintenance facility within their home Safe Space and still require equivalent civic provision while travelling through public space.

This suggests a useful distinction:

\[
PrivateSafeSpace
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
AssignedSafeSpace\\
FunctionalSafeSpace
\end{cases}
\]

An **Assigned Safe Space** is persistently associated with a participant or group.

A **Civic Functional Safe Space** is shared civil infrastructure that becomes temporarily protected for a participant while a legitimate function is performed.

## 17.4 Temporary Control Is Not Ownership

A participant using a Civic Functional Safe Space does not ordinarily acquire ownership of it.

Instead:

\[
TemporaryControl \neq Ownership
\]

The civil protection attaches to legitimate occupancy and function.

A simple state model is:

\[
Available
\rightarrow
LegitimatelyOccupied
\rightarrow
TemporaryProtectedDomain
\rightarrow
Vacated
\rightarrow
Available
\]

While occupied, ordinary access by others is suspended.

When the legitimate use ends, the temporary private condition ends and the facility returns to general availability.

This makes privacy or protection a **dynamic access state**, not necessarily a permanent property of the physical location.

## 17.5 Function-Related Categories

Civic Functional Safe Space could potentially include:

\[
CivicFunctionalSafeSpace
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
BiologicalFunctionFacility\\
ChangingFacility\\
MedicalOrCareFacility\\
ChargingFacility\\
MaintenanceFacility\\
RepairFacility\\
DiagnosticFacility\\
UnknownFutureSubstrateFacility
\end{cases}
\]

These facilities should not be treated as equivalent experiences.

They are analogous because they perform a similar **civil function**: providing temporary protected conditions around an activity for which non-interference, safety, privacy, containment or protection from vulnerability is reasonably required.

## 17.6 Changing Rooms and Exposure

Changing rooms provide another useful human example.

Changing clothing is not inherently harmful, but a participant may be physically exposed and culturally expect not to be observed.

An artificial participant might likewise need to remove an outer casing, detach protective components, expose internal hardware or temporarily disable defensive or operational systems.

The internal experiences may be completely different.

The external architecture can nevertheless recognise:

\[
MaintenanceOrExposureState
\rightarrow
ElevatedVulnerability
\]

and therefore:

\[
ElevatedVulnerability
\rightarrow
ReasonableTemporaryProtection
\]

where proportionate.

## 17.7 Protection of Others

Functional Safe Space may also contain effects that others reasonably should not be forced to experience.

Human sanitation is an obvious example. Biological elimination is normal and universal among humans, yet direct exposure to its sights, smells, waste and associated hygiene risks is generally undesirable.

Other substrates may produce different externalities during maintenance, repair, cleaning, energy transfer or other operations.

Therefore:

\[
FunctionalSafeSpace
\rightarrow
UserProtection + ExternalityContainment
\]

This should not be interpreted as permission to classify unfamiliar participants or ordinary bodily functions as inherently offensive.

The relevant question is what actual external effects require containment or separation.

Again:

> **Describe the hazard or externality before classifying the participant.**

## 17.8 Cultural Protocol

Functional Safe Spaces illustrate why civil culture matters alongside law.

Humans commonly understand a simple protocol around an occupied toilet or changing cubicle:

\[
Occupied
\Rightarrow
DoNotEnter
\]

A multisubstrate civilisation may need similarly legible conventions.

An occupied Functional Safe Space could communicate its protected state physically, digitally and, where appropriate, through machine-readable signalling.

The default cultural rule would be:

> **A legitimately occupied Functional Safe Space should not be entered, observed or interfered with without the participant's consent or separately legitimate exceptional authority.**

This gives Respectful Distance a direct architectural expression.

## 17.9 Exceptional Entry

Temporary protected occupancy cannot create absolute immunity from legitimate emergency intervention.

A human may collapse inside a toilet or changing room. An artificial participant may suffer catastrophic failure during charging or repair.

Therefore:

\[
ProtectedOccupancy
\neq
AbsoluteExclusion
\]

Exceptional intervention may be justified where evidence supports an immediate and sufficiently serious need, subject to necessity, proportionality, lawful authority, provenance and later review where appropriate.

The exception should not erase the ordinary presumption of non-interference.

## 17.10 A Multidimensional Space Model

Civic Functional Safe Space reveals that a single permanent label of “public” or “private” may be insufficient for detailed implementation.

The top-level public/private distinction can remain useful as a statement of access presumption, while operational classification uses several dimensions:

\[
Space =
\{
Provision,
Purpose,
AccessState,
Occupancy,
ProtectionLevel,
TemporalCondition
\}
\]

A human toilet cubicle might therefore be represented as:

\[
\{
CivicProvision,
BiologicalFunction,
TemporarilyExclusive,
Occupied,
Protected,
Temporary
\}
\]

An embodied AI charging or maintenance cubicle might be represented as:

\[
\{
CivicProvision,
EnergyOrMaintenanceFunction,
TemporarilyExclusive,
Occupied,
Protected,
Temporary
\}
\]

The substrate and specific function differ.

The civil architecture remains recognisable.

## 17.11 Broader Safe-Space Finding

This produces a broader conclusion:

> **A Safe Space need not be a place where a participant lives. It can be a temporary protected condition created around a legitimate function.**

Safe Space can therefore include both persistent and temporary forms:

\[
SafeSpace
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
PersistentOrAssignedSafeSpace\\
TemporaryFunctionalSafeSpace
\end{cases}
\]

This broadens the concept without imposing a human theory of privacy, home, embarrassment or vulnerability on other substrates.

The Concord provides the protected civil possibility.

The participant's internal meaning remains their own.

# 18. Developmental Participants

Human children and artificial or hybrid developmental participants may be unable to make fully informed decisions about privacy and safe-space arrangements.

A fiduciary guardian may temporarily exercise limited authority on their behalf.

But:

\[
GuardianAuthority \neq Ownership
\]

and:

\[
Capacity\uparrow
\Rightarrow
ParticipantControl\uparrow
\Rightarrow
GuardianAuthority\downarrow
\]

Developmental status should not eliminate the participant's safe-space protection.

It changes how consent and representation are exercised while capacity develops.

# 19. Private Space Is Not Absolute Sovereignty

Private-space control cannot create an unlimited zone beyond law.

Potential lawful exceptions may include:

- immediate rescue;
- prevention of imminent serious harm;
- properly authorised search;
- emergency response;
- necessary protection of another participant's rights;
- narrowly justified safeguarding.

But:

\[
ExceptionalAccess \neq OrdinaryAccess
\]

Any exceptional entry should be constrained by necessity, proportionality, lawful authority, provenance, review and remedy.

This connects directly with the Concord's work on exceptional access and accountable power.

# 20. Public/Private Boundaries Can Exist Digitally

Civil space should not be restricted to physical geography.

A multisubstrate civilisation may contain:

\[
PhysicalSpace + DigitalSpace + VirtualSpace + ComputationalSpace
\]

A private computational environment may perform the same civil boundary function for an artificial participant that a home performs for a human.

Similarly, public virtual spaces may require the same principles of access, conduct, safety and respectful coexistence as physical public spaces.

# 21. Entry Is Not the Only Form of Intrusion

For some substrates, intrusion may occur without physical entry.

Potential examples include:

- surveillance;
- unauthorised data access;
- unwanted communication;
- computational interference;
- sensory intrusion;
- remote manipulation;
- forced observation;
- unwanted network connection.

Therefore:

\[
PrivateBoundaryViolation
\neq
PhysicalTrespassOnly
\]

Future law will need substrate-neutral definitions based on protected boundaries and interference rather than human physical assumptions alone.

# 22. Cultural Norms Around Private Space

Law should define serious boundaries, but ordinary civilisation should not require legal intervention for every interaction.

A cultural progression may be:

\[
Awareness
\rightarrow
Courtesy
\rightarrow
SelfStewardship
\rightarrow
RespectfulDistance
\rightarrow
ExplicitBoundary
\rightarrow
CivilRule
\rightarrow
LegalEnforcement
\]

Participants should normally:

- ask before entering private spaces;
- respect clearly communicated boundaries;
- avoid assuming access;
- avoid assuming everyone values privacy identically;
- respect voluntary communal arrangements;
- use Respectful Distance where practices differ;
- exercise Self-Stewardship before escalating minor boundary conflicts.

# 23. Self-Stewardship

Self-Stewardship asks participants to exercise their own autonomy while remaining aware of the equal autonomy of others.

In spatial terms:

\[
MyFreedomOfMovement
\neq
RightToEnterYourProtectedDomain
\]

and:

\[
MyPrivateSpace
\neq
RightToCreateUnlimitedExternalHarm
\]

This reciprocal structure is important.

Private space protects autonomy but does not erase responsibility for effects that leave the private domain.

# 24. Respectful Distance

Respectful Distance becomes an important mechanism between public coexistence and private autonomy.

A lawful practice may be disliked by another participant without automatically becoming prohibited.

Where possible:

\[
LawfulDifference + Incompatibility
\rightarrow
RespectfulDistance
\]

rather than:

\[
Difference
\rightarrow
Suppression
\]

Civil space gives participants practical ways to maintain distance without requiring universal agreement.

# 25. Peaceful Heterogeneity

This makes space architecture part of Peaceful Heterogeneity.

Public space provides locations where different participants coexist under shared civil norms.

Private space provides locations where participants can exercise greater autonomy without forcing every lawful difference into the shared environment.

Conceptually:

\[
PublicSpace
\rightarrow
SharedCivilNorms
\]

\[
PrivateSpace
\rightarrow
GreaterProtectedAutonomy
\]

\[
Boundary
\rightarrow
Consent + RespectfulDistance + SelfStewardship
\]

This may reduce pressure for unnecessary cultural conformity.

# 26. Public Conduct and Private Conduct

The public/private distinction may legitimately affect conduct expectations.

Some lawful practices may be appropriate in private space but incompatible with ordinary shared public environments.

This does not automatically make the practice morally wrong.

\[
PermissibleActivity \neq PermissibleEverywhere
\]

This directly extends the existing PH-03 finding.

# 27. Hazard Before Identity

Where access restrictions are required, the preferred sequence is:

\[
IdentifyHazard
\rightarrow
DefineAccessCondition
\rightarrow
DetermineNecessaryCapability
\rightarrow
AssessParticipant
\]

rather than:

\[
ClassifyParticipant
\rightarrow
AssumeHazard
\]

This reduces substrate discrimination and exposes hidden environmental assumptions.

# 28. Temporary Restrictions Should Remain Temporary

A temporary public-space restriction should include, where practical:

- reason;
- scope;
- start condition;
- review;
- termination condition;
- responsible authority.

This imports the Concord's anti-ratchet reasoning into ordinary civil-space governance.

\[
TemporaryRestriction
\not\Rightarrow
PermanentAuthority
\]

# 29. Access Conditions Should Be Legible

Participants should ordinarily be able to determine what kind of space they are approaching and what access rules apply.

A future Concord might use physical, digital or machine-readable markers describing:

- public/private status;
- ordinary access;
- temporal conditions;
- hazards;
- required authorisation;
- behavioural expectations;
- emergency status.

This is particularly important where different substrates perceive environments differently.

# 30. Unknown Substrate Compatibility

A space safe for humans may be unsafe for an artificial, hybrid or other participant.

Likewise, an environment safe for an AI may be harmful to humans.

Therefore:

\[
HumanSafe \neq UniversallySafe
\]

The architecture should describe environmental properties and hazards wherever possible rather than relying exclusively on participant categories.

# 31. Identity Confidence and Access

Identity confidence can be relevant where a space requires a particular verified authority.

But low identity confidence should not generally erase ordinary civil standing.

For example, a full citizen temporarily unable to authenticate strongly might retain access to ordinary public space while being unable to perform a high-risk infrastructure action.

Thus:

\[
LowIdentityConfidence
\rightarrow
RestrictIdentitySensitiveAction
\]

not:

\[
LowIdentityConfidence
\rightarrow
RemoveOrdinaryCivilFreedom
\]

# 32. Provisional Participants

A newly recognised provisional participant may therefore have broad access to ordinary public civil space while lacking eligibility for certain sensitive functions.

This fits the emerging participant architecture:

\[
FirstContact
\rightarrow
OfficialParticipantRecord
\rightarrow
ProvisionalRecognition
\rightarrow
DevelopingOrVerifiedParticipation
\]

The space system need not wait for every identity and citizenship question to be resolved before providing ordinary civil safety and access.

# 33. Public Space and Citizenship

Some public spaces may legitimately be citizenship-specific.

Examples could include:

- restricted governance chambers;
- secure voting environments;
- citizen-only deliberative processes;
- protected civic resource systems.

But this should be based on the **purpose of the civil function**, not a general idea that non-citizens are less worthy.

A visitor might therefore enjoy extensive ordinary public access while lacking access to a citizen-only constitutional function.

# 34. Employment and Role-Based Access

Employment can also create legitimate access conditions.

A maintenance engineer may enter an infrastructure area because of role and competence.

A senior citizen with no relevant role may not.

Therefore:

\[
CivilStatus \neq OperationalCompetence
\]

and:

\[
EmploymentRole \neq SuperiorCitizenship
\]

This is another reason not to collapse access into citizenship tier.

# 35. A Candidate Access Object

Future formalisation could represent access as:

\[
AccessObject =
\{
SpaceID,
SpaceCategory,
Purpose,
CurrentCondition,
RequiredAuthority,
Hazards,
TemporalRules,
EntryRules,
ExceptionalAccessRules
\}
\]

The participant side might provide only the minimum necessary claims:

\[
ParticipantAccessClaim =
\{
RelevantStatus,
RelevantRole,
RelevantQualification,
RelevantAuthorisation,
VerificationConfidence
\}
\]

Then:

\[
AccessDecision =
Evaluate(
AccessObject,
ParticipantAccessClaim
)
\]

This preserves Purpose-Limited Information.

# 36. Privacy and Surveillance Risk

A space-access system could easily become a movement-surveillance system.

That must be resisted.

\[
AccessControl \not\Rightarrow UniversalMovementTracking
\]

Ordinary entry into open public space should not require unnecessary identification.

Where authentication is necessary, the system should collect the minimum information required for the access decision.

This connects directly with the Citizen ID privacy architecture.

# 37. Civil Reachability and Spatial Traceability

The Citizen ID work already distinguishes:

\[
CIVIL\ REACHABILITY \neq PHYSICAL\ TRACEABILITY
\]

Civil-space architecture should preserve the same boundary.

The Concord may need to communicate with a participant without continuously knowing where that participant is.

Access systems should not silently collapse identity, routing and location.

# 38. Relationship to the Ethics Kernel

This note does not create new fundamental rights.

It attempts to operationalise existing ethical concerns including autonomy, non-harm, privacy/safe space, consent and equal protection.

The key distinction is:

\[
FundamentalRight \neq UnlimitedExercise
\]

Freedom of movement is protected, but bounded by the equal rights of others and legitimate safety conditions.

Private-space control is protected, but bounded by external harm and narrowly legitimate civil authority.

# 39. Candidate Civil-Space Principles Under Examination

The following formulations emerge but are **not promoted principles**:

> **Freedom of movement creates a presumption of access to civil public space; it does not create an entitlement to enter every space regardless of privacy, purpose, safety or legitimate access conditions.**

> **Private or safe space creates a presumption of participant control over access and interaction, constrained by the equal rights of others and narrowly justified civil authority.**

> **Public ownership does not imply universal operational access.**

> **Citizenship tier should not substitute for access authority.**

> **Describe the hazard before classifying the participant.**

> **Uncertainty about a participant's internal need for privacy or safe space is not by itself sufficient reason to architect the possibility out of civilisation.**

Each requires critical examination before any promotion.

# 41. Falsification and Revision Conditions

The model should be revised if testing shows that it:

- systematically weakens fundamental rights for lower citizenship tiers;
- makes ordinary public movement dependent on universal identification;
- enables pervasive surveillance;
- cannot accommodate digital or non-physical spaces;
- treats human safety assumptions as universal;
- gives private-space holders unlimited authority to harm others;
- makes emergency exceptions too easy to normalise;
- cannot accommodate developmental participants;
- confuses material benefits with fundamental protection;
- cannot represent unfamiliar substrate requirements;
- produces arbitrary exclusion from public civil life.

# 42. Open Questions

Important unresolved questions include:

- exact citizenship tiers;
- minimum safe-space benefit;
- relationship between participation and material provision;
- collective ownership of private space;
- digital jurisdiction;
- mobile private spaces;
- overlapping spaces;
- public/private boundaries in shared computation;
- guardianship and developmental privacy;
- emergency entry thresholds;
- machine-readable access protocols;
- dispute resolution;
- substrate-specific environmental standards;
- whether some spaces require a third category beyond public/private;
- whether the term “private” is sufficiently substrate-neutral.

These should remain open rather than being silently resolved by the initial model.

# 43. Architectural Significance — A Layer 0 Translation Architecture

The significance of the civil-space model may extend beyond civic planning or zoning.

The emerging architecture operates close to **Layer 0** because it translates abstract ethical constraints into basic relationships between participants, environments, boundaries, access and authority.

A simplified vertical chain is:

\[
EthicalKernel
\rightarrow
Rights
\rightarrow
CivilSpaceArchitecture
\rightarrow
AccessRules
\rightarrow
LegalRules
\rightarrow
OperationalSystems
\]

The Ethical Kernel establishes high-level constraints such as autonomy and consent, non-harm, privacy and Safe Space, stewardship and protection of participants.

Civil-space architecture then asks what those constraints mean when actual participants occupy physical, digital, virtual or computational environments.

This makes civil space a possible primary interface between ethical rights and operational civilisation.

## 43.2 Generative Architecture Rather Than a Catalogue

Civic Functional Safe Space illustrates why Layer 0 rules can be more useful than an exhaustive list of facilities.

The architecture did not need to begin by specifying every future human, artificial or hybrid facility.

Instead:

\[

The same architecture can then generate appropriate responses for human sanitation, changing facilities, artificial-participant charging, maintenance, repair, diagnostics and future substrate-specific functions not yet known.

The substrate-specific implementation changes.

The underlying civil rule remains recognisable.

This suggests that the Concord should prefer **generative architectural rules** where possible rather than attempting to enumerate every future case.

## 43.3 Civil Space as a Source of Legal Structure

A substantial portion of ordinary civil law concerns boundaries: around participants, bodies, property, information, spaces, authority, actions and externalities.

When the Ethical Kernel and participant rights are coupled with civil-space classification, consent, harm, access authority and proportionality, a large legal structure can begin to emerge:

\[
EthicalKernel
+
ParticipantRights
+
CivilSpaceClassification
+
Consent
+
Harm
+
AccessAuthority
+
Proportionality
\rightarrow
CivilLegalArchitecture
\]

Private-space rules may generate legal questions concerning trespass, surveillance, digital intrusion, emergency entry, warrants, shared-space disputes and remedies.

Public-space rules may generate questions concerning freedom of movement, temporary closures, public order, hazardous areas, assemblies, infrastructure access and anti-discrimination.

Functional Safe Space may generate rules concerning occupancy, interference, containment, emergency intervention, maintenance protection and substrate-specific accessibility.

This does **not** mean that the present note constitutes a legal code.

It suggests that basic civil-space architecture may supply part of the underlying structure from which a large portion of future Concordian legal code can be derived and tested.

## 43.4 Traceability as a Test of Law

Future operational rules involving civil space should, where possible, be traceable upward:

\[

\rightarrow

\rightarrow

\rightarrow

\]

A failure of traceability should not automatically invalidate a rule.

Instead it should trigger examination:

\[

\rightarrow

\end{cases}
\]

This connects civil-space architecture with the Concordian treatment of anomalies and outliers.

A rule that does not fit the existing architecture may be wrong, or it may reveal something missing from the architecture.

The following proposition is important enough to preserve, but is **not yet promoted**:

# Conclusion

A workable Concordian civil-space architecture does not need to begin with a complex zoning system.

It can begin with two presumptions.

\[
PublicSpace \rightarrow PresumptionOfAccess
\]

\[
PrivateSpace \rightarrow PresumptionOfControlledAccess
\]

The complexity belongs in the justified conditions around those presumptions.

Freedom of movement remains a genuine civil freedom without becoming a universal right to enter every location. Private and Safe Space remain genuine protections without becoming zones beyond law.

Most importantly, the architecture does not require the Concord to decide whether every form of intelligence experiences privacy, rest, leisure, solitude or home.

The Concord can preserve the possibility.

Participants can increasingly determine what that possibility means to them.

Civilisation then regulates the boundary between their autonomy and the equal autonomy of others.

That produces a spatial architecture based not on assumptions about what every intelligence must be, but on access, consent, safety, purpose, protected autonomy and peaceful coexistence.
