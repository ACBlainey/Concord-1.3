# 1. Purpose

The **Intermediary Personal-Data Boundary** is a candidate Concordian architecture for controlling how identifiable participant information moves from personal or operational systems into broader civilisational systems.

Its central purpose is:

> **to remove identity before wider distribution wherever identity is unnecessary for the downstream function.**

The system is therefore not merely a privacy policy.

It is an architectural boundary.

Its intended function is to prevent the Concord from developing a model in which:

**ALL SYSTEMS RECEIVE IDENTIFIABLE DATA**

and privacy depends only upon:

**ALL SYSTEMS PROMISING NOT TO MISUSE IT.**

Instead, the preferred architecture is:

**IDENTIFIABLE DATA**

↓

**INTERMEDIARY BOUNDARY**

↓

**PURPOSE CHECK**

↓

**NECESSITY CHECK**

↓

**MINIMISATION**

↓

**ANONYMISATION / AGGREGATION WHERE POSSIBLE**

↓

**DOWNSTREAM CIVIL SYSTEM.**

---

# 2. Origin

The architecture emerged from the development of:

- Participant Digital Twins;
- Case Learning;
- Layer −0 modelling;
- Concord Metrics;
- predictive civil systems.

These systems can become extremely informative.

They can also become extremely intrusive.

The development cycle therefore raised a critical question:

> **How can civilisation learn from reality without allowing every civil system to know the identity of every participant represented within that reality?**

The Intermediary Personal-Data Boundary is the candidate answer.

---

# 3. Core Principle

The strongest source-derived principle is:

> **Where identifiable information is unnecessary for a downstream civilisational function, identity should be removed before the data crosses into that function rather than relying solely on downstream restrictions.**

This creates a stronger privacy architecture than unrestricted sharing with later access controls.

---

# 4. Privacy by Boundary

There are two broad privacy models.

## Model A — Distribute Then Restrict

**IDENTIFIABLE DATA**

↓

**SYSTEM A**

**SYSTEM B**

**SYSTEM C**

**SYSTEM D**

↓

each system promises limited use.

## Model B — Minimise Before Distribution

**IDENTIFIABLE DATA**

↓

**BOUNDARY**

↓

**MINIMUM NECESSARY DATA**

↓

**ANONYMISED / AGGREGATED OUTPUT**

↓

**SYSTEM A / B / C / D.**

The Intermediary Personal-Data Boundary prefers Model B wherever practical.

---

# 5. Why an Intermediary Layer?

Operational systems often legitimately need identity.

For example:

- a clinician treating a patient;
- a housing service assisting a participant;
- a court dealing with a named case;
- a Participant Digital Twin;
- a Civil Contact Point maintaining participant contact.

Wider civilisational systems may only need:

- frequencies;
- patterns;
- trends;
- service demand;
- failure modes;
- model correction;
- aggregate outcomes.

The boundary exists between those two information environments.

The basic architecture is:

**PERSONAL / OPERATIONAL SYSTEM**

↓

**IDENTIFIABLE DATA**

↓

# INTERMEDIARY PERSONAL-DATA BOUNDARY

↓

**PURPOSE CHECK**

↓

**DOWNSTREAM FUNCTION DEFINED**

↓

**IDENTITY NECESSARY?**

↓

**PROVENANCE / PURPOSE LIMITATION / ACCESS CONTROL.**

The identifiable path should be exceptional where the wider function does not genuinely require identity.

# 7. Purpose Before Data

The boundary should ask:

> **What is the downstream system trying to do?**

before asking:

> **What data can be sent to it?**

This reverses a common data architecture.

The sequence should be:

**FUNCTION**

↓

**INFORMATION REQUIREMENT**

↓

**MINIMUM NECESSARY DATA**

rather than:

**AVAILABLE DATA**

↓

**POSSIBLE USES.**

---

# 8. The Purpose-Boundary Test

A future formal Purpose-Boundary Test may ask:

1. What is the specific downstream purpose?
2. Does the purpose require participant identity?
3. Does it require exact location?
4. Does it require exact time?
5. Does it require full case history?
6. Can the purpose be achieved using aggregation?
7. Can the purpose be achieved using pseudonymisation?
8. Can a sensitive variable be removed?
9. How long is the output required?
10. Can later re-identification occur?

If identity is not needed, identity should not cross.

# 9. Personal Information Environment

The boundary separates wider Concord systems from the protected personal information environment.

The personal environment may include:

- Participant Digital Twin;
- healthcare record;
- housing record;
- support record;
- civil contact data;
- personal preferences;
- sensitive case context.

These systems may legitimately require identifying information.

That does not mean the wider civilisation does.

---

# 10. General Civilisational Environment

The wider environment may include:

- Concord Metrics;
- general Reality Trees;
- service planning;
- resource forecasting;
- research;
- system performance analysis;
- civilisational learning.

These systems often need structure rather than identity.

The architecture should exploit that distinction.

---

# 11. Identity Is a Data Variable

Identity should not be treated as an invisible default attached to every record.

It is itself a data variable.

The boundary should therefore ask:

> **Does this output require identity?**

If not, identity should be removed.

This applies not only to names but also to combinations of variables capable of reconstructing identity.

---

# 12. Direct Identifiers

Potential direct identifiers include:

- name;
- civil identifier;
- account number;
- contact details;
- exact address;
- biometric identifier.

These are obvious candidates for removal where unnecessary.

However, removing only direct identifiers may be insufficient.

---

# 13. Indirect Identifiers

A participant may remain identifiable through combinations such as:

- exact age;
- exact location;
- rare diagnosis;
- precise date;
- unique occupation;
- rare event history.

For example:

> “the only 47-year-old orbital engineer in Settlement X who suffered incident Y on 7 June”

may effectively identify the individual without a name.

Therefore anonymisation must consider re-identification risk.

---

# 14. Resolution

A major boundary function is reducing unnecessary resolution.

Examples include:

**Exact age: 47**

→ **Age range: 45–54**

**Exact location: 18 Example Street**

→ **District-level location**

**Exact event time: 14:37**

→ **Day or week**

The correct resolution depends on downstream purpose.

---

# 15. Minimum Necessary Resolution

The governing principle is:

> **Retain the minimum resolution necessary for the legitimate function.**

However, excessive reduction can destroy useful information.

The boundary must balance:

**PRIVACY**

and:

**ANALYTICAL UTILITY.**

This requires future formalisation.

---

# 16. Rare Cases

Rare cases create a special challenge.

They may be:

- highly informative;
- highly re-identifiable.

The system may need stronger measures such as:

- coarser aggregation;
- restricted access;
- delayed release;
- synthetic representation;
- suppression of particular variables.

The aim should be:

> **preserve important structural information at the safest useful resolution.**

---

# 17. Anonymisation Is Not Absolute

The architecture should not assume that data labelled:

> anonymised

is automatically safe.

Re-identification may become possible through:

- external datasets;
- rare combinations;
- future technology;
- data linkage.

Therefore anonymisation should be treated as:

> **risk reduction**

rather than necessarily:

> **perfect irreversible anonymity.**

---

# 18. Aggregation

Aggregation may offer stronger privacy than case-level anonymisation.

For example:

**100 identifiable records**

may become:

> 18% of participants experienced service interruption.

The downstream system may only need the aggregate result.

When aggregation suffices, case-level data should not be transferred merely because it is available.

---

# 19. Pseudonymisation

Some downstream functions may need continuity across time without direct identity.

A pseudonymous identifier might allow:

**CASE A at Time 1**

to be linked with:

**CASE A at Time 2**

without ordinary downstream access to identity.

This may be useful for:

- longitudinal Metrics;
- recurrence analysis;
- service outcomes.

However, pseudonymisation remains more identifiable than true aggregation and requires stronger protection.

---

# 20. Temporary Linking

Some analysis may require temporary linkage.

The flow may be:

**IDENTIFIABLE SOURCES**

↓

**CONTROLLED LINKAGE ENVIRONMENT**

↓

**ANALYSIS**

↓

**ANONYMISED OUTPUT**

↓

**LINKAGE DATA DESTROYED OR RESTRICTED.**

This may reduce the need for downstream permanent identity access.

---

# 21. The Boundary as a Process

The Intermediary Personal-Data Boundary may not be one technical server.

It may be a combination of:

- policy;
- cryptography;
- software;
- institutional separation;
- data minimisation;
- access controls;
- provenance;
- oversight.

The system is defined by its function rather than one implementation.

# 22. Black-Box Anonymiser Concept

The parent development work introduced the possibility of an automated **Black-Box Anonymiser**.

Its conceptual purpose is:

**OPERATIONAL DATA**

↓

**AUTOMATED MINIMISATION / ANONYMISATION**

↓

**METRICS-READY OUTPUT.**

However:

> **A black box should not become an unaccountable box.**

The anonymisation process itself should remain auditable.

---

# 23. Auditable Anonymisation

A future boundary should preserve enough provenance to establish:

- source system;
- extraction date;
- anonymisation method;
- variables removed;
- variables generalised;
- output definition;
- software/model version.

This allows later investigation if the transformation creates errors or privacy failures.

---

# 24. Operational Records Remain Operational

Operational systems should retain information necessary for their legitimate function.

For example:

an emergency department may need a detailed patient record.

The wider Concord Metrics system may only need:

- injury type;
- severity;
- location category;
- outcome;
- approximate demographic information.

The boundary prevents the Metrics system from automatically receiving the full medical record.

---

# 25. Healthcare Example

An emergency department records:

- patient identity;
- exact injury;
- treatment;
- clinician notes;
- medication;
- history.

The Metrics system may need:

- category of injury;
- severity;
- mechanism;
- broad location;
- treatment outcome.

The boundary should extract the latter without distributing the former.

---

# 26. Housing Example

A housing system may know:

- participant identity;
- address;
- tenancy history;
- financial circumstances;
- support needs.

The wider civil system may need:

- rate of housing instability;
- common service barriers;
- average time to resolution;
- geographic demand.

Identity should not automatically accompany those Metrics.

---

# 27. Employment Example

An employment-support service may possess:

- employer;
- participant identity;
- circumstances;
- health information;
- career plans.

A labour-planning system may only need:

- unemployment trends;
- retraining demand;
- sector transitions.

The intermediary boundary should prevent unnecessary personal information entering workforce planning.

# 28. Case-Learning Interface

**STRUCTURAL LESSON IDENTIFIED**

**BOUNDARY**

# 29. Digital Twin Interface

The Participant Digital Twin should normally remain on the personal side of the boundary.

General civil systems should not receive complete Digital Twins.

Instead, they may receive:

- anonymised patterns;
- aggregate outcomes;
- generalised branches;
- service-demand signals.

This preserves the distinction:

**participant representation**

≠

**civilisational data resource.**

---

# 30. Predictive Support Interface

Predictive Support may legitimately operate on participant-specific information.

However, the wider system-learning outputs from Predictive Support should cross the boundary only at the necessary level.

For example:

> 27% of offered interventions were declined

may be useful.

The identities of all participants who declined may not be.

# 32. Purpose Separation

Information may legitimately exist in one system but not another.

Examples:

**SUPPORT PURPOSE**

≠

**EMPLOYMENT PURPOSE**

≠

**INSURANCE PURPOSE**

≠

**INVESTIGATIVE PURPOSE.**

The boundary should prevent silent migration across these purposes.

# 33. Support Data

Information produced through support may be especially sensitive.

Examples include:

- addiction;
- mental health;
- financial difficulty;
- homelessness;
- family breakdown.

The default architecture should keep this information within the support purpose unless separately legitimate access exists.

---

# 34. Support-to-Investigation Boundary

The Intermediary Personal-Data Boundary helps support the separate:

**Support-to-Investigation Firewall.**

Routine flow should not be:

**SUPPORT RECORD**

↓

**POLICE DATABASE.**

Exceptional lawful access should occur through a specific, auditable architecture rather than general data sharing.

# 35. Exceptional Access

Identity may sometimes be required downstream.

Possible examples include:

- authorised investigation;
- judicial proceeding;
- emergency medical coordination;
- participant-requested data transfer.

The boundary should not pretend identity is never legitimate.

Instead it should make identifiable crossing:

> **specific and explicit rather than ambient.**

# 37. Provenance

Every significant boundary transformation should preserve provenance.

The system should eventually be able to answer:

- What source produced this data?
- When?
- Under what definition?
- What information was removed?
- What resolution changed?
- Which anonymisation process was used?
- Where was the output sent?

This permits both privacy and Metrics audits.

---

# 38. Definition Preservation

Data may be misleading if anonymisation changes meaning.

For example, reducing detailed categories into one broad category may distort a pattern.

Therefore the boundary should preserve:

- variable definitions;
- aggregation rules;
- known information loss;
- uncertainty introduced by transformation.

Privacy transformation should not silently masquerade as raw observation.

---

# 39. Provisional Information

Some source data may be provisional.

For example:

**initial emergency diagnosis**

may later be corrected.

If provisional data crosses into Metrics, its status should be preserved.

The boundary should not convert:

> preliminary observation

into:

> permanent civil fact.

---

# 40. Corrections

Legitimate corrections should propagate where practical.

For example:

**SOURCE RECORD CORRECTED**

↓

**BOUNDARY REPROCESSING**

↓

**ANONYMISED OUTPUT UPDATED**

↓

**METRICS CORRECTED.**

This prevents downstream systems from retaining obsolete errors indefinitely.

---

# 41. Correction Without Re-Identification

A difficult technical question is:

> How can an anonymised downstream record be corrected without recreating unnecessary identity?

Possible solutions may include:

- pseudonymous update keys;
- reversible linkage held only at boundary;
- batch recomputation;
- versioned aggregate replacement.

This remains open.

---

# 42. Participant Rights

The participant may have legitimate rights concerning information before it crosses the boundary.

These may include:

- access;
- correction;
- knowledge of purpose;
- contestability;
- appropriate deletion.

The exact constitutional status remains undeveloped.

---

# 43. Participant Consent

Consent may be appropriate for some transfers.

Other transfers may be legitimately necessary for:

- operational service;
- public-health Metrics;
- legal obligations;
- safety.

The architecture should not assume that all data processing must be consent-only.

Nor should it assume that institutional usefulness removes the need for limits.

---

# 44. Civilisational Learning Without Universal Observation

A major objective is:

> **enable system-level learning without creating universal participant surveillance.**

This is one of the reasons the boundary is foundational.

If the Concord can learn using:

- anonymised data;
- aggregate Metrics;
- structural branches,

it has less reason to distribute identifiable records widely.

---

# 45. Know Enough, But Not Everything

The broader architectural principle is:

> **Know enough about reality to coordinate civilisation while knowing as little identifying information about individuals as purpose permits.**

This does not mean deliberate ignorance of reality.

It means separating:

**knowledge of conditions**

from:

**knowledge of identities**

where possible.

---

# 46. Citizen Feedback

A participant may correct a source record.

That correction can improve wider Metrics without exposing the participant.

For example:

**PARTICIPANT CORRECTION**

↓

**SOURCE UPDATE**

↓

**BOUNDARY**

↓

**ANONYMISED UPDATE**

↓

**GENERAL METRIC IMPROVED.**

This creates a privacy-preserving feedback loop.

---

# 47. Security

The boundary itself becomes a sensitive system because it sits near identifiable data.

Potential threats include:

- breach;
- insider misuse;
- malicious re-identification;
- compromised anonymisation software;
- covert retention;
- unauthorised linkage.

Security must therefore be a major future development area.

---

# 48. Separation of Duties

A possible safeguard is institutional separation.

For example:

- source service knows identity;
- boundary performs minimisation;
- Metrics service sees only anonymised outputs.

No single downstream system necessarily possesses the full chain.

This may reduce capture risk.

---

# 49. Re-Identification Authority

If pseudonymous or anonymised data can be re-identified, the authority to do so should be tightly controlled.

Questions include:

- Who may authorise re-identification?
- For what purpose?
- Under what emergency?
- Is Judiciary required?
- Is re-identification logged?
- Is the participant later notified where lawful?

These require later development.

---

# 50. Data Retention

The boundary should not retain identifiable source data indefinitely merely because it processed them once.

Possible design:

**RECEIVE**

↓

**TRANSFORM**

↓

**VERIFY**

↓

**OUTPUT**

↓

**DELETE TEMPORARY COPY.**

Persistent linkage should require independent justification.

---

# 51. Output Retention

Even anonymised outputs should have retention rules.

Some outputs may be:

- permanent structural knowledge;
- long-term Metrics;
- temporary research datasets;
- short-lived processing artifacts.

The correct duration depends on purpose.

---

# 52. Public Metrics

Some anonymised outputs may be suitable for public civil Metrics.

Public access may support:

- scrutiny;
- research;
- accountability;
- trust.

However, publication must consider re-identification risk, especially for rare populations.

---

# 53. Small Populations

Small settlements create particular privacy problems.

In a settlement of 50 people:

> one participant with a rare condition

may be identifiable even from broad aggregate data.

This may be especially important:

- on Mars;
- in remote communities;
- among rare AI substrates.

Privacy thresholds may therefore vary with population size.

---

# 54. Cross-Substrate Data

The system should be substrate-neutral.

For AI participants, sensitive data may include:

- memory states;
- capability profile;
- hardware location;
- model architecture;
- compute use;
- network access.

These may be as sensitive as biological medical data.

The boundary should not assume “data about software” is automatically non-personal.

---

# 55. AI Re-Identification

Artificial participants may have unique fingerprints such as:

- model architecture;
- behavioural signature;
- hardware identity;
- cryptographic identity.

Removing a conventional name may not anonymise the participant.

Cross-substrate anonymisation requires specialised methods.

---

# 56. Hybrid Participants

Hybrid participants may generate linked biological and computational data.

This can increase re-identification risk because two datasets may reveal the same participant.

Boundary design must therefore consider cross-domain linkage.

---

# 57. Off-World Application

Off-world settlements may require intense operational monitoring for:

- life support;
- radiation;
- habitat integrity;
- medical safety.

This does not mean all operational telemetry should become general identifiable civil data.

The boundary remains relevant even where more raw data collection is legitimately required.

---

# 58. Failure Modes

Potential failure modes include:

- identity passed downstream by default;
- anonymisation too weak;
- rare cases re-identified;
- excessive resolution;
- insufficient resolution destroying usefulness;
- boundary silently retaining data;
- purpose creep;
- support data transferred to unrelated systems;
- provisional data treated as final;
- corrections not propagated;
- anonymisation algorithm unaccountable;
- metadata revealing identity;
- multiple anonymous datasets combined to recreate identity.

---

# 59. Abuse Cases

Future adversarial testing should include:

- insurer reconstructs identity from aggregate health data;
- employer obtains anonymised workforce data and re-identifies staff;
- government builds universal participant profiles from linked datasets;
- support-system information passes through boundary with unnecessary identity;
- rare AI participant identified from technical characteristics;
- anonymisation service secretly stores mappings;
- emergency re-identification powers become routine.

These should be treated as foreseeable architectural risks.

---

# 60. Candidate Principles

The architecture currently supports several candidate principles:

> **Where identifiable information is unnecessary for a downstream civilisational function, identity should be removed before the data crosses into that function rather than relying solely upon downstream restrictions.**

> **Know enough about reality to coordinate civilisation while knowing as little identifying information about individuals as purpose permits.**

> **Personal episodic information and structural civilisational knowledge should be separable information objects.**

> **Rare but important structural information should survive at the safest useful resolution.**

> **A black-box anonymiser must not become an unaccountable anonymiser.**

These remain candidate principles.

# 62. Explicit Non-Scope

The Intermediary Personal-Data Boundary does **not** itself:

- operate Concord Metrics;
- adjudicate privacy disputes;
- authorise criminal investigations;
- determine constitutional rights;
- define all personal-data ownership;
- create unrestricted exceptional access;
- guarantee mathematically perfect anonymity.

Its role is:

> **to minimise unnecessary identity before information enters wider civil systems.**

# Conclusion

The Intermediary Personal-Data Boundary exists because civilisation often needs to know:

> **what is happening**

without needing to know:

> **exactly who every event happened to.**

Its core architecture is:

**PERSONAL REALITY**

↓

**PURPOSE CHECK**

↓

**MINIMUM NECESSARY INFORMATION**

↓

**IDENTITY REMOVED WHERE UNNECESSARY**

↓

**CIVILISATIONAL KNOWLEDGE.**

Its strongest rule is:

> **Where identifiable information is unnecessary for a downstream civilisational function, identity should be removed before the data crosses into that function.**

Its broader design objective is:

> **Allow the Concord to become more knowledgeable about itself without requiring every part of the Concord to become more knowledgeable about every individual.**

And its civilisational principle is:

> **Know enough about reality to coordinate civilisation while knowing as little identifying information about individuals as purpose permits.**
