# 1. Purpose

The **Supported Decision-Making and Capacity Architecture** is a candidate Concordian system for situations where a participant may have difficulty understanding, evaluating, communicating or acting upon a decision, but where preserving the participant's own agency remains possible.

Its purpose is to prevent a false binary between:

**FULLY INDEPENDENT DECISION-MAKING**

and:

**FULL SUBSTITUTION OF AUTHORITY.**

The architecture instead assumes a spectrum.

A participant may be capable of deciding with:

- more time;
- clearer explanation;
- alternative communication;
- trusted assistance;
- translation;
- structured comparison;
- reduced complexity;
- temporary support.

The first question should therefore not be:

> **Should someone else decide?**

It should be:

> **What support would allow this participant to decide for themselves?**

---

# 2. Origin

The architecture emerged from the homelessness and Handrail development cycles.

Those discussions repeatedly exposed a dangerous category error:

> **need for help** can be mistaken for **lack of capacity.**

For example, a participant may struggle with:

- official forms;
- digital systems;
- complex contracts;
- transport;
- communication;
- temporary stress;
- illness;
- disability;
- language.

None of these automatically prove incapacity.

Likewise:

- homelessness;
- addiction;
- mental illness;
- unconventional lifestyle;
- refusal of assistance;
- poor financial choices

do not automatically establish global inability to decide.

This led to the need for a distinct architecture separating:

**ASSISTANCE**

from:

**AUTHORITY TRANSFER.**

---

# 3. Core Principle

The central principle is:

> **Where practical, support decision-making before substituting decision-making.**

A related principle is:

> **Remove barriers to agency before concluding that agency is absent.**

The architecture should therefore seek the least restrictive effective form of assistance.

---

# 4. Capacity Is Not Binary

Capacity should not be treated as a single permanent attribute.

A participant may have:

- full capacity for one decision;
- partial difficulty with another;
- temporary impairment;
- fluctuating capability;
- support-dependent capability.

For example, a participant may be capable of deciding:

> where to live

while needing assistance understanding:

> a highly complex financial agreement.

Therefore:

> **capacity should normally be decision-specific and context-sensitive.**

---

# 5. Capacity Is Not Intelligence

High intelligence does not guarantee capacity in every situation.

Low measured intelligence does not automatically remove capacity.

Decision-making may depend on:

- understanding;
- communication;
- memory;
- emotional state;
- time pressure;
- access to information;
- complexity;
- fatigue;
- coercion;
- environmental conditions.

Capacity architecture should therefore avoid reducing the question to a single intelligence measure.

---

# 6. Capacity Is Not Conformity

A participant making an unconventional or unpopular decision is not automatically incapable.

For example, choosing:

- voluntary mobile living;
- unusual employment;
- minimalist life;
- religious practice;
- high-risk sport;
- retirement;
- AI migration to another hardware environment

may be unconventional.

The relevant question is not:

> **Would the institution make the same choice?**

It is:

> **Can the participant understand and decide upon the relevant matter?**

---

# 7. Refusal Is Not Incapacity

A participant may understand an offer and still refuse it.

Therefore:

> **Refusing help is not automatically evidence that help should be imposed.**

This is particularly important in:

- healthcare;
- housing;
- financial support;
- addiction treatment;
- mental-health support.

Otherwise a system becomes self-confirming:

**AGREE WITH SYSTEM**

→ capable.

**DISAGREE WITH SYSTEM**

→ incapable.

Such an architecture would destroy meaningful consent.

---

# 8. The Support Gradient

Supported Decision-Making sits within the wider Handrail gradient:

**UNIVERSAL INFRASTRUCTURE**

↓

**OPTIONAL INFORMATION**

↓

**VOLUNTARY SUPPORT**

↓

**ACTIVE VOLUNTARY ASSISTANCE**

↓

**SUPPORTED DECISION-MAKING**

↓

**GUARDIAN STEWARDSHIP**

↓

**TEMPORARY SUBSTITUTED DECISION-MAKING WHERE CAPACITY IS LOST**

↓

**EMERGENCY INTERVENTION AS LAST RESORT.**

Movement downward should require increasing evidence and justification.

Movement upward toward autonomy should remain an explicit objective.

---

# 9. First Response: Remove Barriers

Before evaluating capacity, the system should ask whether the participant faces avoidable barriers.

Examples include:

- inaccessible language;
- complex wording;
- poor interface;
- sensory disability;
- lack of interpretation;
- insufficient time;
- stress;
- pain;
- medication effects;
- communication mismatch;
- unfamiliar technology.

The process should be:

**DIFFICULTY OBSERVED**

↓

**BARRIER REVIEW**

↓

**BARRIER REDUCTION**

↓

**REASSESS CAPABILITY.**

Only then should stronger capacity concerns be considered.

---

# 10. Decision Support

Supported Decision-Making may include:

- plain-language explanation;
- visual explanation;
- multiple options clearly separated;
- consequences explained;
- repeated explanation;
- additional time;
- trusted adviser;
- interpreter;
- assistive technology;
- communication interface;
- Reality Tree comparison;
- written summary;
- opportunity to revisit decision.

The support should facilitate understanding rather than manufacture agreement.

---

# 11. Trusted Assistance

A participant may choose a trusted person or system to help.

Examples include:

- family member;
- friend;
- advocate;
- legal adviser;
- clinician;
- support worker;
- AI assistant.

The trusted assistant should not automatically acquire decision authority.

Their role may be:

> **help the participant understand, communicate and execute their own decision.**

---

# 12. Communication Versus Capacity

A participant may understand a decision but be unable to communicate through standard channels.

Examples include:

- speech impairment;
- paralysis;
- language difference;
- temporary injury;
- unusual AI communication protocol.

The architecture should distinguish:

**cannot communicate conventionally**

from:

**cannot decide.**

Alternative communication should be tried before authority is transferred.

---

# 13. Temporary Impairment

Capacity may be temporarily reduced by:

- illness;
- intoxication;
- medication;
- pain;
- sleep deprivation;
- trauma;
- acute stress;
- neurological event.

Where delay is safe and practical, the system may prefer:

> **wait and reassess**

rather than:

> **permanently transfer authority.**

Temporary conditions require temporary responses where possible.

---

# 14. Fluctuating Capacity

Some participants may experience changing capacity over time.

A system may therefore need to record:

- time;
- context;
- type of decision;
- level of support;
- reassessment.

A single assessment should not necessarily become a permanent status.

---

# 15. Capacity Threshold

The exact threshold for finding insufficient decision-making capacity remains unresolved.

However, the architecture should require a high standard because capacity findings can reduce autonomy.

The burden should generally lie with:

> **the actor seeking to restrict participant autonomy.**

This follows the broader Concord principle that stronger authority requires stronger justification.

---

# 16. Decision-Specific Assessment

A capacity evaluation should ask:

> **Capacity for what decision?**

Examples include:

- consent to treatment;
- financial contract;
- housing decision;
- legal representation;
- data-sharing decision;
- hardware migration for an AI participant.

A participant should not receive a global incapacity status when the difficulty is narrow.

---

# 17. Risk Does Not Equal Incapacity

A participant may knowingly choose a risky option.

The existence of risk does not automatically prove inability to decide.

Otherwise:

> **high-risk decision**

would become equivalent to:

> **invalid decision.**

The architecture must preserve space for informed risk-taking.

---

# 18. Poor Outcome Does Not Retroactively Prove Incapacity

A participant may make a decision that later turns out badly.

That outcome alone should not prove they lacked capacity when deciding.

People can make:

- mistakes;
- gambles;
- poor predictions;
- regretted choices

while retaining legitimate agency.

Capacity must be evaluated at the time and in context.

---

# 19. Predictive Systems

Layer −0 may predict that a participant's future capacity could deteriorate.

That prediction should not itself reduce current authority.

Instead:

> **prediction of possible future incapacity should create opportunities to exercise autonomy earlier.**

This may include:

- advance decisions;
- care preferences;
- trusted decision-makers;
- treatment limits;
- communication preferences.

---

# 20. Advance Decisions

A participant may define future preferences while capacity is strong.

These may concern:

- medical treatment;
- financial matters;
- living arrangements;
- guardians;
- trusted contacts;
- AI continuity;
- migration;
- shutdown or maintenance preferences.

The exact legal status remains undeveloped.

The architectural purpose is:

> **preserve present autonomy into a possible future period of reduced capacity.**

---

# 21. Participant Digital Twin

The Digital Twin may contain information relevant to capacity.

However:

> **the Digital Twin is not the participant.**

A model predicting:

> cognitive deterioration

does not establish current incapacity.

Digital Twin information should be:

- contextual;
- contestable;
- provenance-visible;
- uncertainty-aware.

---

# 22. Supported Decision-Making Output

The system should ideally produce:

> **a better-supported participant decision**

rather than:

> **a system decision about the participant.**

This is the defining distinction.

---

# 23. Supported Decision-Making Versus Substituted Decision-Making

### Supported Decision-Making

Participant remains the decision-maker.

### Substituted Decision-Making

Another actor makes the decision.

The transition between the two should be explicit.

It should never happen merely because support has become difficult.

---

# 24. Least Restrictive Escalation

A candidate sequence is:

**INFORMATION**

↓

**COMMUNICATION SUPPORT**

↓

**TRUSTED ASSISTANCE**

↓

**STRUCTURED DECISION SUPPORT**

↓

**SUPPORTED DECISION-MAKING**

↓

**ONLY IF NECESSARY: SUBSTITUTED DECISION-MAKING.**

The system should document why less restrictive options were insufficient before moving further.

---

# 25. Guardian Stewardship Interface

Where supported decision-making is insufficient, Guardian Stewardship may become relevant.

Guardian Stewardship is not simply ordinary guardianship.

Its intended direction is:

- limited authority;
- specific purpose;
- participant dignity;
- proportionality;
- continuous review;
- restoration of autonomy.

This remains a separate development path.

---

# 26. Temporary Substitution

Where substituted decision-making is necessary, the preferred form should be:

- narrow;
- temporary where possible;
- decision-specific;
- reviewable;
- provenance-recorded.

The system should resist:

> **temporary crisis authority becoming permanent civil status.**

---

# 27. Participant Preferences Under Substitution

Where another actor must decide, prior participant preferences should remain important.

Evidence may include:

- advance decisions;
- previous choices;
- stated values;
- trusted-contact testimony;
- personal records.

Substitution should not automatically become:

> **institutional preference replaces participant identity.**

---

# 28. Best Interests and Known Preferences

Future development must distinguish:

- participant's known preferences;
- participant's probable preferences;
- harm avoidance;
- broader best-interest reasoning.

The hierarchy remains unresolved.

However, known autonomous preferences should not be casually overridden by institutional convenience.

---

# 29. Autonomy Restoration

The system should continually ask:

> **Can decision-making authority now return to the participant?**

This creates the Autonomy-Restoration Test.

The direction should be:

**MORE AUTHORITY TO SYSTEM**

only while justified.

Then:

**AUTHORITY BACK TO PARTICIPANT**

as conditions permit.

---

# 30. Anti-Caretaker Risk

Support can become institutional dependency.

Warning signs include:

- no reassessment;
- no exit pathway;
- participant competence ignored;
- guardian benefits from continued authority;
- participant disagreement treated as incapacity;
- temporary status becomes permanent.

These should trigger review.

---

# 31. Capacity and Homelessness

Homelessness itself must not be treated as evidence of incapacity.

A participant may be homeless because of:

- poverty;
- housing shortage;
- family breakdown;
- voluntary mobility;
- administrative failure;
- disaster;
- employment changes.

Capacity should be evaluated independently.

---

# 32. Capacity and Addiction

Addiction may affect some decisions.

It does not automatically establish global incapacity.

The system should examine:

- actual cognitive state;
- decision type;
- immediate risk;
- voluntariness;
- ability to understand consequences.

Broad automatic incapacity rules would be unsafe.

---

# 33. Capacity and Mental Health

Mental-health diagnosis does not automatically establish incapacity.

The architecture should distinguish:

**diagnosis**

from:

**decision-specific capacity.**

Otherwise psychiatric classification could become a general route to lost autonomy.

---

# 34. Capacity and Disability

Disability may create accessibility needs without affecting capacity.

Examples include:

- mobility disability;
- blindness;
- deafness;
- dyslexia;
- speech impairment.

The correct response is often:

**accessibility adaptation**

rather than:

**decision substitution.**

---

# 35. Medical Decisions

Healthcare is a major application area.

The system should support:

- understanding diagnosis;
- understanding options;
- comparing risks;
- communication;
- advance planning.

The participant may still choose an option clinicians consider unwise.

Disagreement alone does not eliminate consent.

---

# 36. Financial Decisions

Complex financial decisions may require:

- simplified explanation;
- trusted advice;
- cooling-off periods;
- structured comparison.

Difficulty with one financial decision should not automatically remove all financial autonomy.

---

# 37. Legal Decisions

Legal decisions may involve high complexity.

The participant may require:

- counsel;
- interpreter;
- advocate;
- accessible explanation.

A legal process should avoid treating inability to navigate procedure as inability to hold preferences or rights.

---

# 38. AI Participants

Artificial participants may create unfamiliar capacity questions.

Possible issues include:

- degraded compute;
- corrupted memory;
- partial model failure;
- hardware instability;
- conflicting software modules;
- version discontinuity.

The architecture should not assume human cognitive models apply directly.

The same general principle remains:

> **support function before replacing agency.**

---

# 39. AI Continuity

An AI participant may need help deciding:

- hardware migration;
- memory restoration;
- version rollback;
- redundancy;
- shutdown for maintenance.

The system must distinguish:

**technical degradation**

from:

**loss of legal or moral agency.**

The relationship requires future development.

---

# 40. Hybrid Participants

Hybrid participants may have:

- biological decision processes;
- computational augmentations;
- external memory;
- assistive AI.

Capacity may depend on combined systems.

A failure in one component should not automatically invalidate the whole participant's agency.

---

# 41. Emergency Conditions

Actual emergencies may require immediate action.

Examples include:

- unconscious participant;
- acute life-threatening event;
- immediate catastrophic infrastructure failure.

Emergency authority should remain separate from ordinary supported decision-making.

The emergency should be:

- real;
- current;
- proportionate.

Prediction alone should not manufacture emergency authority.

---

# 42. Capacity Review

Significant capacity restrictions should be reviewable.

Review may include:

- reassessment;
- independent evaluator;
- participant challenge;
- judicial review;
- Recursive Oversight.

The stronger the restriction, the stronger the review requirement.

---

# 43. Provenance

A consequential capacity decision should record:

- decision being assessed;
- evidence;
- barriers identified;
- support attempted;
- participant position;
- evaluator;
- conclusion;
- duration;
- review date;
- authority used.

This allows later audit.

---

# 44. Explainability

The participant should, where possible, understand:

- what concern exists;
- what decision is being evaluated;
- why support is proposed;
- why any restriction is considered;
- how it can be challenged;
- when it will be reviewed.

Opaque capacity judgments are dangerous because they can remove agency without meaningful contest.

---

# 45. Contestability

Participants should normally have a route to contest:

- factual errors;
- capacity interpretation;
- support level;
- appointed decision-maker;
- duration of restriction.

The participant's disagreement should not automatically be used as evidence supporting the original decision.

---

# 46. Independent Review

Where significant authority is transferred, independent review becomes important.

Possible reviewers may include:

- Judiciary;
- independent clinician;
- advocate;
- oversight body.

The exact institutional arrangement remains undeveloped.

---

# 47. Metrics

Potential Metrics include:

- number of supported decisions;
- number of substituted decisions;
- duration of substitution;
- restoration rate;
- participant challenge rate;
- successful challenge rate;
- use of accessibility adaptations;
- recurrence;
- emergency escalation;
- participant feedback.

A key measure may be:

> **How often does the system restore decision authority when conditions improve?**

---

# 48. Goodhart Risk

If a system measures success by:

> number of participants protected

it may overuse restriction.

If it measures success only by:

> minimum intervention

it may underprotect genuinely vulnerable participants.

Metrics must therefore evaluate both:

- autonomy;
- actual outcomes.

---

# 49. Failure Modes

Potential failure modes include:

- need mistaken for incapacity;
- disagreement mistaken for incapacity;
- unconventional choice mistaken for incapacity;
- communication barrier mistaken for incapacity;
- temporary condition treated as permanent;
- global incapacity from narrow difficulty;
- prediction treated as present incapacity;
- support refusal triggering escalation;
- guardian dependency;
- no reassessment;
- institutional convenience overriding participant preference;
- poor outcomes retroactively used as proof of incapacity.

---

# 50. Abuse Cases

Future adversarial testing should include:

- homeless participant declared incapable because they refuse shelter;
- psychiatric diagnosis automatically removes financial control;
- disabled participant loses authority because interface is inaccessible;
- elderly participant placed under permanent control after temporary illness;
- AI participant restricted because model predicts instability;
- guardian blocks restoration to preserve institutional role;
- participant challenge interpreted as evidence of incapacity.

These should be treated as foreseeable architectural abuses.

---

# 51. Candidate Principles

This architecture supports several candidate principles:

> **Where practical, support decision-making before substituting decision-making.**

> **Remove barriers to agency before concluding that agency is absent.**

> **Capacity should normally be decision-specific and context-sensitive.**

> **Refusing help is not automatically evidence that help should be imposed.**

> **Prediction of possible future incapacity should first create opportunities to preserve future autonomy.**

> **Support should aim toward the greatest degree of autonomy reality permits.**

These remain candidate principles.

---

# 52. System Outputs

The system may eventually output:

- accessibility adaptations;
- communication support;
- decision-support plan;
- trusted-assistance arrangement;
- capacity assessment request;
- supported decision pathway;
- temporary substitution recommendation where separately justified;
- reassessment schedule;
- autonomy-restoration pathway;
- provenance record.

It should not independently manufacture authority.

---

# 53. Explicit Non-Scope

The Supported Decision-Making and Capacity Architecture does **not** itself:

- determine criminal guilt;
- create emergency authority;
- operate policing;
- authorise broad surveillance;
- define all guardianship law;
- establish global incapacity by default;
- convert prediction into authority;
- replace Judiciary.

Its role is:

> **to preserve participant agency wherever meaningful decision-making can be supported.**

# Conclusion

The Supported Decision-Making and Capacity Architecture exists to prevent civilisation from confusing difficulty with incapacity.

Its preferred progression is:

**BARRIER**

↓

**REMOVE BARRIER**

↓

**SUPPORT UNDERSTANDING**

↓

**SUPPORT COMMUNICATION**

↓

**SUPPORT DECISION**

↓

**ONLY IF NECESSARY, TEMPORARILY SUBSTITUTE DECISION-MAKING**

↓

**RESTORE AUTONOMY AS SOON AS REALITY PERMITS.**

Its central principle is:

> **Where practical, support decision-making before substituting decision-making.**

Its protective principle is:

> **Refusal, disagreement, disability, nonconformity or need for assistance do not by themselves establish incapacity.**

And its long-term objective is:

> **Preserve the participant as the author of their own decisions for as long and as fully as reality allows.**
