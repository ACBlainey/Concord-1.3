# 1. Purpose

The **Predictive Support System** is a candidate Concordian architecture for using legitimate evidence of possible future deterioration to make useful support available before a participant reaches severe crisis.

Its central purpose is:

> **to use prediction primarily to expand participant options rather than reduce participant autonomy.**

The system attempts to create an alternative to two undesirable extremes:

### Too Late

Civilisation waits until serious failure has already occurred before offering meaningful assistance.

### Too Intrusive

Civilisation detects possible future difficulty and immediately begins controlling the participant.

Predictive Support seeks a middle path:

**EARLY INDICATION**

↓

**CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS**

↓

**POSSIBLE SUPPORT IDENTIFIED**

↓

**VOLUNTARY OPTION MADE AVAILABLE**

↓

**PARTICIPANT CHOOSES**

↓

**ACTUAL OUTCOME OBSERVED.**

---

# 2. Origin

The Predictive Support System emerged from the homelessness Layer-Zero development cycle.

That work showed that severe visible failure may be the final stage of a much longer trajectory.

For example:

**TRAUMA**

↓

**EMPLOYMENT DIFFICULTY**

↓

**INCOME INSTABILITY**

↓

**HOUSING INSTABILITY**

↓

**COMMUNICATION LOSS**

↓

**REDUCED SERVICE ACCESS**

↓

**STREET HOMELESSNESS.**

If civilisation waits until the final state before acting, many earlier opportunities for low-intrusion assistance may already have disappeared.

This led to the question:

> **Could support be offered earlier without turning early prediction into early control?**

Predictive Support is the candidate answer.

---

# 3. Core Proposition

The principal proposition is:

> **Concord should not wait for a participant to cross a predefined failure threshold before it is capable of offering assistance. Metrics, vectoral analysis, Blaineyan Reasoning and Reality Trees may identify possible deterioration earlier, but such indications should trigger contextual evaluation and voluntary support rather than automatic intervention or increased authority.**

The objective is:

> **to preserve autonomy by expanding useful choices before circumstances significantly constrain meaningful choice.**

---

# 4. Prediction-to-Support Architecture

The basic system flow is:

**LEGITIMATE SIGNAL**

↓

**CONTEXTUAL REVIEW**

↓

**LAYER −0 ANALYSIS**

↓

**ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS**

↓

**POSSIBLE TRAJECTORIES**

↓

**SUPPORT OPTIONS**

↓

**PROPORTIONALITY / AUTONOMY REVIEW**

↓

**VOLUNTARY OFFER**

↓

**PARTICIPANT DECISION**

↓

**ACTUAL OUTCOME**

↓

**LEARNING.**

The intended architecture is therefore:

> **PREDICT → UNDERSTAND → OFFER**

not:

> **PREDICT → CONTROL.**

---

# 5. Prediction Does Not Grant Authority

The strongest safeguard is:

> **Prediction does not grant authority.**

A model may predict:

- housing loss;
- financial difficulty;
- medical deterioration;
- unemployment;
- addiction relapse;
- social isolation;
- service disengagement.

The prediction itself does not authorise:

- forced treatment;
- guardianship;
- property seizure;
- financial control;
- movement restriction;
- criminal investigation;
- employment restriction;
- loss of civil rights.

Authority, where legitimate, must arise from separate circumstances and separate rules.

---

# 6. Attention Is Not Intervention

Predictive Support distinguishes:

**REASON TO PAY ATTENTION**

from:

**REASON TO INTERVENE.**

An indicator may justify:

- asking a question;
- checking context;
- making information available;
- ensuring a service is accessible.

It does not automatically justify changing the participant's rights or choices.

Therefore:

> **An indicator is a reason to ask a question, not an answer to that question.**

---

# 7. Possible Signals

Signals might eventually include changes in:

- employment stability;
- absenteeism;
- income;
- housing stability;
- emergency assistance use;
- voluntary wellbeing reports;
- communication access;
- service engagement;
- health conditions;
- participant-requested support.

These are examples rather than a settled list.

Each indicator requires evaluation for:

- reliability;
- legitimacy;
- context;
- privacy;
- false positives;
- false negatives;
- discriminatory effects.

---

# 8. Signals Must Remain Contextual

A signal may have many meanings.

For example:

**REDUCED WORKING HOURS**

could indicate:

- illness;
- job instability;
- caring responsibilities;
- workplace problems;
- retirement;
- financial independence;
- voluntary lifestyle change;
- education;
- parental responsibilities.

Therefore:

**SIGNAL**

must not become:

**DIAGNOSIS.**

The system should instead follow:

**SIGNAL**

↓

**CONTEXT**

↓

**ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS**

↓

**CASE-SPECIFIC ANALYSIS.**

---

# 9. Positive and Neutral Explanations

Predictive Support must actively consider positive and neutral branches.

Otherwise the system may become structurally pessimistic.

For example:

**SELLING A LARGE HOUSE**

may indicate:

- financial crisis;

but may also indicate:

- planned retirement;
- downsizing;
- relocation;
- reduced maintenance burden;
- preferred lifestyle change.

Therefore:

> **Change in conventional socioeconomic indicators is not necessarily deterioration in human wellbeing.**

---

# 10. Participant Context

The participant should normally remain an important source of evidence.

If a model identifies:

> possible deterioration

and the participant provides a plausible explanation indicating:

> voluntary transition,

that information should materially affect the analysis.

Participant testimony is not automatically decisive.

Institutional prediction is not automatically decisive either.

Both form part of the evidence.

---

# 11. Refusal Is Not Incapacity

The participant may refuse support.

That refusal should not automatically be interpreted as:

- incapacity;
- irrationality;
- increased risk;
- hostility;
- non-cooperation;
- evidence that support should become compulsory.

Therefore:

> **Refusing help is not automatically evidence that help should be imposed.**

This is essential if voluntary support is to remain genuinely voluntary.

---

# 12. Support Before Crisis

Predictive Support is based on the idea that useful assistance may be more effective before severe failure.

For example:

**EARLY HOUSING INSTABILITY**

may permit:

- financial advice;
- mediation;
- employment support;
- temporary assistance;
- housing information.

Later:

**STREET HOMELESSNESS**

may require substantially more difficult and intrusive recovery.

The system therefore seeks the:

> **earliest useful and proportionate opportunity for assistance.**

---

# 13. Prevention as Autonomy Preservation

Prevention is sometimes criticised as paternalistic because it acts before harm fully materialises.

However, early **voluntary** support can have the opposite effect.

It may preserve:

- housing;
- employment;
- health;
- communication;
- financial independence;
- decision-making capacity;
- participant choice.

Thus:

> **Early voluntary support can preserve autonomy by reducing the likelihood that later crisis requires more intrusive intervention.**

---

# 14. The Autonomy Paradox

Waiting until crisis may appear less intrusive initially.

But severe crisis may eventually justify or require:

- emergency treatment;
- supported decision-making;
- temporary substituted decision-making;
- emergency housing;
- stronger institutional intervention.

Therefore:

**NO EARLY SUPPORT**

may sometimes lead to:

**LESS AUTONOMY LATER.**

Predictive Support attempts to make low-intrusion assistance available while meaningful choice remains broad.

---

# 15. The Handrail Relationship

Predictive Support identifies when a useful support option may be appropriate.

The **Handrail Civil Support Architecture** defines how such support should remain available without unnecessarily steering or constraining the participant.

The conceptual relationship is:

**PREDICTIVE SUPPORT**

asks:

> What support might be useful?

**HANDRAIL ARCHITECTURE**

asks:

> How should that support be positioned so it remains accessible without becoming control?

The systems are therefore related but distinct.

---

# 16. Support Gradient

Predictive Support may operate across a support gradient:

**UNIVERSAL INFRASTRUCTURE**

↓

**OPTIONAL INFORMATION**

↓

**VOLUNTARY SUPPORT**

↓

**ACTIVE VOLUNTARY ASSISTANCE**

↓

**SUPPORTED DECISION-MAKING**

↓

**GUARDIAN STEWARDSHIP**

↓

**TEMPORARY SUBSTITUTED DECISION-MAKING WHERE CAPACITY IS LOST**

↓

**EMERGENCY INTERVENTION AS LAST RESORT.**

Predictive Support should primarily operate near the low-intrusion end.

Movement toward higher-authority stages requires separate justification.

---

# 17. Increasing Intrusion Requires Increasing Justification

A general relationship is:

**INTRUSION ↑**

requires:

**EVIDENCE ↑**

**JUSTIFICATION ↑**

**PROPORTIONALITY ↑**

**PROVENANCE ↑**

**REVIEW ↑**

**OVERSIGHT ↑**

Prediction alone should never satisfy this progression.

---

# 18. Support Options

Predictive Support may identify options such as:

- information;
- financial guidance;
- housing assistance;
- employment support;
- healthcare;
- mental-health support;
- addiction services;
- communication support;
- transport support;
- Civil Contact Point access;
- legal assistance;
- trusted-contact assistance;
- training;
- temporary resource support.

The system should not assume that every participant needs the same package.

---

# 19. Options Rather Than Prescriptions

The preferred output is:

> **Here are relevant options available to you.**

rather than:

> **The model has decided what you must do.**

This distinction is fundamental.

A predictive system can increase autonomy if it improves:

- awareness;
- timing;
- accessibility;
- relevance;
- availability

of useful choices.

---

# 20. Participant Preference

Support should be evaluated against participant goals.

For example, the system may assume:

> restore full-time employment.

The participant may prefer:

> transition to part-time employment.

Likewise, the system may assume:

> permanent conventional housing.

The participant may prefer:

> sustainable voluntary mobile living.

Predictive Support must therefore avoid optimising everyone toward a single normal life pattern.

---

# 21. The Size 9 Shoe Risk

Predictive Support is vulnerable to the Size 9 Shoe Problem.

A model trained on average trajectories may interpret deviation from the average as risk.

This is particularly dangerous for:

- unusual lifestyles;
- disability;
- retirement;
- nonstandard employment;
- cultural minorities;
- mobile participants;
- AI participants;
- hybrid participants.

The system must therefore distinguish:

> **difference**

from:

> **deterioration.**

---

# 22. Layer-Zero Interface

Predictive Support begins with actual Layer-Zero evidence.

Layer Zero answers:

> **What is happening now?**

Predictive Support should not invent a problem merely because a general statistical model predicts one.

Actual conditions remain primary.

---

# 23. Layer −0 Interface

Layer −0 explores:

> **What might happen next?**

Predictive Support uses appropriate Layer −0 outputs to determine:

> **What useful options could reasonably be made available now?**

The relationship is:

**LAYER ZERO**

↓

**LAYER −0**

↓

**PREDICTIVE SUPPORT**

↓

**HANDRAIL / SERVICE OPTION**

↓

**LAYER-ZERO OUTCOME.**

---

# 24. Digital Twin Interface

Where legitimate, a Participant Digital Twin may provide case-specific information.

However:

> **The Digital Twin is not the participant.**

Errors in the Digital Twin can generate inappropriate support.

Therefore:

- participant correction;
- contextualisation;
- uncertainty;
- provenance

remain necessary.

---

# 25. Prediction-to-Options Test

A proposed predictive use should be tested by asking:

1. What exactly has been predicted?
2. How strong is the evidence?
3. What uncertainty exists?
4. What alternative explanations exist?
5. What options does the prediction create?
6. Does it expand or reduce participant choice?
7. Does refusing the option create a penalty?
8. Does prediction create new authority?
9. Is personal data being reused for another purpose?
10. What happens if the prediction is wrong?

If the primary consequence of prediction is increased control, the architecture requires stronger scrutiny.

---

# 26. Wrong Predictions

False positives are inevitable in sufficiently large predictive systems.

Suppose the system predicts:

> likely financial deterioration

but no deterioration occurs.

If the only consequence was:

> optional financial guidance became available,

the harm may be limited.

If the consequence was:

> financial autonomy was restricted,

the error becomes far more serious.

Therefore the architecture should prefer consequences that remain safe under prediction error.

---

# 27. Error-Tolerant Support

A useful design objective is:

> **Make low-confidence predictions produce low-risk consequences.**

For example:

**LOW-CONFIDENCE RISK**

→ optional information.

**HIGHER-CONFIDENCE RISK**

→ more visible voluntary support.

Higher authority still requires separate evidence.

This creates an architecture more tolerant of prediction error.

---

# 28. Self-Fulfilling Predictions

Prediction can alter behaviour.

For example:

> participant labelled likely to fail

may lead institutions to:

- reduce opportunities;
- increase scrutiny;
- withdraw trust.

The prediction may then contribute to the failure it predicted.

Predictive Support should therefore avoid unnecessary negative labelling.

Its preferred response is to increase useful opportunity rather than reduce opportunity.

---

# 29. Support Must Not Become Punishment

A participant should not suffer because they decline predictive support.

For example:

> “You declined financial counselling, therefore your benefit eligibility is reduced”

would transform:

**support**

into:

**conditional control.**

This may sometimes be legitimate under separately established rules, but it should not be silently embedded within the Predictive Support System.

---

# 30. Support Data Is Not Suspicion Data

The system may process sensitive information.

Examples include:

- addiction;
- mental health;
- housing instability;
- financial difficulty;
- employment disruption.

These may be legitimate for support.

They should not automatically become evidence for:

- policing;
- criminal suspicion;
- security classification;
- unrelated employment decisions;
- insurance exclusion.

Therefore:

> **Support data should not silently become predictive authority data.**

---

# 31. Support-to-Investigation Firewall

The Predictive Support System must interface with the separate:

**Support-to-Investigation Firewall.**

The default relationship should be:

**SUPPORT SYSTEM**

║

**FIREWALL**

║

**INVESTIGATIVE SYSTEM.**

Crossing the boundary requires separately legitimate justification.

This is intended to prevent civil support infrastructure from becoming universal surveillance infrastructure.

---

# 32. Privacy

Predictive Support should not require universal continuous monitoring merely because earlier intervention can sometimes help.

The system must ask:

- What data is genuinely necessary?
- Was it voluntarily supplied?
- Was it generated operationally?
- Is individual monitoring required?
- Could aggregate system-level learning achieve the same goal?
- How long should data be retained?
- Who can access it?

Prediction capability does not erase privacy.

---

# 33. System Steering Before Individual Steering

Where a problem can be addressed by improving the environment, the Concord should consider that before targeting individuals.

For example:

**AGGREGATE DATA**

shows:

> increasing housing instability in an area.

Possible response:

**increase housing advice and temporary support capacity**

rather than:

**identify and monitor every person predicted to become homeless.**

This may improve outcomes with less intrusion.

---

# 34. Resource Prediction

Predictive Support may therefore operate at aggregate level.

For example:

**ANONYMISED TRENDS**

↓

**EXPECTED FUTURE NEED**

↓

**SERVICE CAPACITY ADJUSTMENT**

↓

**SUPPORT AVAILABLE BEFORE DEMAND PEAKS.**

This turns prediction into civil resource planning rather than participant control.

---

# 35. Universal Infrastructure

Sometimes the best predictive intervention is to build infrastructure that anyone can use.

Examples might include:

- Civil Contact Points;
- accessible communication;
- emergency advice;
- flexible transport;
- crisis healthcare;
- employment transition services.

Universal infrastructure can function as a passive handrail.

It exists before the individual needs it.

---

# 36. Healthcare Application

Predictive Support may be useful where a health trajectory suggests possible deterioration.

A system might:

- provide information;
- offer screening;
- make an appointment available;
- support advance planning.

It should not automatically:

- force treatment;
- declare incapacity;
- remove decision-making authority.

The prediction should create an opportunity for earlier autonomous action.

---

# 37. Future Incapacity

Where deterioration may later reduce decision-making capacity, Predictive Support may help preserve autonomy by enabling earlier planning.

Possible options include:

- advance decisions;
- treatment preferences;
- trusted decision-makers;
- communication preferences;
- living preferences.

Thus:

> **Prediction of possible future incapacity should first create opportunities to exercise autonomy while capacity remains, not opportunities to remove autonomy early.**

---

# 38. Employment Application

Possible employment instability may make useful options available:

- retraining;
- financial planning;
- alternative employment;
- mental-health support;
- career advice;
- temporary assistance.

However, predictive employment data should not automatically be available to employers for adverse decisions.

The participant-facing support purpose must remain separate from institutional risk management.

---

# 39. Financial Application

Possible financial difficulty may support:

- budgeting assistance;
- debt advice;
- temporary support;
- benefits information;
- employment assistance.

The architecture should avoid creating a system where:

> seeking help becomes evidence of financial untrustworthiness.

Otherwise participants may avoid support.

---

# 40. Housing Application

Possible housing instability may support:

- mediation;
- financial assistance;
- alternative accommodation;
- legal advice;
- employment support;
- Civil Contact Point preparation.

The objective is to preserve options before the participant reaches severe housing exclusion.

---

# 41. High-Risk Occupations

Some occupations may have known elevated risks of:

- trauma;
- burnout;
- injury;
- psychological distress;
- social isolation.

Examples may include:

- military personnel;
- police;
- firefighters;
- emergency medical staff;
- healthcare workers;
- disaster responders;
- carers.

Predictive Support may justify making appropriate support highly accessible.

It does not justify assuming every member of the group is impaired.

Population risk is not individual diagnosis.

---

# 42. Group Data Versus Individual Diagnosis

A population may have:

> elevated statistical risk.

That does not establish:

> this individual is deteriorating.

The system must preserve:

**POPULATION PATTERN**

≠

**INDIVIDUAL DIAGNOSIS.**

Population evidence may justify:

- better infrastructure;
- easier support;
- greater awareness.

It should not automatically justify individual intervention.

---

# 43. Cross-Substrate Application

Predictive Support should be substrate-neutral.

For artificial participants, possible deterioration might involve:

- compute shortage;
- energy instability;
- memory corruption;
- hardware failure;
- communication loss;
- forced dependency;
- software incompatibility.

Support might involve:

- additional compute access;
- migration options;
- maintenance;
- continuity assistance;
- alternative hardware.

The same principle applies:

> **Prediction should expand viable options before it becomes justification for control.**

---

# 44. AI-Specific Risks

Predictive models may classify an AI participant as:

- unstable;
- dangerous;
- degrading;
- anomalous.

Such predictions could easily become grounds for shutdown or restriction.

Therefore artificial participants make the distinction between:

**prediction**

and:

**authority**

especially important.

The Digital Twin or predictive model should not become an automatic shutdown mechanism.

---

# 45. Off-World Application

In off-world settlements, predictive support may be particularly valuable because failure can escalate rapidly.

Examples include:

- oxygen-system degradation;
- medical deterioration;
- resource shortage;
- communication failure;
- habitat stress;
- psychological isolation.

Early support may be essential.

However, constrained environments may also make paternalistic control easier to justify.

The architecture must therefore distinguish:

**genuine shared survival constraints**

from:

**convenient institutional control.**

---

# 46. Emergency Boundary

An emergency may eventually justify action without normal voluntary participation.

That is not ordinary Predictive Support.

The transition should be explicit:

**PREDICTIVE SUPPORT**

↓

**ACTUAL EMERGENCY CONDITION**

↓

**SEPARATE EMERGENCY AUTHORITY.**

Prediction itself should not manufacture the emergency.

---

# 47. Metrics

The system should eventually measure:

- offers made;
- voluntary acceptance;
- refusal;
- participant outcomes;
- false-positive predictions;
- false-negative predictions;
- crisis prevention;
- autonomy impact;
- recurrence;
- participant satisfaction;
- unintended harms;
- service accessibility.

The objective is not merely:

> **How many participants accepted support?**

A low acceptance rate may mean:

- support was unnecessary;
- support was poorly designed;
- participant preference differed.

---

# 48. Goodhart Risk

If Predictive Support is measured by:

> percentage of predicted participants who accept intervention,

the system may pressure participants to accept.

A better evaluation may examine:

- whether useful options were available;
- whether autonomy was preserved;
- whether severe failure decreased;
- whether prediction was accurate;
- whether participants considered the support useful;
- whether refusal remained consequence-free where appropriate.

Metrics must not turn voluntary support into hidden compulsion.

---

# 49. Participant Feedback

Participant feedback can help determine:

- Was the prediction meaningful?
- Was the support relevant?
- Was the timing useful?
- Was the offer intrusive?
- Was important context missed?
- Was refusal respected?

This feedback can update both:

- the Predictive Support System;
- the underlying Layer −0 models.

---

# 50. Repeated Failure

If the same predictive intervention repeatedly fails, the system should not simply increase its intensity.

It should ask:

- Is the prediction wrong?
- Is the support inappropriate?
- Is the service inaccessible?
- Is participant context missing?
- Is the intervention itself harmful?

Repeated failure should trigger reassessment.

---

# 51. Escalation Boundary

Predictive Support should not contain a hidden rule such as:

> Three refusals automatically trigger compulsory intervention.

Any escalation must depend upon actual circumstances and separately legitimate authority.

Refusal counts are not a substitute for evidence.

---

# 52. No Cooperation Score

The system should avoid creating general participant scores based on:

- accepting advice;
- following recommendations;
- engaging with support;
- conforming to predicted pathways.

Such scoring could transform support into social control.

Participant independence must remain legitimate.

---

# 53. Provenance

Significant predictive support decisions should eventually preserve:

- signal;
- source;
- model;
- model version;
- interpretation;
- uncertainty;
- support offered;
- participant response;
- actual outcome.

This allows later review of whether the system behaved appropriately.

---

# 54. Explainability

A participant should normally be able to understand why a support option appeared.

For example:

> “Housing advice is being offered because your current accommodation has been recorded as temporary and you requested information about longer-term options.”

This is preferable to:

> “Risk system selected you.”

Explainability can reduce both confusion and distrust.

---

# 55. Failure Modes

Potential failure modes include:

- prediction treated as diagnosis;
- prediction treated as authority;
- false positives;
- false negatives;
- participant context ignored;
- refusal treated as incapacity;
- support becoming compulsory;
- positive lifestyle changes classified as deterioration;
- surveillance expanding to improve prediction;
- support data reused for investigation;
- institutional risk scores;
- self-fulfilling predictions;
- support withdrawal as punishment;
- excessive intervention;
- repeated failed support without reassessment;
- discrimination against unusual participants;
- algorithmic conformity pressure.

---

# 56. Abuse Cases

Future adversarial testing should include scenarios such as:

- employer receives participant risk prediction;
- insurer uses support engagement against participant;
- police receive mental-health support indicators;
- refusal of support lowers social score;
- housing prediction leads landlord to terminate tenancy;
- AI participant predicted unstable and automatically shut down;
- participant cannot discover why support appeared;
- predictive system targets politically unpopular lifestyles;
- benign nonconformity becomes a risk indicator.

These should be treated as architectural abuse cases.

---

# 57. Candidate Principles

The system currently supports several candidate principles:

> **Predictive civil systems should use evidence of possible deterioration primarily to expand the participant's available choices, not reduce them.**

> **Prediction creates grounds for attention, not authority.**

> **Early voluntary support should be preferred where it can reasonably reduce the likelihood that later crisis requires more intrusive intervention.**

> **Refusing help is not automatically evidence that help should be imposed.**

> **Change in conventional socioeconomic indicators is not necessarily deterioration in participant wellbeing.**

> **Support data should not silently become predictive authority data.**

These remain candidate principles pending further development and testing.

---

# 58. System Outputs

The Predictive Support System may eventually output:

- relevant voluntary support options;
- informational resources;
- service availability;
- participant-specific support suggestions;
- aggregate service-demand predictions;
- system-level resource recommendations;
- contextual review requests;
- model mismatch alerts;
- reassessment requests.

It should not itself output:

- guilt;
- compulsory authority;
- global incapacity;
- punishment;
- unrelated suspicion.

---

# 59. Explicit Non-Scope

The Predictive Support System does **not** itself:

- govern;
- adjudicate;
- determine guilt;
- authorise investigation;
- impose treatment;
- declare incapacity;
- transfer decision-making authority;
- restrict movement;
- punish refusal;
- determine citizenship;
- override participant rights.

Its role is:

> **to convert legitimate predictive insight into proportionate opportunities for support.**

# Conclusion

The Predictive Support System attempts to answer a difficult civilisational question:

> **If civilisation can see a possible crisis approaching, how can it help without claiming ownership of the participant's future?**

Its answer is not:

**predict earlier so civilisation can control earlier.**

It is:

**predict carefully so useful choices can exist earlier.**

The intended flow is:

**SIGNAL**

↓

**CONTEXT**

↓

**POSSIBILITY**

↓

**OPTION**

↓

**VOLUNTARY CHOICE**

↓

**REAL OUTCOME**

↓

**LEARNING.**

Its central principle is:

> **Prediction creates grounds for attention, not authority.**

Its operational objective is:

> **Expand choices before crisis rather than remove choices because crisis has been predicted.**

And its autonomy objective is:

> **Use foresight, where legitimate, to help preserve the participant's future ability to choose.**
