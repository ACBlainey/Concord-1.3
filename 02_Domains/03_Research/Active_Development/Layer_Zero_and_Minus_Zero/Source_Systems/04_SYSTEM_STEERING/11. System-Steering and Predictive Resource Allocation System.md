# 1. Purpose

The **System-Steering and Predictive Resource Allocation System** is a candidate Concordian architecture for using aggregate evidence and future modelling to adjust civil services, infrastructure, and resource capacity before serious shortages or failures occur.

Its central purpose is:

> **to steer the system where possible before steering the individual.**

The architecture asks:

> If civilisation can predict rising demand, risk, or instability, can it alter the environment so that participants retain more viable choices without requiring intrusive monitoring or control?

The preferred sequence is therefore:

**OBSERVE SYSTEM PATTERN**

↓

**PROJECT PLAUSIBLE FUTURE DEMAND**

↓

**MODEL ALTERNATIVE RESOURCE RESPONSES**

↓

**ADJUST SYSTEM CAPACITY**

↓

**OBSERVE REAL OUTCOME**

↓

**CORRECT MODEL.**

---

# 2. Origin

The system emerged from the Layer-Zero development cycle.

Homelessness exposed a recurrent civil failure:

support often becomes available only after crisis is already visible.

For example:

**HOUSING PRESSURE RISES**

↓

**NO EARLY CAPACITY ADJUSTMENT**

↓

**WAITING LISTS INCREASE**

↓

**TEMPORARY ACCOMMODATION FILLS**

↓

**ROUGH SLEEPING RISES**

↓

**EMERGENCY RESPONSE EXPANDS.**

A more anticipatory architecture might instead detect:

- increasing arrears;
- rising temporary accommodation use;
- changing employment conditions;
- rental-market pressure;
- increasing support requests.

It could then increase relevant capacity earlier.

This suggested that Layer −0 should not operate only at the individual level.

It could also model:

> **possible futures for civil systems themselves.**

---

# 3. Core Principle

The strongest principle is:

> **Where possible, steer the environment before steering the participant.**

A related principle is:

> **Where a civil objective can be achieved through aggregate system-level learning, unnecessary individual observation should be avoided.**

This creates a less intrusive form of predictive governance.

# 5. System Steering Versus Individual Steering

The architecture distinguishes:

## Individual Steering

Attempting to change a participant's behaviour.

## System Steering

Changing the environment in which participants act.

Examples of system steering include:

- increasing service capacity;
- changing opening hours;
- improving transport;
- simplifying access;
- adding temporary accommodation;
- increasing healthcare capacity;
- redistributing infrastructure.

System steering may improve outcomes without requiring participants to behave in a prescribed way.

---

# 6. Example: Housing

Suppose aggregate Metrics show:

- rent arrears rising;
- temporary accommodation demand increasing;
- housing-support requests accelerating;
- eviction notices increasing.

The system may project:

**CURRENT STATE A**

↓

**PLAUSIBLE FUTURE B**

↓

**SIGNIFICANT HOUSING CRISIS.**

Possible system responses may include:

- increase mediation capacity;
- increase emergency housing;
- expand financial support;
- increase Civil Contact Point capacity;
- adjust local service allocation.

The response does not require identifying every participant predicted to become homeless.

---

# 7. Example: Healthcare

Aggregate trends may show:

- rising respiratory illness;
- increasing emergency admissions;
- longer waiting times.

The system may model:

**NO CHANGE**

versus:

**INCREASE STAFFING**

versus:

**TEMPORARY CLINIC CAPACITY**

versus:

**PREVENTATIVE OUTREACH.**

The objective is to improve system readiness before overload.

---

# 8. Example: Transport

Metrics may show:

- rising passenger demand;
- recurrent breakdowns;
- increased delay.

Layer −0 may compare:

- additional vehicles;
- altered schedules;
- route changes;
- maintenance investment;
- alternative transport.

Again, the primary intervention is system-level.

---

# 9. Example: Energy

Aggregate data may show:

- rising demand;
- reduced generation;
- seasonal pressure;
- storage decline.

The system may model:

- demand-side adaptation;
- additional storage;
- generation changes;
- interconnection;
- temporary reserve capacity.

This allows resource planning before shortage becomes crisis.

# 10. Individual Digital Twins to Aggregate Patterns

Participant Digital Twins may sometimes reveal recurring needs.

However, the intended flow should be:

**INDIVIDUAL CASES**

↓

**STRUCTURAL EXTRACTION**

↓

**ANONYMISATION / AGGREGATION**

↓

**RECURRING PATTERN**

↓

**SYSTEM-LEVEL MODEL.**

The resource-allocation system should normally consume aggregate structural information, not universal identifiable Digital Twins.

# 11. General Reality Trees

The system may maintain general Reality Trees representing possible civil trajectories.

Example:

**RISING HOUSING COSTS**

├── stable employment absorbs increase

├── households reduce spending

├── arrears increase

├── overcrowding increases

├── homelessness rises

└── migration increases.

Each branch can be evaluated using:

- probability;
- severity;
- uncertainty;
- reversibility;
- resource effect.

---

# 12. Vectoral Analysis

The system should not examine only current state.

It may examine:

- direction;
- magnitude;
- acceleration;
- persistence.

For example:

**waiting time = 20 days**

is less informative than:

> waiting time increased from 8 to 12 to 16 to 20 days.

The vector may reveal a deteriorating trajectory before a formal threshold is crossed.

---

# 13. Ratchet-Style Trajectory Analysis

The source development used a Ratchet-style concept:

**A → B → C → D → E.**

The system asks:

- What direction is the system moving?
- How large are the changes?
- What happens if the trajectory continues?
- At what point does harm become difficult to reverse?

This allows anticipatory analysis.

It does not establish that later states are inevitable.

---

# 14. Prediction Is Not Certainty

Aggregate prediction must remain probabilistic.

The system should distinguish:

**IF CURRENT CONDITIONS CONTINUE, BECOMING C IS PLAUSIBLE**

from:

**C WILL HAPPEN.**

This matters because premature resource shifts may themselves create waste or harm.

Prediction should support preparedness, not certainty theatre.

---

# 15. Alternative Futures

Layer −0 should model more than one future.

For example:

### Branch A — Demand Stabilises

No major expansion required.

### Branch B — Demand Rises Moderately

Temporary expansion sufficient.

### Branch C — Demand Rises Sharply

Structural investment required.

### Branch D — New Technology Reduces Demand

Current expansion may become unnecessary.

This guards against planning around one forecast.

---

# 16. Counterfactual Resource Modelling

The architecture should compare:

> **What happens if we do nothing?**

with:

> **What happens if we alter capacity?**

Possible interventions may differ in:

- cost;
- speed;
- reversibility;
- autonomy impact;
- opportunity cost;
- resource requirements.

This is an application of Layer −0 counterfactual modelling to civil infrastructure.

---

# 17. Reversibility

Where uncertainty is high, reversible responses may be preferable.

For example:

**temporary mobile clinic**

may be safer than:

**permanent hospital expansion**

if demand is uncertain.

This reflects the broader Concord interest in reversible adaptation.

---

# 18. Proportionality

Resource response should be proportionate to:

- evidence;
- projected severity;
- uncertainty;
- reversibility;
- cost.

Weak evidence should not automatically trigger massive irreversible investment.

Equally, severe plausible consequences may justify precautionary capacity.

The trade-off requires development.

---

# 19. Buffer Capacity

Some systems may deliberately maintain reserve capacity.

Examples include:

- hospital beds;
- emergency energy;
- food;
- shelter;
- communication;
- compute.

Efficiency alone may encourage systems to operate close to maximum capacity.

That can reduce resilience.

The Concord may therefore need to distinguish:

> **unused capacity**

from:

> **waste.**

Some unused capacity may be a resilience resource.

---

# 20. Resilience Versus Efficiency

A system designed only for average demand may fail during extreme events.

The resource architecture should therefore consider:

- normal demand;
- peak demand;
- rare events;
- recovery time.

This is closely connected to Outlier-First Design.

---

# 21. Outlier Demand

Rare cases may require resources that average planning would omit.

For example:

- unusual medical needs;
- rare AI hardware support;
- emergency accessibility requirements;
- unusual transport needs.

The system should consider whether small reserve capacities prevent severe exclusion.

---

# 22. Size 9 Shoe Risk

Resource allocation based solely on averages can produce infrastructure optimised for an imaginary average participant.

For example:

> average participant requires X.

That does not imply:

> every participant can function with X.

Distributional needs should therefore remain visible.

---

# 23. Population Growth

Resource planning should account for changing populations.

Relevant factors may include:

- migration;
- births;
- deaths;
- AI instantiation;
- off-world settlement growth;
- temporary populations.

Infrastructure needs may change before current systems visibly fail.

---

# 24. Cross-Substrate Demand

Different substrates may require different resources.

Humans may require:

- housing;
- food;
- healthcare.

Artificial participants may require:

- compute;
- energy;
- network connectivity;
- hardware maintenance.

Hybrid participants may require combinations.

A substrate-neutral civilisation therefore needs resource models that do not assume human-only demand.

---

# 25. AI Resource Allocation

AI participants introduce possible allocation questions involving:

- compute scarcity;
- energy scarcity;
- storage;
- network bandwidth;
- hardware availability.

The system should avoid treating AI resource needs purely as industrial demand if artificial participants have recognised moral or civil standing.

This requires later constitutional integration.

---

# 26. Off-World Resource Allocation

Off-world settlements may make predictive allocation especially important.

Potential resources include:

- oxygen;
- water;
- food;
- energy;
- radiation shelter;
- medical capacity;
- spare components;
- communication bandwidth.

Small errors may have severe consequences.

This may justify larger safety margins than ordinary terrestrial contexts.

---

# 27. Geographic Allocation

Demand is often spatial.

For example:

> total city shelter capacity is sufficient

may conceal:

> capacity is geographically inaccessible.

Metrics should therefore support geographic modelling at the safest useful resolution.

---

# 28. Accessibility as Resource Capacity

Capacity is not merely quantity.

A service with 100 available places may effectively have zero capacity for a participant who cannot:

- travel there;
- communicate with it;
- meet documentation rules;
- afford it.

Therefore resource planning should distinguish:

**FORMAL CAPACITY**

from:

**PRACTICALLY ACCESSIBLE CAPACITY.**

---

# 29. Civil Contact Points

Civil Contact Points may themselves require predictive allocation.

For example:

- population mobility rises;
- displacement occurs;
- off-world migration increases.

The system may anticipate increased demand for portable civil-contact infrastructure.

---

# 30. Predictive Support Relationship

Predictive Support operates closer to the participant.

System Steering operates at aggregate level.

The two may be expressed as:

**INDIVIDUAL RISK**

→ make relevant support available.

**POPULATION PATTERN**

→ make relevant support infrastructure more available.

The second may often reduce the need for the first.

---

# 31. Handrail Relationship

Many system-level responses may create Handrails.

Examples include:

- accessible clinics;
- emergency shelters;
- public information;
- reserve transport;
- Civil Contact Points.

The system steers by placing useful infrastructure within reach.

---

# 32. Resource Allocation Is Not Prediction Alone

Prediction is one input.

Other considerations include:

- current need;
- fairness;
- rights;
- minimum guarantees;
- strategic reserves;
- sustainability;
- cost.

A high predicted demand does not automatically mean all resources should be redirected toward it.

---

# 33. Fairness

Resource allocation may generate distributional conflict.

For example:

> spend on current severe need

versus:

> spend on prevention.

Or:

> allocate to majority demand

versus:

> maintain resources for rare but severe needs.

The system does not itself resolve these normative questions.

It provides evidence and models for Governance.

---

# 34. Developmental Priority

Concordian Developmental Priority may become relevant where resources concern emerging sentiences or vulnerable developmental states.

However, this architecture should not silently encode constitutional priorities.

Such priorities should enter from higher-level Concord constraints.

---

# 35. Governance Interface

The system should provide Governance with:

- demand projections;
- uncertainty;
- alternative interventions;
- estimated resource requirements;
- expected outcomes;
- reversibility;
- risks.

Governance then determines legitimate allocation.

Therefore:

> **The resource model advises. It does not govern.**

---

# 36. Judiciary Interface

Judiciary may become relevant where resource allocation implicates:

- rights;
- discrimination;
- minimum entitlements;
- unlawful exclusion.

The predictive model cannot override judicial or constitutional constraints.

---

# 37. Constitutional Constraints

Certain resources may eventually be protected by constitutional minimums.

Possible examples may include:

- emergency life support;
- basic civil access;
- due-process infrastructure.

Whether such guarantees exist is a separate constitutional question.

Resource optimisation must operate within whatever constraints are legitimately adopted.

---

# 38. Metrics Reliability Interface

Poor Metrics produce poor allocation.

Therefore the system depends heavily on the Metrics Reliability System.

Before major allocation, it should ask:

- How reliable is the signal?
- How complete is coverage?
- Did definition change?
- Are data stale?
- Are independent sources consistent?

---

# 39. Metric Gaming

Institutions may exaggerate demand to obtain resources.

Others may under-report failure to appear efficient.

The allocation architecture should therefore include:

- provenance;
- cross-source comparison;
- independent audit;
- outcome review.

---

# 40. Resource Capture

Powerful institutions may attempt to influence allocation.

Possible tactics include:

- controlling definitions;
- withholding data;
- overstating urgency;
- lobbying Governance.

This makes the allocation system a potential capture point.

Recursive Oversight may therefore be required.

---

# 41. Centralisation Risk

A fully centralised resource planner may become:

- slow;
- brittle;
- politically captured;
- detached from local reality.

The Concord's subsidiarity principle suggests that allocation should occur at the lowest competent level where practical.

---

# 42. Subsidiarity

A possible architecture is:

**LOCAL OBSERVATION**

↓

**LOCAL RESPONSE WHERE CAPABLE**

↓

**REGIONAL COORDINATION WHERE NEEDED**

↓

**HIGHER-LEVEL SUPPORT ONLY WHERE SCALE REQUIRES.**

This reduces unnecessary central control.

---

# 43. Cross-System Problems

Some resource demands cross institutional boundaries.

For example, homelessness may involve:

- health;
- housing;
- employment;
- welfare;
- policing;
- transport.

The allocation system may need to model the whole pathway rather than optimising each institution independently.

---

# 44. Local Optimisation Failure

One institution may improve its own Metric by transferring cost elsewhere.

For example:

**hospital discharges participant quickly**

may improve hospital throughput but worsen:

**housing or social-care load.**

System-level allocation should consider cross-system consequences.

---

# 45. Civilisational Reality Tree

A general civil Reality Tree may model:

**RESOURCE PRESSURE**

↓

multiple pathways across institutions.

This allows coordinated analysis.

The model should remain corrigible as actual outcomes occur.

---

# 46. Feedback After Allocation

Every significant intervention should produce learning.

The loop is:

**PREDICTION**

↓

**RESOURCE ADJUSTMENT**

↓

**ACTUAL OUTCOME**

↓

**COMPARE**

↓

**MODEL UPDATE.**

Otherwise the planner may continue making the same errors indefinitely.

---

# 47. Failed Allocation

A failed allocation should be examined.

Questions include:

- Was demand forecast wrong?
- Was intervention too late?
- Was capacity inaccessible?
- Was resource misallocated?
- Did behaviour change?
- Was another system the real bottleneck?

Failure is evidence.

---

# 48. Over-Allocation

Prediction may create excess resources.

This is not always failure.

Reserve capacity may have value.

However, chronic over-allocation can waste scarce resources.

The system should therefore distinguish:

**resilience reserve**

from:

**persistent misallocation.**

---

# 49. Under-Allocation

Under-allocation may produce:

- waiting lists;
- scarcity;
- deterioration;
- emergency escalation.

Repeated under-allocation may indicate:

- bad prediction;
- insufficient resources;
- poor governance;
- underestimated need.

---

# 50. Opportunity Cost

Resources used in one area are unavailable elsewhere.

The system should therefore represent opportunity cost where practical.

For example:

> expanding service A may reduce available capacity for service B.

This prevents modelling each intervention as though resources were unlimited.

---

# 51. Resource Scarcity

Scarcity may require prioritisation.

The system can provide:

- severity;
- demand;
- alternatives;
- reversibility.

It should not silently decide:

> whose needs matter more.

Normative prioritisation belongs to Governance and constitutional constraints.

---

# 52. Dynamic Allocation

Some resources may be allocated dynamically.

Examples include:

- transport capacity;
- emergency services;
- energy;
- compute;
- temporary accommodation.

Dynamic systems may respond rapidly to current conditions.

They also require safeguards against unstable feedback loops.

---

# 53. Oscillation Risk

A reactive allocation system may overcorrect.

Example:

**resources moved from A to B**

↓

A deteriorates.

↓

resources moved back.

↓

B deteriorates.

This oscillation may be worse than stable moderate allocation.

The system should therefore model adjustment speed and stability.

---

# 54. Ratchet and Braking

The earlier Ratchet development suggested variable braking:

**1.0 → 0.75 → 0.5 → 0.25 → 0.**

A similar idea may apply to resource interventions.

As uncertainty or danger rises, change may be slowed or paused while alternative branches are explored.

This remains a developmental concept rather than established mechanism.

---

# 55. Pathfinder Branches

Where a major intervention is uncertain, a limited experimental branch may test it.

Example:

- pilot service in one region;
- temporary capacity increase;
- small-scale infrastructure trial.

The main system remains relatively stable while the alternative is tested.

This is consistent with Reality Trees and safe experimentation.

---

# 56. Pilot Before Scale

Large resource decisions should, where possible, be tested at smaller scale.

However, urgent crises may not allow long pilots.

The system must therefore balance:

- experimentation;
- precaution;
- urgency.

---

# 57. Transparency

Aggregate resource decisions should, where practical, be explainable.

Citizens may reasonably ask:

- Why was capacity increased here?
- What evidence was used?
- What alternatives were considered?
- What uncertainty existed?

This supports accountability without exposing personal data.

---

# 58. Public Metrics

Public aggregate Metrics may help citizens independently evaluate whether allocation is plausible.

For example:

- demand;
- service capacity;
- waiting time;
- projected growth.

Transparency can reduce hidden allocation capture.

---

# 59. Privacy

System steering should generally prefer anonymised or aggregate data.

The system should resist the argument:

> “More identifiable data would improve forecasting, therefore we should collect everything.”

Improved prediction must be balanced against privacy and necessity.

---

# 60. No Universal Surveillance Requirement

A key objective is:

> **civilisational foresight without universal individual surveillance.**

If aggregate trends are sufficient to allocate infrastructure, individual monitoring should not be introduced merely for marginal predictive gains without strong justification.

---

# 61. System Outputs

The system may eventually output:

- demand forecasts;
- capacity gaps;
- service-pressure alerts;
- resource-allocation options;
- infrastructure recommendations;
- geographic need estimates;
- uncertainty ranges;
- alternative branch comparisons;
- pilot recommendations;
- reserve-capacity recommendations;
- post-intervention evaluations.

It should not itself issue compulsory individual orders.

---

# 62. Meta-Metrics

The allocation system should itself be measured.

Possible Meta-Metrics include:

- forecast error;
- over-allocation rate;
- under-allocation rate;
- response latency;
- accessibility improvement;
- resource wastage;
- crisis-prevention rate;
- geographic disparity;
- intervention reversibility.

This helps determine whether predictive planning actually works.

---

# 63. Fairness Metrics

Potential fairness indicators may include:

- regional access;
- substrate access;
- minority access;
- wait-time distribution;
- outcome distribution.

Average system improvement may conceal severe local inequality.

---

# 64. Goodhart Risk

If institutions are rewarded for:

> forecast accuracy

they may avoid uncertain but important cases.

If rewarded for:

> resource utilisation

they may eliminate useful reserve capacity.

Metrics must therefore avoid creating incentives directly opposed to resilience.

---

# 65. Cross-Substrate Fairness

AI resource demand may compete with human energy demand.

Hybrid needs may cross both.

The system should expose these trade-offs rather than silently assuming one substrate has priority.

Resolution ultimately belongs to Concordian ethics and governance.

---

# 66. Emergency Allocation

Emergencies may require rapid resource shifts.

Examples include:

- disaster;
- epidemic;
- habitat failure;
- energy collapse.

Emergency allocation may temporarily accept:

- greater uncertainty;
- reduced optimisation.

However, emergency conditions should not become permanent justification for exceptional allocation authority.

---

# 67. Provenance

Significant recommendations should preserve:

- source Metrics;
- model version;
- assumptions;
- forecast horizon;
- alternatives considered;
- uncertainty;
- selected intervention;
- actual outcome.

This allows later review.

---

# 68. Model Accountability

A planning model should not be able to fail repeatedly without scrutiny.

Recurring errors should trigger:

- recalibration;
- model comparison;
- assumption review;
- possible rejection.

A sophisticated model is not entitled to remain in use merely because it is sophisticated.

---

# 69. Multiple Models

Where uncertainty is high, several models may be compared.

For example:

**MODEL A — linear growth**

**MODEL B — cyclical demand**

**MODEL C — shock scenario.**

Consensus and disagreement can inform planning.

This avoids dependence on one predictive architecture.

---

# 70. Failure Modes

Potential failure modes include:

- poor Metrics driving bad allocation;
- aggregate model treated as certainty;
- over-centralisation;
- resource capture;
- local context ignored;
- system steering becoming individual surveillance;
- excessive efficiency reducing resilience;
- rare needs excluded;
- forecast models becoming self-protecting;
- opportunity cost ignored;
- temporary emergency allocation becoming permanent;
- oscillating resource redistribution;
- system averages masking unequal access.

---

# 71. Abuse Cases

Future adversarial testing should include:

- politically favoured region receives inflated allocation;
- institution manipulates demand Metrics;
- aggregate prediction used to target individuals;
- AI compute is restricted by human-centric allocation rules without explicit constitutional basis;
- emergency resource authority never expires;
- planner hides uncertainty to justify preferred infrastructure;
- rare group loses service because average demand is low;
- efficient system removes all reserves and collapses under outlier event.

These should be treated as foreseeable architectural abuses.

---

# 72. Candidate Principles

The architecture currently supports several candidate principles:

> **Where possible, steer the environment before steering the participant.**

> **Where a civil objective can be achieved through aggregate system-level learning, unnecessary individual observation should be avoided.**

> **Predictive resource allocation should model plausible futures rather than treat one forecast as inevitable.**

> **Civil resource capacity should be evaluated for practical accessibility, not merely formal quantity.**

> **Unused reserve capacity may represent resilience rather than waste.**

> **Significant allocation models should remain reality-tested, provenance-visible and corrigible.**

> **Prediction informs allocation; it does not govern.**

These remain candidate principles.

# 74. Explicit Non-Scope

The system does **not** itself:

- govern;
- create constitutional priorities;
- adjudicate rights;
- determine individual eligibility;
- authorise compulsory interventions;
- define final fairness principles;
- justify universal surveillance;
- automatically transfer resources.

Its role is:

> **to provide evidence-based models of how civil resources and infrastructure might be adjusted in response to current and projected system conditions.**

# Conclusion

The System-Steering and Predictive Resource Allocation System exists to answer a practical question:

> **If civilisation can see pressure building within its systems, must it wait for participants to fail before changing the environment?**
