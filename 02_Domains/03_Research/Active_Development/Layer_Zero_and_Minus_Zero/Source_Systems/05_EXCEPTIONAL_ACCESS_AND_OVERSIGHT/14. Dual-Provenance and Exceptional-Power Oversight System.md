# 1. Purpose

The **Dual-Provenance and Exceptional-Power Oversight System** is a candidate Concordian architecture for making exceptional exercises of public or institutional power highly auditable without unnecessarily exposing the identities of the participants subject to that power.

Its central purpose is to solve a difficult civilisational problem:

> **How can intrusive or secret powers remain accountable when public disclosure of the participant's identity would itself violate privacy, safety or legitimate investigative secrecy?**

The candidate answer is:

> **Dual Provenance.**

One provenance path preserves the detailed restricted information necessary for the legitimate operational or investigative purpose.

A second provenance path records the exercise of power in a form suitable for independent oversight while minimising unnecessary identity exposure.

---

# 2. Origin

The architecture emerged from development of exceptional identifiable access.

Once the Concord introduced a legitimate pathway for accessing otherwise protected personal information, a second problem appeared.

If exceptional access is fully secret:

- abuse may remain invisible;
- scope creep may be difficult to detect;
- institutional capture may occur;
- emergency powers may become normal.

If all exceptional access is fully public:

- participant privacy may be destroyed;
- investigations may be compromised;
- vulnerable individuals may be exposed;
- legitimate security needs may be undermined.

The system therefore seeks:

> **transparency of procedure without unnecessary transparency of identity.**

---

# 3. Core Principle

The strongest source-derived principle is:

> **The exercise of public power should be highly observable even where the person subject to that power must remain private.**

A related principle is:

> **Transparency of procedure without unnecessary transparency of identity.**

And the central rule is:

> **Every legitimate exercise of exceptional access should generate provenance, including oversight access itself.**

---

# 4. The Fundamental Separation

The architecture separates two questions:

## Question A

Who was accessed, why, under what evidence, and what information was disclosed?

## Question B

Was exceptional power used legitimately, proportionately, and under appropriate oversight?

Question A may require restricted identifiable information.

Question B often does not require public disclosure of the participant's identity.

Dual Provenance preserves both.

---

# 5. Core Architecture

The candidate architecture is:

**EXCEPTIONAL POWER EXERCISED**

↓

### PROVENANCE PATH A

**RESTRICTED OPERATIONAL / INVESTIGATIVE PROVENANCE**

Contains necessary identity and case-specific detail.

AND

### PROVENANCE PATH B

**OVERSIGHT PROVENANCE**

Contains sufficient information to establish:

- power used;
- authority invoked;
- scope;
- timing;
- category;
- review status;
- outcome of oversight;

while concealing participant identity where appropriate.

---

# 6. Restricted Provenance

The restricted provenance record may include:

- participant identity;
- case identifier;
- requesting official;
- requesting institution;
- evidential basis;
- authority basis;
- data requested;
- data accessed;
- timestamps;
- authoriser;
- secrecy status;
- retention conditions;
- later corrections;
- outcome of review.

This record may be available only to legitimately authorised actors.

---

# 7. Oversight Provenance

The oversight provenance record may instead include:

- non-identifying case identifier;
- type of exceptional power;
- authority category;
- approving authority;
- date or time range;
- scope category;
- whether emergency authority was used;
- whether judicial approval occurred;
- review status;
- compliance finding;
- expiry.

This enables scrutiny without unnecessarily publishing participant identity.

---

# 8. Case Identifiers

A non-identifying case identifier may allow different oversight systems to refer to the same exercise of power.

For example:

> Exceptional Access Case EA-2026-00174

This does not itself require public knowledge of the participant's identity.

The identity bridge, if one exists, should remain separately controlled.

---

# 9. Why Two Provenance Paths?

A single provenance system may create one of two failures.

### Failure A — Too Much Identity

Oversight requires broad access to full personal records.

### Failure B — Too Little Detail

Privacy removes so much information that meaningful oversight becomes impossible.

Dual Provenance attempts to preserve:

**operational specificity**

and:

**oversight visibility**

without requiring the same information in both places.

---

# 10. Provenance as Part of Legitimate Power

The system treats provenance as part of the lawful or legitimate exercise of exceptional authority.

The principle is:

> **A powerful action that leaves no meaningful trace is difficult to distinguish from an unauthorised action.**

Therefore provenance should not be optional administrative documentation added afterward.

It should be architecturally tied to the use of power.

---

# 11. Access Creates Provenance

The basic rule is:

**EXCEPTIONAL ACCESS**

↓

**PROVENANCE CREATED AUTOMATICALLY OR MANDATORILY.**

Not:

**EXCEPTIONAL ACCESS**

↓

**OFFICIAL MAY CHOOSE WHETHER TO RECORD IT.**

The stronger the power, the stronger this requirement should become.

---

# 12. Authority–Provenance Scaling

The architecture uses the relationship:

**AUTHORITY ↑**

→ **JUSTIFICATION ↑**

→ **PROVENANCE ↑**

→ **OVERSIGHT ↑.**

This means more intrusive power should create:

- more detailed justification;
- stronger records;
- stronger review;
- stronger auditability.

---

# 13. Low-Intrusion Example

A participant voluntarily authorises transfer of one document.

Possible provenance may be relatively simple:

- consent;
- document;
- recipient;
- time.

The event is legitimate but low in coercive authority.

---

# 14. High-Intrusion Example

A secret investigative search of a Participant Digital Twin may require:

- judicial authority;
- specific evidence;
- narrow scope;
- restricted investigative provenance;
- external oversight provenance;
- access logs;
- later review;
- expiry;
- possible later notification.

The provenance burden rises because intrusion rises.

---

# 15. Secrecy–Oversight Compensation

Some exceptional powers require secrecy.

The system adopts the relationship:

**REQUIRED SECRECY ↑**

→ **INDEPENDENT OVERSIGHT ↑.**

The logic is simple:

If the participant cannot observe or contest power in real time, another independent mechanism should become stronger.

---

# 16. Secrecy Must Not Eliminate Traceability

The architecture rejects:

> **Secret means unrecorded.**

Instead:

> **Secret may mean restricted visibility, but not absence of provenance.**

A secret action should still be observable to appropriately authorised oversight.

---

# 17. Restricted Secrecy

Secrecy itself should be scoped.

Questions include:

- What must remain secret?
- From whom?
- For how long?
- Why?
- Who may review the secrecy decision?
- When should secrecy expire?

The architecture should avoid treating secrecy as one indivisible binary state.

---

# 18. Secrecy Expiry

Where secrecy is temporary, provenance should record:

- start;
- reason;
- review date;
- expiry;
- renewal.

This helps prevent:

**TEMPORARY SECRET POWER**

from becoming:

**PERMANENT HIDDEN POWER.**

---

# 19. Later Disclosure

Where legitimate secrecy ends, a future system may permit:

- participant notification;
- broader oversight disclosure;
- public aggregate reporting.

The exact rules remain unresolved.

The architecture simply preserves the information necessary for such later accountability to be possible.

---

# 20. Oversight Without Participant Exposure

An oversight body may need to know:

> Was this access lawful and proportionate?

without necessarily needing unrestricted publication of:

> Who was the participant?

This supports:

> **privacy for the participant, visibility for the power.**

---

# 21. Privacy and Accountability Are Not Opposites

A core architectural insight is that:

**participant privacy**

and:

**institutional accountability**

need not be treated as opposing objectives.

The dual-provenance system attempts to increase both simultaneously.

---

# 22. Privacy Protects the Participant

The architecture supports:

> **Privacy should protect the participant from unnecessary observation.**

It should not automatically protect institutions from scrutiny.

---

# 23. Secrecy Protects a Legitimate Process, Not the Institution

Where secrecy is justified, it should exist because disclosure would materially harm:

- investigation;
- safety;
- security;
- another legitimate purpose.

It should not exist merely because the institution prefers not to be examined.

---

# 24. Power Should Remain Observable

A major source-derived principle is:

> **The exercise of public power should be highly observable even where the person subject to that power must remain private.**

This means the system should make it possible to know:

- that power was exercised;
- under what authority;
- how often;
- how broadly;
- whether it was reviewed.

---

# 25. Oversight Access Is Also Power

Oversight bodies may need access to:

- restricted files;
- participant identity;
- investigation detail.

That access itself is sensitive.

Therefore:

> **Oversight access is also an exercise of power.**

It must not create an unlogged secondary surveillance pathway.

---

# 26. Provenance of Oversight Access

When an overseer accesses the restricted record:

**OVERSIGHT ACCESS**

↓

**OVERSIGHT PROVENANCE EVENT.**

Potential fields include:

- overseer;
- oversight purpose;
- case;
- time;
- scope;
- outcome.

This creates recursive accountability.

---

# 27. Recursive Provenance

The architecture may therefore look like:

**EXCEPTIONAL ACCESS**

↓

**PROVENANCE**

↓

**OVERSIGHT REVIEW**

↓

**OVERSIGHT ACCESS PROVENANCE**

↓

**RECURSIVE OVERSIGHT**

where necessary.

The recursion must remain bounded.

---

# 28. Recursion Must Not Manufacture Authority

A central Concordian rule applies:

> **Recursion must never manufacture authority.**

An oversight institution does not gain unlimited access merely because it oversees another institution.

Its own authority remains:

- defined;
- scoped;
- reviewable.

---

# 29. Recursive Oversight

Recursive Oversight may examine questions such as:

- Is the oversight body actually reviewing cases?
- Are reviews substantive or ceremonial?
- Is one overseer repeatedly accessing unusual records?
- Are oversight findings acted upon?

This reduces the risk of oversight becoming mere institutional decoration.

---

# 30. Who Oversees the Overseer?

The architecture does not assume infinite hierarchy.

Instead, recursive oversight may use:

- rotation;
- peer review;
- independent audit;
- multi-body review;
- public aggregate Metrics.

The objective is not endless layers.

It is to prevent concentrated invisible authority.

---

# 31. Dual Provenance and Judiciary

Judiciary may participate as:

- authoriser;
- reviewer;
- appellate body;
- rights protector.

Judicial actions themselves should also generate provenance where appropriate.

The principle applies to institutions regardless of prestige.

---

# 32. Judicial Secrecy

Some judicial authorisations may legitimately remain sealed temporarily.

That does not mean the action disappears from the accountability architecture.

It may still generate:

- restricted judicial provenance;
- anonymised oversight statistics;
- later review.

---

# 33. Law-Enforcement Example

Suppose law enforcement obtains authority to access one participant's support record.

The architecture may generate:

### Restricted Record

- Participant X
- case reference
- offence under investigation
- authorised fields
- approving judge
- dates accessed.

### Oversight Record

- Case EA-4728
- law-enforcement access
- judicial approval
- narrow support-record access
- 3 data objects
- reviewed
- compliant.

The oversight system can therefore examine the use of power without publishing Participant X.

---

# 34. Emergency Example

Suppose emergency personnel use break-glass access to identify an unconscious participant.

The system may record:

### Restricted Record

- participant identity;
- emergency;
- medical data accessed.

### Oversight Record

- emergency identifiable access used;
- purpose: urgent treatment;
- duration;
- later review completed.

Emergency speed does not eliminate later accountability.

---

# 35. AI Participant Example

Suppose an authorised investigation accesses part of an AI participant's private memory state.

Restricted provenance might contain:

- AI identity;
- specific memory range;
- legal basis;
- investigator.

Oversight provenance may record:

- exceptional internal-state access;
- high-intrusion category;
- authority;
- scope;
- review result.

The architecture should not treat artificial identity as less deserving of privacy by default.

---

# 36. Hybrid Participant Example

A hybrid participant's data may include:

- biological medical records;
- computational memory;
- neural-interface logs.

An exceptional access event may cross several privacy domains simultaneously.

Oversight provenance should indicate the categories accessed even if participant identity remains protected.

---

# 37. Access Categories

Future provenance may classify exceptional power into categories such as:

- medical emergency;
- criminal investigation;
- security investigation;
- judicial discovery;
- participant-authorised transfer;
- safeguarding;
- infrastructure emergency.

Classification could assist oversight and pattern detection.

Exact categories remain undeveloped.

---

# 38. Intrusion Levels

The system may eventually classify access by intrusion level.

For example:

### Level 1

Single confirmation.

### Level 2

Limited field disclosure.

### Level 3

Restricted record access.

### Level 4

Cross-system linkage.

### Level 5

Deep personal or internal-state access.

Such a framework could help scale oversight.

It is not yet defined.

---

# 39. Scope Provenance

Oversight should know not merely:

> access occurred

but also:

> how much access occurred.

Possible scope indicators include:

- records viewed;
- systems queried;
- duration;
- data categories;
- identities affected.

Without scope, access counts alone may hide expansion.

---

# 40. Scope Creep

A case may begin narrowly and expand.

Provenance should preserve:

**ORIGINAL SCOPE**

↓

**EXPANSION REQUEST**

↓

**NEW AUTHORITY**

↓

**EXPANDED SCOPE.**

This allows oversight to distinguish legitimate development from silent expansion.

---

# 41. Bulk Access

Bulk access should be easily visible in oversight provenance.

For example:

> 20,000 participant records queried.

That is structurally different from:

> one specific participant record.

The oversight system should make large-scale exceptional power difficult to hide inside ordinary access statistics.

---

# 42. Aggregate Oversight Metrics

Public or institutional oversight may use Metrics such as:

- number of exceptional accesses;
- access types;
- approving authorities;
- emergency access rate;
- bulk access rate;
- average scope;
- average duration;
- denial rate;
- noncompliance rate;
- secrecy duration.

These Metrics can reveal systemic drift.

---

# 43. Public Reporting

Where compatible with security and privacy, aggregate reports may be publicly available.

For example:

> 472 exceptional identifiable accesses occurred this quarter.

> 84% judicially authorised.

> 5% emergency access.

> 3 cases found non-compliant.

This creates civil accountability without identifying the affected participants.

---

# 44. Minimum Reporting Resolution

Public oversight data must still consider re-identification risk.

In a small settlement, reporting:

> one access involving the only AI physician

may identify the participant indirectly.

Public resolution may therefore need adjustment.

---

# 45. Small-Population Oversight

Off-world or small-substrate populations create tension between:

- transparency;
- privacy.

Possible responses include:

- longer reporting periods;
- category aggregation;
- delayed publication;
- independent restricted oversight.

The power should remain reviewable even if public reporting must be less granular.

---

# 46. Oversight Provenance Is Not Public by Default

The second provenance path is:

> **oversight-compatible**

not necessarily:

> **fully public.**

Some information may be visible only to independent oversight institutions.

Public access can operate at a still more aggregated level.

---

# 47. Three Visibility Layers

A future architecture may therefore use:

### Layer A — Restricted Operational Provenance

Full case-specific detail.

### Layer B — Independent Oversight Provenance

Pseudonymised or identity-minimised case-level information.

### Layer C — Public Aggregate Accountability

Statistics and findings without case-level exposure.

This is a natural extension of dual provenance.

---

# 48. Independent Oversight

Oversight should be meaningfully independent from the institution exercising the power.

If an agency:

- requests access;
- authorises access;
- records access;
- reviews itself;

then provenance exists but independence is weak.

---

# 49. Separation of Duties

A stronger structure may separate:

**REQUESTER**

↓

**AUTHORISER**

↓

**DATA CUSTODIAN**

↓

**OVERSIGHT BODY.**

No single actor necessarily controls the full chain.

---

# 50. Institutional Capture

Oversight institutions can themselves be captured.

Potential causes include:

- political appointments;
- revolving employment;
- shared institutional culture;
- resource dependence;
- secrecy.

Recursive Oversight is partly intended to address this.

---

# 51. Rotation

Potential future anti-capture mechanisms may include:

- reviewer rotation;
- multiple oversight bodies;
- random case allocation;
- external audits.

These are development possibilities, not settled architecture.

---

# 52. Sampling

Oversight may not always be able to deeply review every access.

A future system may combine:

- mandatory baseline checks;
- automated anomaly detection;
- random deep review;
- targeted review of high-intrusion cases.

The acceptable model remains unresolved.

---

# 53. High-Intrusion Mandatory Review

Certain access categories may deserve mandatory independent review.

Possible examples include:

- AI internal-state access;
- bulk population access;
- cross-system linkage;
- secret long-term surveillance.

The exact thresholds require development.

---

# 54. Automated Oversight

Software may assist oversight by detecting:

- unusual access volume;
- repeated emergency access;
- after-hours access;
- excessive scope;
- repeated requester-authoriser pairing.

Automation may support human review.

It should not itself become unquestioned adjudication.

---

# 55. Oversight Algorithms Need Provenance

If an automated system flags access as suspicious, the system should preserve:

- algorithm version;
- rule triggered;
- data used.

Otherwise oversight itself becomes opaque.

---

# 56. False Oversight Alerts

A high access rate may be legitimate.

An anomaly creates grounds for review, not automatic misconduct finding.

The same Concord principle applies:

> **Indicator is a reason to ask a question, not an answer to that question.**

---

# 57. Provenance Integrity

The value of the architecture depends on whether provenance can be trusted.

Threats include:

- deletion;
- alteration;
- retroactive editing;
- fabricated authority;
- missing records.

The system therefore needs strong integrity protections.

---

# 58. Tamper-Evident Records

Possible future technical approaches include:

- cryptographic hashes;
- append-only logs;
- distributed verification;
- secure timestamps.

No specific technology is currently canonical.

The architectural requirement is:

> provenance should be difficult to alter invisibly.

---

# 59. Log Deletion

If legitimate deletion of provenance is ever allowed, deletion itself should create provenance.

Otherwise:

> deleting the audit record

could become the method for concealing abuse.

---

# 60. Provenance Retention

Exceptional-power provenance may need long retention because abuse may be discovered years later.

However, retaining identifiable records indefinitely also creates privacy risk.

This produces a major unresolved tension between:

- accountability;
- privacy;
- security;
- historical auditability.

Dual provenance may allow different retention periods for the two records.

---

# 61. Restricted Provenance Retention

The identifiable record may perhaps be retained for:

- active investigation;
- legal requirement;
- review period.

Longer retention needs stronger justification.

The exact rules remain open.

---

# 62. Oversight Provenance Retention

The identity-minimised oversight record may potentially be retained longer to permit:

- institutional pattern analysis;
- historical accountability;
- policy evaluation.

This is one advantage of separating the two provenance types.

---

# 63. Correction

If source information used in exceptional access is later corrected, provenance should record the correction.

For example:

**ACCESS BASED ON SOURCE VERSION A**

↓

**SOURCE CORRECTED TO B**

↓

**PROVENANCE UPDATED WITH CORRECTION EVENT.**

The original historical fact that A was used should not disappear.

---

# 64. Incorrect Authority

An access may later be ruled improperly authorised.

The provenance system should preserve:

- original claimed authority;
- later ruling;
- remedial action.

This allows the civilisation to learn from institutional failure.

---

# 65. Oversight Findings

Possible findings may include:

- compliant;
- partially compliant;
- excessive scope;
- insufficient authority;
- documentation failure;
- privacy breach.

The taxonomy remains undeveloped.

Findings should be distinguishable from allegations.

---

# 66. Remedies

The oversight system may refer failures to:

- Judiciary;
- disciplinary processes;
- governance;
- data correction;
- participant remedy mechanisms.

The system itself does not necessarily impose sanctions.

---

# 67. Participant Contestability

Where secrecy no longer applies, a participant may potentially access some provenance relating to powers used against them.

This may support:

- challenge;
- correction;
- remedy.

The limits remain open.

---

# 68. Participant Privacy in Oversight

The participant should not need to become publicly identifiable in order for institutional misconduct to be exposed.

This is one of dual provenance's strongest benefits.

---

# 69. Oversight of Non-Governmental Power

The architecture need not apply only to government.

Other powerful institutions might include:

- hospitals;
- AI infrastructure providers;
- civil identity systems;
- major employers;
- resource allocators.

If they exercise exceptional access or comparable power, similar provenance principles may be relevant.

---

# 70. Private Infrastructure Power

A privately operated system may possess technically powerful access.

Technical possession of access should not itself define legitimate authority.

Concord governance may need to constrain private infrastructure powers similarly to public ones.

---

# 71. AI Oversight Actors

Artificial intelligences may eventually serve as:

- auditors;
- investigators;
- judges;
- oversight agents.

The same provenance principles should apply.

An AI overseer should not possess invisible unrestricted access simply because it can process information efficiently.

---

# 72. Human Oversight Actors

Human officials likewise should not receive special exemption merely because oversight has traditionally been performed by humans.

The architecture is substrate-neutral.

---

# 73. Hybrid Oversight

Mixed human-AI oversight may improve:

- anomaly detection;
- contextual interpretation;
- consistency.

It may also create:

- shared blind spots;
- responsibility ambiguity.

Provenance should record who or what performed significant oversight actions.

---

# 74. Accountability Attribution

A system should be able to determine:

- who requested;
- who authorised;
- who accessed;
- who reviewed.

Otherwise institutional action becomes difficult to attribute.

This supports responsibility.

---

# 75. Collective Decisions

Where authorisation is collective, provenance should record the responsible decision structure rather than hiding behind:

> “the system decided.”

Collective governance should not erase accountability.

---

# 76. Machine Decisions

If an automated system grants or denies access, provenance should record:

- system identity;
- version;
- rule/model;
- human supervision where relevant.

Automation should not make responsibility disappear.

---

# 77. Public Confidence

The architecture may help support public trust by making a credible claim:

> **Exceptional powers exist, but their exercise is not invisible.**

Trust should emerge from inspectable architecture rather than institutional reassurance alone.

---

# 78. Chilling Effect and Oversight

Participants may avoid support or civil services if they believe hidden access is common.

Aggregate exceptional-access reporting may therefore help demonstrate whether exceptions are genuinely exceptional.

---

# 79. Exception Normalisation

One key oversight task is detecting when:

**EXCEPTION**

becomes:

**NORMAL PRACTICE.**

Indicators might include:

- rapidly increasing access volume;
- repeated emergency authority;
- broad standing permissions;
- declining denial rates.

---

# 80. Emergency Normalisation

Emergency pathways deserve special attention.

A system may initially justify bypasses for rare crises.

Over time, institutions may redefine routine inconvenience as emergency.

Meta-Metrics should track this drift.

---

# 81. Secrecy Normalisation

Similarly:

**TEMPORARY SECRECY**

may become:

**DEFAULT SECRECY.**

Oversight should monitor:

- percentage of cases sealed;
- average secrecy duration;
- renewal frequency.

---

# 82. Oversight Meta-Metrics

Potential Metrics include:

- review completion rate;
- average review delay;
- percentage of high-intrusion cases independently reviewed;
- noncompliance discovery rate;
- corrective-action completion;
- repeated institutional violations.

This makes oversight itself measurable.

---

# 83. Metrics About Oversight

The broader principle applies:

> **The civilisation should measure how well it is overseeing exceptional power.**

Examples include:

- review latency;
- reviewer disagreement;
- missed violations later discovered;
- provenance completeness.

---

# 84. Goodhart Risk in Oversight

If oversight bodies are rewarded for:

> low violation rate

they may classify fewer actions as violations.

If rewarded for:

> high violation detection

they may over-classify.

Oversight Metrics must therefore remain context-sensitive.

---

# 85. External Audit

Independent external review may periodically examine:

- provenance integrity;
- access patterns;
- secrecy;
- emergency use;
- oversight quality.

This reduces closed-system self-validation.

---

# 86. Public Research

Anonymised oversight data may support independent research into:

- institutional capture;
- access patterns;
- rights impacts;
- emergency-power drift.

This can strengthen civilisational learning.

---

# 87. Data Minimisation in Oversight

Oversight should not automatically receive every personal detail.

The system should ask:

> **What does the overseer need in order to review the exercise of power?**

This applies the same privacy principles to oversight itself.

---

# 88. Purpose-Boundary Test

Oversight access should therefore pass its own Purpose-Boundary Test:

- What is being reviewed?
- Is identity necessary?
- What detail is necessary?
- How long is access required?
- Can a pseudonymous record suffice?

Oversight is not an exemption from data minimisation.

---

# 89. Oversight Identity Bridge

Where an overseer genuinely needs the participant's identity, the system may use a controlled bridge.

That bridge should generate its own provenance event.

This keeps the exceptional-power chain auditable.

---

# 90. Cross-Institutional Correlation

Oversight may detect that the same participant has been subject to powers from multiple institutions.

This information may be highly sensitive.

The system should avoid unnecessary identity correlation unless the oversight purpose requires it.

---

# 91. Pattern Detection Without Identity

Many institutional patterns can be detected through:

- case IDs;
- categories;
- access counts;
- scope;
- timing.

This enables oversight without universal identity visibility.

---

# 92. Serious Misconduct Investigation

If oversight discovers evidence of serious misconduct, a separate process may justify deeper access to identifiable records.

Again, deeper access should be:

- specific;
- authorised;
- provenance-recorded.

The system does not allow oversight concerns to erase the privacy boundary automatically.

---

# 93. Whistleblowers

Whistleblowers may provide evidence that provenance records are incomplete or manipulated.

The architecture should eventually consider interfaces with protected whistleblowing systems.

This remains outside the present extraction but is a foreseeable development path.

---

# 94. Missing Provenance

An action for which required provenance is missing should itself become an oversight concern.

Possible explanations include:

- technical failure;
- procedural failure;
- unauthorised access;
- deliberate concealment.

Missing provenance is not proof of abuse, but it is evidence requiring examination.

---

# 95. Provenance Mismatch

A record may claim one scope while technical logs reveal another.

Such mismatches should trigger review.

This suggests future cross-validation between:

- declared provenance;
- technical access logs;
- source-system logs.

---

# 96. Cross-System Audit

Where an exceptional access crosses multiple systems, provenance may need to follow the event across those systems.

For example:

**INVESTIGATOR**

→ **IDENTITY BRIDGE**

→ **HEALTH SYSTEM**

→ **SUPPORT SYSTEM.**

Each step should remain auditable.

---

# 97. Provenance Chain

The system may therefore use a linked provenance chain rather than isolated logs.

A future chain could show:

**REQUEST**

↓

**AUTHORISATION**

↓

**IDENTITY RESOLUTION**

↓

**ACCESS**

↓

**EXPORT**

↓

**REVIEW**

↓

**EXPIRY.**

This helps reconstruct what actually happened.

# 98. Chain Integrity

If any part of the chain is missing, review becomes weaker.

The system should therefore eventually measure provenance completeness.

# 99. Scope of Exceptional Power

The architecture applies most directly to:

- exceptional identifiable access.

Its principles may later generalise to other powers such as:

- involuntary treatment;
- emergency detention;
- forced resource restriction;
- AI containment.

The broader pattern is:

**stronger authority should create stronger provenance and oversight.**

- review;

# 101. Failure Modes

Potential failure modes include:

- only restricted provenance exists and no independent oversight path;
- oversight record leaks participant identity;
- access is performed without provenance;
- oversight accesses records without logging;
- secrecy never expires;
- emergency power becomes routine;
- public reporting is too detailed and re-identifies participants;
- public reporting is too vague to reveal abuse;
- oversight body is institutionally captured;
- provenance can be edited invisibly;
- review is ceremonial;
- missing logs ignored;
- recursive oversight creates new uncontrolled authority.

---

# 102. Abuse Cases

Future adversarial testing should include:

- agency secretly accesses participant records and omits oversight provenance;
- oversight official accesses celebrity records out of curiosity;
- institution marks routine access as emergency;
- case remains secret for decades without review;
- public transparency report exposes a rare participant indirectly;
- oversight body rubber-stamps every access;
- technical logs disagree with official provenance;
- investigator deletes access record;
- AI oversight system acquires unrestricted access to all personal data;
- institution uses privacy as justification for hiding its own misconduct.

These should be treated as foreseeable abuses.

# 103. Candidate Principles

The architecture currently supports several candidate principles:

> **The exercise of public power should be highly observable even where the person subject to that power must remain private.**

> **Transparency of procedure should not require unnecessary transparency of identity.**

> **Every legitimate exercise of exceptional access should generate provenance, including oversight access itself.**

> **The more intrusive the lawful power, the stronger the required justification, provenance and oversight.**

> **Required secrecy should increase independent oversight rather than reduce accountability.**

> **Privacy should protect the participant from unnecessary observation; it should not protect the exercise of public power from legitimate oversight.**

> **Recursion must never manufacture authority.**

These remain candidate principles.

# 105. Explicit Non-Scope

The system does **not** itself:

- authorise all exceptional powers;
- conduct investigations;
- determine guilt;
- define criminal law;
- create unlimited oversight authority;
- require public disclosure of participant identity;
- abolish legitimate secrecy;
- replace the Exceptional Identifiable Access System;
- make every operational detail public.

Its role is:

> **to make the use and review of exceptional power provenance-visible while preserving participant privacy and legitimate secrecy where necessary.**
