# 1. Purpose

The **Evidence-Conditioned Investigative Modelling** architecture is a high-risk research concept for examining whether predictive or counterfactual modelling could ever be used legitimately within an investigation without converting prediction into guilt, suspicion into authority, or possibility into punishment.

Its purpose is not to establish a predictive policing system.

Its purpose is to define the constraints under which such modelling would have to operate before it could even be considered legitimate.

The central question is:

> **Can investigative modelling be used to explore evidence-conditioned possibilities without treating modelled futures as acts already committed or as authority to intrude upon participants?**

This question remains unresolved.

# 3. Why the Classification Is Necessary

Predictive investigative systems can create severe civilisational risks.

Possible harms include:

- precriminalisation;
- self-fulfilling suspicion;
- discrimination;
- false positives;
- surveillance expansion;
- punishment for unrealised futures;
- circular evidence;
- authority derived from prediction;
- opaque risk scoring;
- disproportionate targeting of outlier populations.

The Concord therefore requires a much higher burden of proof before such systems could be considered legitimate.

---

# 4. Origin

The concept emerged while separating:

**Predictive Support**

from:

**Investigation.**

Predictive Support asked:

> Could early indicators allow useful voluntary assistance before crisis?

This led to a second question:

> If an actual investigation already exists for independent evidential reasons, could counterfactual modelling help investigators compare plausible explanations, consequences or investigative branches?

The key distinction is:

**evidence creates the investigative context**

not:

**prediction creates the investigation.**

---

# 5. Core Principle

The strongest candidate principle is:

> **Investigative modelling should be conditioned by independently legitimate evidence rather than used to manufacture suspicion from general prediction alone.**

A related principle is:

> **Prediction does not grant authority.**

And an absolute candidate boundary is:

> **A simulated future must never be treated as though the participant has already chosen, caused or committed acts represented within it.**

---

# 6. Evidence Before Modelling

The preferred sequence is:

**SPECIFIC LEGITIMATE EVIDENCE**

↓

**INVESTIGATIVE BASIS**

↓

**APPROPRIATE AUTHORITY**

↓

**DEFINED INVESTIGATIVE QUESTION**

↓

**MODELLED ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS OR PATHWAYS**

↓

**REAL-WORLD EVIDENCE TESTING**

↓

**REVISE / REJECT MODEL.**

The prohibited sequence is:

**POPULATION PREDICTION**

↓

**PARTICIPANT FLAGGED**

↓

**MODEL GENERATES SUSPICION**

↓

**INVESTIGATION BEGINS**

↓

**MODEL USED TO JUSTIFY ITS OWN ASSUMPTIONS.**

---

# 7. Evidence-Conditioned Investigation

The concept may therefore be stated as:

> **Modelling may assist an already legitimate investigation, but should not itself manufacture the legitimacy of the investigation.**

This is the key boundary between:

**investigative reasoning**

and:

**predictive precriminalisation.**

---

# 8. Observation Versus Prediction

The system should preserve the distinction:

**OBSERVED EVIDENCE**

≠

**INFERRED POSSIBILITY**

≠

**PREDICTED FUTURE.**

For example:

### Observation

Participant entered Location A at 14:00.

### Inference

Participant may have met Person B.

### Prediction

Participant may later travel to Location C.

Only the first is directly observed.

The others require different evidentiary treatment.

---

# 9. Epistemic Labels

Any model used in this system should preserve categories such as:

- Observation;
- Inference;
- Hypothesis;
- Speculation;
- Prediction.

These distinctions should remain visible to investigators, Judiciary and oversight.

A hypothesis should never silently become fact merely because it appears inside an official investigative model.

---

# 10. Reality Trees in Investigation

Reality Trees could potentially represent competing explanations.

Example:

**OBSERVED EVENT X**

├── Explanation A

├── Explanation B

├── Explanation C

└── Unknown explanation.

Each branch could be assessed for:

- supporting evidence;
- contradicting evidence;
- uncertainty;
- additional testable observations.

This may reduce premature convergence on one explanation.

---

# 11. Multiple Explanations

A major safeguard is to require competing hypotheses.

Instead of:

> “Participant did Y.”

the model may represent:

- Participant did Y;
- another actor did Y;
- Y did not occur as initially believed;
- evidence is incomplete;
- source data are erroneous.

This helps prevent confirmation bias.

---

# 12. Model Must Seek Disconfirmation

A legitimate investigative model should not merely accumulate support for its preferred branch.

It should also ask:

> **What evidence would falsify this branch?**

This is central to avoiding self-reinforcing models.

---

# 13. Model as Investigative Tool

The model may potentially help organise:

- evidence;
- alternative hypotheses;
- missing information;
- testable questions;
- contradictions.

It should not become:

> **an automated judge of guilt.**

---

# 14. Prediction Is Not Evidence of Completion

If a model predicts:

> Participant may travel to Location X.

That prediction is not evidence that the participant:

- travelled there;
- intended to travel there;
- committed any act there.

Future possibility remains future possibility.

---

# 15. No Punishment for Unrealised Branches

The system should contain a strong prohibition:

> **No participant should be punished, sanctioned or deprived of rights merely because a model contains an adverse future branch.**

This should remain one of the highest-level constraints.

---

# 16. No Precriminalisation

The architecture rejects:

> **possible future offence**

as equivalent to:

> **current guilt.**

A civilisation should not treat participants as criminals because a model assigns them a probability of future wrongdoing.

---

# 17. Risk Scores

Generic individual risk scores create severe dangers.

Potential problems include:

- opaque weights;
- biased historical data;
- circular feedback;
- inability to contest;
- false precision;
- institutional overreliance.

This research architecture should therefore avoid assuming that a universal criminal-risk score is desirable.

---

# 18. Evidence-Conditioned, Not Population-Conditioned

The concept should remain:

**CASE EVIDENCE**

→ **CASE-SPECIFIC MODELLING.**

Not:

**POPULATION MODEL**

→ **INDIVIDUAL SUSPICION.**

This distinction is critical.

---

# 19. General Metrics

Aggregate Metrics may indicate:

> a type of offence is increasing.

That may justify:

- resource allocation;
- public-safety analysis;
- system-level prevention.

It does not automatically justify:

> investigating individuals who resemble the statistical pattern.

---

# 20. Demographic Proxies

Predictive models can unintentionally use demographic or social variables as proxies for protected or irrelevant characteristics.

Examples might include:

- postcode;
- poverty;
- support-service use;
- health status;
- substrate type.

These require severe scrutiny.

---

# 21. Support Data Must Remain Firewalled

The Support-to-Investigation Firewall remains active.

Support data should not automatically become investigative features.

For example:

- mental-health care;
- addiction treatment;
- housing support;
- financial assistance

should not be treated as generic criminal-risk predictors.

---

# 22. Support Is Not Suspicion

The parent principle remains:

> **Support is not suspicion.**

This system must not weaken that boundary.

If support data are exceptionally accessed, the Exceptional Identifiable Access System should govern that access.

---

# 23. Digital Twin Risk

Participant Digital Twins may contain rich information that could make them attractive to investigators.

This creates severe danger.

A Digital Twin may contain:

- inferred preferences;
- behavioural models;
- support history;
- hypothetical branches;
- participant goals.

These should not automatically become investigative evidence.

---

# 24. Digital Twin Is Not the Participant

The core Digital Twin principle applies:

> **The Digital Twin is a model of the participant. It is not the participant.**

An investigative model based on a Digital Twin therefore contains at least two layers of representation:

**participant**

↓

**Digital Twin**

↓

**investigative model.**

Each layer may introduce error.

---

# 25. Model-on-Model Error

If investigative prediction is built from another predictive model, uncertainty can compound.

For example:

**Digital Twin inference**

↓

**investigative risk model**

↓

**predicted offence branch.**

The output may appear precise while resting upon several speculative layers.

The system should therefore preserve provenance of inference chains.

---

# 26. Provenance of Reasoning

An investigative model should be able to distinguish:

- observed evidence;
- source;
- derived inference;
- model assumption;
- predicted branch;
- human interpretation.

This makes later audit possible.

---

# 27. Model Versioning

If modelling software changes, the system should preserve:

- model version;
- rules;
- parameters;
- training or reference data where relevant;
- decision context.

Otherwise investigators may be unable to explain why a branch was generated.

---

# 28. Explainability

A model should not be permitted to create consequential investigative direction solely through inaccessible reasoning.

Where significant rights implications exist, affected authorities should be able to understand:

- what evidence drove the branch;
- what assumptions were used;
- what uncertainty exists.

---

# 29. Contestability

If model output materially affects an investigation, there may eventually need to be a mechanism for:

- legal challenge;
- expert review;
- independent replication;
- correction.

The exact procedural rights remain undeveloped.

---

# 30. Circular Evidence

One severe failure mode is:

**MODEL FLAGS PARTICIPANT**

↓

**PARTICIPANT INVESTIGATED MORE**

↓

**MORE DATA COLLECTED**

↓

**MODEL SEES MORE INCIDENTS**

↓

**MODEL CONFIDENCE INCREASES.**

This can create circular reinforcement.

The system should explicitly test for this.

---

# 31. Observation Bias

Groups subjected to more surveillance will generate more observable incidents.

A model trained on observed incidents may therefore infer that the surveilled group is more dangerous.

This may reflect:

**measurement intensity**

rather than:

**underlying behaviour.**

---

# 32. Self-Fulfilling Suspicion

Investigative attention can alter the evidence environment.

For example:

- increased searches produce more discoveries;
- increased monitoring produces more recorded anomalies.

The system should distinguish:

**risk**

from:

**observation intensity.**

---

# 33. False Positives

A false positive may result in:

- investigation;
- reputational harm;
- privacy intrusion;
- lost opportunities;
- psychological harm.

Therefore model performance should not be assessed only by:

> detection rate.

The cost of incorrect suspicion must also be measured.

---

# 34. False Negatives

A model may also fail to identify a genuine threat.

False negatives matter.

However, reducing false negatives by flagging nearly everyone would create unacceptable false positives.

Trade-offs require explicit evaluation.

---

# 35. Base-Rate Problem

Where serious harmful acts are rare, even an apparently accurate model may produce many false positives.

This is a fundamental statistical risk.

Any future research must account for base rates rather than relying on headline accuracy.

---

# 36. Probability Is Not Certainty

A predicted probability should not be treated as a factual state.

For example:

> 70% modelled likelihood

does not mean:

> participant is 70% guilty.

The model may be estimating something entirely different.

Interpretation must remain precise.

---

# 37. Confidence Versus Probability

The system should distinguish:

- estimated branch probability;
- confidence in the model;
- quality of evidence.

These are not equivalent.

A model can assign a high probability while itself being poorly validated.

---

# 38. Evidence Thresholds

Future development would need to define what independent evidence is sufficient before modelling becomes permissible.

Possible categories might include:

- verified event;
- corroborated report;
- judicially recognised investigative basis.

No threshold is established here.

This remains a major open question.

---

# 39. Authority Thresholds

Similarly, the system does not establish which authority is sufficient for:

- initial modelling;
- access to additional data;
- intrusive surveillance;
- physical intervention.

Each level may require different authority.

---

# 40. Modelling Does Not Expand Authority Automatically

A core constraint should be:

> **The discovery of an alarming model branch should not automatically enlarge investigative authority.**

Additional authority should require:

- additional evidence;
- additional justification;
- separate approval where required.

---

# 41. Scope Expansion

If a model suggests a new line of inquiry:

**NEW BRANCH**

↓

**NEW EVIDENCE QUESTION**

↓

**SEPARATE JUSTIFICATION**

↓

**AUTHORISATION WHERE NECESSARY.**

The model itself should not be an unlimited scope-expansion mechanism.

---

# 42. Minimal Necessary Data

Investigative modelling should use the minimum data required for the legitimate question.

A model should not ingest:

> every available civil record

merely because more data may improve prediction.

---

# 43. Purpose Limitation

Data obtained for one investigation should not automatically become training data for universal investigative models.

This is particularly important for:

- support data;
- health data;
- private communications.

Use in one case does not automatically legitimise secondary use.

---

# 44. Case-Specific Model

The preferred research direction is toward:

> **case-specific modelling**

rather than:

> **population-wide suspicion engines.**

Case-specific modelling begins with existing evidence and explores alternatives within a defined investigative context.

---

# 45. General Investigative Knowledge

The civilisation may still learn general structural knowledge from completed cases.

For example:

> certain evidence combinations frequently indicate equipment failure rather than sabotage.

This may improve future reasoning.

However, identity should normally be removed before this becomes general knowledge.

---

# 46. Case-Learning Interface

The flow may be:

**COMPLETED INVESTIGATION**

↓

**ACTUAL OUTCOME**

↓

**COMPARE WITH MODEL**

↓

**IDENTIFY ERROR / SUCCESS / MISSING BRANCH**

↓

**ANONYMISE**

↓

**GENERAL STRUCTURAL KNOWLEDGE.**

This preserves learning without indefinitely retaining every participant as the object of learning.

---

# 47. Failed Investigative Predictions

If the model predicted branch A but reality showed branch C, that failure should be preserved.

It should not be hidden because the model was “almost right.”

Failures help reveal:

- missing branches;
- bias;
- poor assumptions.

---

# 48. Wrongly Suspected Participants

Cases in which a participant was strongly modelled as suspicious but later cleared are especially important.

These cases should be studied as:

> **model failure evidence.**

They may reveal dangerous structural bias.

---

# 49. Successful Predictions

Even successful predictions require caution.

One successful case does not validate the entire architecture.

The system should avoid survivorship bias.

---

# 50. Controlled Validation

Before any operational use, research would need:

- historical case testing;
- synthetic scenarios;
- adversarial evaluation;
- false-positive analysis;
- cross-population testing;
- cross-substrate testing;
- legal and ethical review.

No such validation is presently established.

---

# 51. Human Baseline

Any predictive investigative model should be compared against realistic alternatives.

Possible baselines include:

- trained human investigators;
- conventional evidence analysis;
- structured Reality Trees without predictive scoring;
- hybrid human-AI review.

The question is not:

> Does the model produce useful-looking outputs?

It is:

> Does it improve outcomes compared with realistic alternatives without unacceptable rights costs?

---

# 52. Outcome Measures

Possible future measures may include:

- correct hypothesis identification;
- false-positive rate;
- false-negative rate;
- investigative efficiency;
- unnecessary intrusion;
- rights violations;
- model correction rate;
- participant harm;
- oversight findings.

Accuracy alone is insufficient.

---

# 53. Rights-Cost Metric

A model that improves detection slightly but dramatically increases:

- intrusive searches;
- false suspicion;
- privacy loss

may be unacceptable.

Evaluation must therefore include:

> **rights cost per investigative benefit.**

The exact formalisation remains open.

---

# 54. Proportionality

The greater the potential intrusion resulting from model output, the stronger the required evidence and validation.

A low-stakes analytic suggestion and a model supporting physical detention are not equivalent.

---

# 55. No Automated Detention

This research architecture should not support:

**MODEL SCORE**

↓

**AUTOMATIC DETENTION.**

Any such use would represent a severe category error between predictive analysis and adjudicated authority.

---

# 56. No Automated Guilt

Similarly:

**MODEL OUTPUT**

must never become:

**GUILT.**

Guilt or liability belongs to legitimate adjudicative processes.

---

# 57. Judiciary

Judiciary may need to evaluate:

- whether model-derived evidence is admissible;
- whether methodology is reliable;
- whether access was authorised;
- whether scope was proportionate.

The model itself should not replace judicial interpretation.

---

# 58. Governance

Governance may define:

- permitted use cases;
- prohibited data;
- validation thresholds;
- oversight standards.

The modelling system should operate only within such adopted constraints.

---

# 59. Recursive Oversight

Recursive Oversight may examine:

- model error;
- institutional overreliance;
- demographic bias;
- scope expansion;
- secrecy abuse;
- emergency use.

High-risk investigative modelling should be subject to particularly strong oversight.

---

# 60. Dual Provenance

If investigative modelling affects consequential decisions, provenance should include:

- evidence inputs;
- model version;
- branches generated;
- outputs used;
- investigator interpretation;
- authority actions taken.

Oversight provenance should permit independent review without unnecessary public exposure of participant identity.

---

# 61. Investigative Model Access

Access to the investigative model itself may need protection.

It may contain:

- sensitive evidence;
- private information;
- hypotheses about uninvolved participants.

Oversight access should be purpose-limited and provenance-recorded.

---

# 62. Secret Modelling

Some investigative modelling may legitimately remain secret during an active case.

The principle applies:

**REQUIRED SECRECY ↑**

→ **INDEPENDENT OVERSIGHT ↑.**

Secret models should not become unreviewable models.

---

# 63. Later Notification

If model outputs materially contributed to intrusive actions, a future legal system may need to determine whether participants are later informed.

This may support:

- challenge;
- correction;
- transparency.

The rules remain unresolved.

---

# 64. Algorithmic Bias

Potential bias sources include:

- historical enforcement patterns;
- under-reporting;
- discriminatory surveillance;
- demographic imbalance;
- substrate imbalance.

A model may reproduce these patterns even without explicitly using protected characteristics.

---

# 65. Proxy Bias

Variables such as:

- location;
- employment;
- support use;
- device type

may act as proxies.

Bias testing must therefore examine correlations, not only explicit variables.

---

# 66. Cross-Substrate Bias

A model trained on human behaviour may perform poorly on AI participants.

Conversely, AI-specific behavioural features may not translate to humans.

Investigative architecture must not assume universal behavioural equivalence.

---

# 67. AI Participants

For artificial participants, investigators might be tempted to inspect:

- internal memory;
- logs;
- model weights;
- hidden state.

These may reveal extraordinarily intimate information.

Such access should be treated as potentially highly intrusive, not as routine technical inspection.

---

# 68. AI Behaviour Prediction

Artificial systems may appear more predictable because they are computational.

This should not justify:

- pre-emptive punishment;
- universal internal-state inspection.

High predictive confidence does not erase autonomy or due process.

---

# 69. Hybrid Participants

Hybrids may generate combined:

- biological behaviour;
- computational telemetry;
- memory extensions.

These may produce unusually powerful predictive models.

The stronger predictive capability becomes, the more important constitutional restraint may become.

---

# 70. Off-World Context

Small off-world populations may intensify:

- surveillance availability;
- predictive confidence;
- re-identification.

Survival risks may also be higher.

This creates a difficult balance between:

- safety;
- privacy;
- autonomy.

Small population should not automatically mean unlimited predictive authority.

---

# 71. Emergency Investigation

A severe emergency may justify faster investigative modelling.

For example:

- sabotage in a life-support habitat.

Even then:

- model output remains prediction;
- provenance remains necessary;
- authority remains separate;
- later review remains required.

---

# 72. Model-Mismatch Escalation

If the participant's actual behaviour contradicts the model, the response should be:

> **re-examine the model**

rather than:

> **assume the participant is concealing the truth.**

A source-derived principle is:

> **The stronger the mismatch between an individual case and the general predictive model, the greater the need for contextual and case-specific analysis rather than stronger conformity to the model.**

---

# 73. Participant Contradiction

A participant's explanation should be treated as evidence.

It should not automatically be dismissed because it conflicts with the model.

The model is not a privileged source of reality.

---

# 74. Reality as Final Arbiter

The central epistemic rule remains:

> **Reality is the final arbiter.**

The investigative model exists to organise and test possibilities.

Actual evidence determines which branches survive.

---

# 75. Model Rejection

If a model repeatedly produces:

- bias;
- excessive false positives;
- poor explanatory value;
- rights harms,

it should be rejected.

Operational inertia should not protect it.

---

# 76. Failed Research Is Useful

If controlled evaluation shows the system cannot satisfy Concordian constraints, that is valuable knowledge.

The research pathway must permit the conclusion:

> **Do not deploy this system.**

This is a legitimate outcome.

---

# 77. Non-Deployment as Success

A research programme can succeed by discovering that a proposed architecture is unsafe.

The Concord should preserve rejected models as developmental evidence.

---

# 78. High-Risk Research Environment

Any experimental work should initially remain isolated from live authority.

Possible stages include:

**SYNTHETIC CASES**

↓

**HISTORICAL DATA**

↓

**BLINDED RETROSPECTIVE TESTS**

↓

**SIMULATION**

↓

only if justified:

**NON-CONSEQUENTIAL SHADOW PILOT.**

No participant rights should depend on unvalidated experimental output.

---

# 79. Shadow Mode

A future research stage might allow the model to generate outputs without those outputs influencing actual investigations.

Researchers could compare:

- model prediction;
- investigator decision;
- actual outcome.

This may provide evidence without immediate operational risk.

Whether such research is lawful or ethical with real case data remains a separate issue.

---

# 80. Historical Testing

Historical cases may help test:

- model accuracy;
- alternative-hypothesis generation;
- bias.

However, hindsight bias is a major risk.

Researchers know the eventual outcome.

Testing should attempt to recreate the information available at the historical decision point.

---

# 81. Synthetic Testing

Synthetic cases allow controlled manipulation of:

- evidence quality;
- base rates;
- bias;
- missing information.

They are useful for architecture testing.

They do not establish real-world validity.

---

# 82. Adversarial Testing

Adversarial testers should try to make the model:

- falsely accuse;
- overfit;
- discriminate;
- leak data;
- expand scope;
- misinterpret support information.

The aim should be to discover failure before deployment.

---

# 83. Red-Team Rights Testing

Testing should explicitly ask:

- Can a participant be wrongly escalated?
- Can a protected characteristic be reconstructed?
- Can refusal of support become suspicion?
- Can the model bootstrap its own authority?
- Can speculative branches be treated as facts?

These should be treated as critical failure tests.

---

# 84. Constitutional Constraints Before Adoption

Unlike ordinary software systems, this architecture may require constitutional constraints before any operational adoption.

Possible constraints include:

- no precriminalisation;
- no punishment for predicted acts;
- no automatic authority from model output;
- evidence-conditioned access;
- provenance;
- contestability;
- independent oversight.

These remain candidate directions.

---

# 85. The Model Must Not Manufacture Authority

One of the most important rules is:

> **The model may assist reasoning, but it must not manufacture the legal or constitutional authority under which it operates.**

Authority must come from the legitimate civil architecture.

---

# 86. Recursion Must Not Manufacture Authority

The broader Concord principle also applies:

> **Recursion must never manufacture authority.**

A predictive branch generating another predictive branch should not gradually produce greater authority simply through recursive modelling.

---

# 87. Authority Escalation

The prohibited pattern is:

**LOW-CONFIDENCE PREDICTION**

↓

**MORE DATA COLLECTED**

↓

**MODEL CONFIDENCE INCREASES**

↓

**MORE AUTHORITY**

↓

**MORE DATA**

↓

**MORE CONFIDENCE.**

This can create a self-reinforcing surveillance ratchet.

---

# 88. Evidence Escalation Instead

The preferred pattern is:

**MODEL SUGGESTS TESTABLE QUESTION**

↓

**INDEPENDENT REAL-WORLD EVIDENCE SOUGHT**

↓

**EVIDENCE CONFIRMS / DISCONFIRMS**

↓

**ANY NEW AUTHORITY BASED ON LEGITIMATE EVIDENCE.**

This keeps reality between prediction and authority.

---

# 89. Investigative Friction

The system should contain intentional friction.

High-consequence actions may require:

- additional review;
- independent evidence;
- separate authorisation.

Friction is not necessarily inefficiency.

It may protect against irreversible error.

---

# 90. Reversibility

Where model uncertainty is high, reversible investigative steps should be preferred where practical.

For example:

- request additional public evidence

before:

- intrusive personal search.

This follows the broader Concordian interest in proportionality and reversibility.

---

# 91. Irreversible Harm

Some investigative actions may cause harms that cannot be fully reversed.

Examples include:

- public accusation;
- detention;
- destruction of private data;
- shutdown of an AI participant.

The threshold for model influence should therefore rise with irreversibility.

---

# 92. Transparency

Where compatible with legitimate secrecy, the civilisation should disclose:

- that such modelling exists;
- permitted purposes;
- prohibited uses;
- validation results;
- error rates;
- oversight framework.

A secret predictive architecture would carry especially high capture risk.

---

# 93. Public Metrics

Aggregate public reporting might eventually include:

- number of investigations using modelling;
- model versions;
- false-positive rates;
- later exonerations;
- oversight violations.

Whether operational deployment should ever occur remains unresolved.

---

# 94. Open Scientific Evaluation

High-risk systems should be subject to external scrutiny where compatible with security and privacy.

Independent researchers should be able to challenge claims of effectiveness.

Internal validation alone is insufficient.

---

# 95. Architecture Is Not Proof

A core Concord distinction applies directly:

> **Architecture is not proof.**

The fact that safeguards can be described does not establish that they work.

---

# 96. Formalisation Is Not Validation

Similarly:

> **Formalisation is not validation.**

A mathematically sophisticated investigative model may still be:

- biased;
- ineffective;
- unconstitutional;
- socially harmful.

---

# 97. Simulation Is Not Reality

And:

> **Simulation is not reality.**

High simulated performance does not establish safe real-world use.

---

# 98. Internal Consistency Is Not External Evidence

A coherent theory of constrained investigative modelling remains only a theory until evidence supports it.

This system currently has no such validation.

---

# 99. Strongest Current Claim

The strongest defensible claim is:

> **If predictive investigative modelling is ever explored, it should be constrained so that evidence precedes investigation, prediction does not create authority, simulated futures do not become guilt, and every consequential use remains provenance-visible and independently reviewable.**

This is a design constraint, not an effectiveness claim.

---

# 100. System Outputs

If research were ever developed further, possible outputs might include:

- competing investigative hypotheses;
- evidence gaps;
- disconfirming questions;
- alternative causal branches;
- uncertainty profiles;
- branch-comparison reports.

It should not output:

- guilt;
- sentence;
- automatic detention order;
- constitutional authority.

---

# 101. Explicit Prohibited Outputs

The following should be treated as out of scope:

**“Participant will commit offence X.”**

used as:

> authoritative fact.

The architecture does **not** itself:

# 104. Critical Failure Conditions

Research should consider the architecture unsuitable for operational use if it cannot reliably prevent:

1. prediction becoming authority;
2. prediction becoming guilt;
3. support becoming suspicion;
4. simulated acts becoming treated as completed acts;
5. demographic bias;
6. circular evidence;
7. self-fulfilling surveillance;
8. unreviewable black-box decisions;
9. excessive false positives;
10. authority escalation through recursive modelling.

Any of these may be sufficient grounds for rejection.

# 105. Candidate Principles

The architecture currently supports several candidate principles:

> **Investigative modelling should be conditioned by independently legitimate evidence rather than used to manufacture suspicion from general prediction alone.**

> **Prediction does not grant authority.**

> **A simulated future must never be treated as though the participant has already chosen, caused or committed acts represented within it.**

> **Support is not suspicion.**

> **The stronger the mismatch between a case and a predictive model, the greater the need for contextual and case-specific analysis rather than stronger conformity to the model.**

> **Model output may generate questions; real-world evidence must determine which branches survive.**

> **The model may assist reasoning, but it must not manufacture the authority under which it operates.**

These remain candidate research principles only.

# Conclusion

Evidence-Conditioned Investigative Modelling exists because advanced civilisations may eventually possess powerful predictive tools, and simply refusing to articulate the associated dangers does not make those dangers disappear.

The candidate architecture therefore begins not with:

> **How can we predict who will do harm?**

but with:

> **What constraints would prevent prediction from becoming precriminalisation, authority, or guilt?**

Its preferred sequence is:

**REAL EVIDENCE**

↓

**LEGITIMATE INVESTIGATIVE BASIS**

↓

**MODEL ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS**

↓

**TEST AGAINST REALITY**

↓

**REJECT OR RETAIN BRANCHES**

↓

**ANY NEW AUTHORITY MUST COME FROM LEGITIMATE EVIDENCE AND PROCESS.**

Its central rule is:

> **Prediction does not grant authority.**

Its evidentiary rule is:

> **Investigative modelling should be conditioned by independently legitimate evidence rather than used to manufacture suspicion from prediction alone.**

Its strongest rights boundary is:

> **A simulated future must never be treated as though the participant has already chosen, caused or committed acts represented within it.**

Its epistemic boundary is:

> **Model output may generate questions; reality must determine which branches survive.**

And its present civilisational status is deliberately limited:

> **This is a high-risk research question, not an operational Concord system. Its eventual rejection would be a valid and potentially successful outcome of Concordian evaluation.**
