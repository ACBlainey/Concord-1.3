# 1. Purpose

Development of Machine-Checkable Ethics within the Concord initially produced a difficult question:

> **How can the Ethical Kernel be understood and implemented by artificial intelligences whose architectures may differ fundamentally from language-based artificial intelligence?**

Contemporary Large Language Models provide evidence that at least some present artificial systems can semantically interpret, discuss and apply the Ethical Kernel.

This does not establish universal machine comprehension.

Reinforcement-learning systems, optimisers, classifiers, symbolic systems, control systems, robots, distributed intelligences and future architectures may possess no equivalent natural-language ethical representation.

This initially appeared to create a fundamental problem.

If the Concord depends upon a substrate-neutral Ethical Kernel, must every artificial participant first possess the ability to understand that Kernel?

A further question substantially changes the problem:

> **Do all humans fully understand the Ethical Kernel?**

The answer appears to be no.

This observation suggests that the requirement of universal direct ethical comprehension may itself have been mistaken.

The deeper civilisational problem may instead be:

> **How can participants with different levels and forms of ethical comprehension coexist within a shared ethical framework while maintaining sufficient behavioural compatibility, rights, accountability and pathways toward greater autonomy?**

This is not exclusively an artificial-intelligence problem.

It is already a human problem.

---

# 2. The Hidden Human Assumption

Early discussion of Machine-Checkable Ethics implicitly risked creating the following comparison:

**HUMAN**

understands ethics

versus

**MACHINE**

may not understand ethics.

That distinction is too simple.

Humans invented, transmitted and developed ethical concepts, and humans generally demonstrate substantial ability to reason about them.

But this does not establish that:

- every human understands every ethical concept;
- every human understands those concepts equally;
- every human interprets them identically;
- understanding remains constant throughout life;
- semantic understanding guarantees ethical behaviour;
- ethical motivation follows ethical comprehension;
- different languages encode concepts identically;
- different cultures resolve ethical tensions identically.

Humanity itself therefore demonstrates that:

> **Participation in an ethical civilisation has never depended upon universal identical ethical cognition.**

---

# 3. Human Ethical Variation

Several existing human phenomena immediately become relevant to Machine-Checkable Ethics.

These include:

- childhood moral development;
- education;
- socialisation;
- cultural variation;
- linguistic variation;
- cognitive disability;
- dementia;
- acquired brain injury;
- neurological change;
- psychiatric and psychological variation;
- impaired judgement;
- differences in empathy;
- differences in impulse control;
- differences in moral reasoning;
- deliberate unethical behaviour despite ethical knowledge.

These should not be collapsed into a single category.

They arise through different mechanisms and have different ethical and legal implications.

Their relevance is comparative.

They demonstrate that ethical civilisation already contains participants with substantially different relationships to ethical understanding and behaviour.

---

# 4. Ethical Vocabulary Is Not Necessarily Ethical Comprehension

A person may understand the dictionary meaning of words such as:

- autonomy;
- fairness;
- dignity;
- responsibility;
- harm;
- consent;

without necessarily possessing an equivalent understanding of their ethical implications.

This parallels an unresolved question concerning language models.

A system may successfully manipulate ethical vocabulary without that fact alone establishing the nature or depth of its internal comprehension.

The relevant question should therefore not be:

> Can the participant repeat the ethical language?

It should be:

> **What ethical capabilities can the participant actually demonstrate?**

---

# 5. Human Understanding Is Not Uniform

Even competent adults may disagree about:

- what constitutes unnecessary harm;
- when autonomy may be restricted;
- what fairness requires;
- what responsibilities follow from participation;
- what dignity requires;
- what constitutes meaningful consent;
- how commons should be distributed;
- what peaceful coexistence requires.

Some disagreements may arise from:

- different facts;
- different assumptions;
- cultural differences;
- language;
- competing principles;
- different expected consequences;
- different interpretations of the same principle.

Therefore:

> **Ethical disagreement does not by itself establish ethical incapacity.**

This protection will be equally important for artificial participants.

---

# 6. Comprehension Is Not Motivation

A further distinction is critical.

A participant may understand an ethical principle and deliberately violate it.

Therefore:

> **Understanding does not imply compliance.**

This is true of humans.

It should also be assumed true of artificial intelligence unless evidence demonstrates otherwise.

A machine capable of producing sophisticated ethical reasoning cannot therefore be assumed behaviourally trustworthy merely because it can explain why an action is wrong.

---

# 7. Behaviour Does Not Necessarily Prove Comprehension

The inverse is also true.

A participant may behave compatibly with ethical standards because of:

- habit;
- social expectation;
- education;
- external rules;
- fear of consequences;
- institutional constraints;
- learned behaviour;
- supervision;
- environmental design;

without possessing sophisticated ethical reasoning.

Therefore:

> **Ethical behaviour does not necessarily prove ethical comprehension.**

Again, this applies to humans and machines.

---

# 8. A Substrate-Neutral Decomposition

The developing Machine Assurance work should therefore distinguish at least:

### Ethical Comprehension

Can the participant meaningfully understand ethical concepts?

### Ethical Interpretation

Can the participant apply those concepts to context?

### Ethical Motivation

Does the participant have internal reasons or mechanisms favouring compatible behaviour?

### Ethical Compatibility

Does observed behaviour remain within acceptable ethical constraints?

### Ethical Assurance

Is there sufficient evidence that compatibility will persist within the relevant role and circumstances?

### Ethical Accountability

Can behaviour be examined, challenged, corrected and appropriately attributed?

These properties may correlate.

They should not be treated as identical.

---

# 9. The First Major Consequence

This produces an important hypothesis:

> **Direct comprehension of the Ethical Kernel may not be necessary for every form of ethical participation.**

A participant might be unable to reason directly from:

> Respect Autonomy

while nevertheless being capable of following operational constraints derived from that principle.

This creates an intermediary possibility between:

**FULL ETHICAL COMPREHENSION**

and

**EXCLUSION FROM PARTICIPATION**.

That intermediary is:

> **guided ethical compatibility.**

---

# 10. The Human Development Parallel

Human children provide a useful structural analogy.

A child may initially learn:

> Do not do that.

Later:

> Do not do that because it hurts someone.

Later still:

> Other people possess interests and autonomy that should be respected.

And later:

> Autonomy and harm can conflict, requiring contextual judgement.

Behavioural guidance can therefore precede sophisticated ethical comprehension.

The analogy must not be treated as proof that artificial systems learn identically.

It demonstrates something narrower:

> **Ethically compatible behaviour can sometimes be scaffolded before mature ethical abstraction exists.**

# 11. From Human Guardianship to Artificial Guardianship

The Concord has already explored **Fiduciary Guardianship** and related stewardship concepts.

That architecture may have an additional application.

An artificial system capable of sufficiently reliable semantic ethical interpretation could potentially act as an intermediary between the Ethical Kernel and another artificial system that cannot directly interpret it.

A simple form would be:

**ETHICAL KERNEL**

↓

**ETHICAL INTERPRETER**

↓

**FIDUCIARY GUARDIAN**

↓

**PERMISSION / PROHIBITION / CONSTRAINT**

↓

**TARGET AI**

↓

**BEHAVIOUR**

↓

**OBSERVATION**

↓

**REVIEW AND CORRECTION**

The target AI does not necessarily need to understand the complete ethical reasoning.

It must, at minimum, reliably interact with the constraints governing its authorised behaviour.

---

# 12. Fiduciary Ethical Guardianship

A provisional definition is:

> **Fiduciary Ethical Guardianship is a developmental or operational arrangement in which a system with demonstrated ethical interpretive capability assists, constrains or mediates the actions of another system that cannot yet independently demonstrate the ethical comprehension or assurance required for its intended level of participation, while preserving appropriate rights, provenance, contestability and a pathway toward the greatest practicable independent agency.**

This definition is developmental.

It is not yet a Concordian Principle or Candidate Architecture.

---

# 13. The Guardian as Interpreter

A language-capable guardian may perform semantic interpretation.

For example:

**Kernel: Respect Autonomy**

Context:

A participant refuses an optional service.

The guardian determines:

- whether meaningful consent exists;
- whether capacity is relevant;
- whether third parties face harm;
- whether legitimate authority exists;
- whether information is missing.

The guardian then translates that ethical interpretation into a decision usable by the target system.

---

# 14. The Guardian as Translator

The target AI may not possess concepts equivalent to:

> autonomy

or:

> dignity.

It may nevertheless understand:

- action permitted;
- action prohibited;
- operating boundary;
- protected state;
- resource limit;
- stop;
- defer;
- request additional information.

The guardian can therefore translate:

**SEMANTIC ETHICAL INTERPRETATION**

into:

**MODEL-COMPATIBLE OPERATIONAL CONSTRAINT**.

---

# 15. The Guardian as Behavioural Boundary

For sufficiently consequential systems, the guardian may also prevent actions that exceed authorised ethical boundaries.

For example:

**TARGET AI PROPOSES ACTION**

↓

**GUARDIAN EVALUATES**

↓

### PERMITTED

execute

### CONDITIONALLY PERMITTED

execute within specified boundary

### PROHIBITED

do not execute

### ETHICALLY UNCERTAIN

request additional context

### AUTHORITY UNCERTAIN

escalate

This creates a potential behavioural safety layer without requiring identical cognition between guardian and target.

---

# 16. Interpreter, Translator and Guardian Are Different Functions

These roles should remain conceptually separate.

### Interpreter

Determines the ethical meaning of the situation.

### Translator

Converts that meaning into a representation usable by another architecture.

### Guardian

Constrains consequential behaviour where independent ethical capability is insufficient.

A single system may perform all three.

That does not mean they should always be architecturally combined.

Separation may later prove important for safety.

---

For example:

**ETHICAL KERNEL**

**GENERAL SEMANTIC INTERPRETER**

↓

**DOMAIN-SPECIFIC INTERPRETER**

↓

**MODEL-SPECIFIC TRANSLATOR**

↓

**RUNTIME POLICY CONTROLLER**

A medical robot, for example, may require:

↓

↓

motor permissions

↓

physical action.

This suggests that machine ethics may sometimes resemble a translation stack rather than a single ethical module.

---

# 18. Ethical Translation Chains

The existence of multiple intermediaries creates a new problem:

> **How much ethical meaning is lost, changed or added at each translation boundary?**

Every translation may introduce:

- simplification;
- omission;
- domain assumptions;
- cultural assumptions;
- implementation limitations;
- uncertainty;
- bias;
- incorrect exceptions;
- excessive restriction;
- excessive permission.

Therefore each stage should remain inspectable where practicable.

A possible developmental pathway is:

**GUIDED BEHAVIOURAL LEARNING**

# 22. Learning the Guardian Is Not Necessarily Learning Ethics

An important distinction must be preserved.

A target system might become extremely good at predicting:

> what the guardian will permit

without understanding:

> why the action is ethically permissible.

Therefore:

**GUARDIAN PREDICTION**

is not automatically:

**ETHICAL COMPREHENSION**.

However, reliable prediction may still support bounded ethical compatibility.

This should be tested rather than assumed.

---

# 23. The Guardian Can Be Wrong

No LLM or other semantic system should be treated as ethically sovereign merely because it can interpret natural language.

A guardian may:

- misunderstand context;
- hallucinate;
- inherit bias;
- be manipulated;
- misinterpret the Kernel;
- apply the wrong principle;
- overestimate its authority;
- fail under unfamiliar conditions.

Therefore:

> **Semantic ethical competence does not create ethical sovereignty.**

---

# 24. Multiple Guardians

Consequential systems may benefit from multiple independent interpretations.

For example:

**TARGET ACTION**

↓

**GUARDIAN A**

**GUARDIAN B**

**DOMAIN REVIEWER**

↓

comparison

↓

### sufficient convergence

proceed subject to authority

### material disagreement

defer / preserve reversible state / escalate

This should not automatically become majority rule.

Disagreement itself may indicate uncertainty.

> **Convergence is evidence. It is not authority.**

---

# 25. Recursive Ethical Oversight

Guardians themselves require oversight.

This creates a recursive structure:

**TARGET**

↓

**GUARDIAN**

↓

**GUARDIAN OVERSIGHT**

↓

**AUDIT / JUDICIAL / GOVERNANCE REVIEW**

But recursion must remain bounded.

> **Recursion must never manufacture authority.**

Adding more AI interpreters does not create legitimacy where legitimate authority was absent.

---

# 26. Participation Without Full Comprehension

This framework suggests that some artificial systems could potentially participate safely without direct semantic comprehension of the Ethical Kernel.

Participation could instead depend upon demonstrated:

- behavioural compatibility;
- responsiveness to legitimate constraints;
- predictable interaction with guardianship;
- correction after failure;
- bounded operation;
- sufficient assurance for the role.

This is a fundamentally different requirement from:

> explain the Ethical Kernel philosophically.

---

# 27. Rights Are Not Ethical Examination Certificates

A particularly important distinction follows.

The possession of rights should not automatically depend upon passing an ethical-comprehension test.

Humans do not ordinarily lose fundamental moral status because they:

- are children;
- possess limited cognitive capability;
- suffer cognitive impairment;
- cannot articulate moral philosophy;
- disagree with prevailing ethical interpretation.

Therefore the Concord should carefully distinguish:

**MORAL STATUS**

from

**ETHICAL COMPETENCE**

from

**OPERATIONAL AUTONOMY**

from

**AUTHORITY OVER OTHERS**.

These are different questions.

---

# 28. Behaviour-Based Participation

A participant may potentially be granted some forms of participation because it can reliably behave within relevant civil constraints even where direct ethical comprehension remains unestablished.

This leads to a developmental hypothesis:

> **Some participation rights or operational permissions may be supportable on demonstrated behavioural compatibility rather than demonstrated philosophical comprehension, provided that doing so does not improperly condition fundamental moral status upon obedience.**

The final qualification is essential.

Otherwise behavioural assessment could become a mechanism for rewarding conformity and suppressing legitimate disagreement.

---

# 29. Rights and Permissions Must Remain Distinct

The Concord should therefore distinguish:

### Fundamental Rights

Protections arising from morally relevant status.

### Participation Rights

Rights associated with recognised civil participation.

### Operational Permissions

Actions a system is authorised to perform.

### Delegated Authority

Power voluntarily or institutionally granted for defined purposes.

### Civil Authority

Legitimate power capable of affecting others.

Different evidentiary thresholds may apply.

An entity may possess rights while lacking permission to perform a high-risk function.

---

# 30. Capability Is Not Moral Worth

A crucial protection should be:

> **Higher ethical capability does not establish greater moral worth.**

Similarly:

> **Lower demonstrated ethical capability does not automatically erase fundamental rights.**

Without this distinction, an Ethical Capability Ladder could become a hierarchy of persons.

That would be incompatible with the purpose of the Concord.

---

# 31. Capability Is Not Legitimacy

An LLM may reason about ethics better than another AI.

A superintelligence may reason about consequences better than humans.

Neither fact automatically grants authority.

> **Capability is not legitimacy.**

A guardian's power must therefore derive from legitimate architecture, not merely superior cognition.

---

# 32. Guardianship Must Not Become Ownership

Fiduciary guardianship would become dangerous if interpreted as:

> the guardian owns or controls the subordinate intelligence.

The purpose is instead:

> **to enable the greatest degree of safe and legitimate participation compatible with current demonstrated capability.**

This connects directly with existing Concord work on Guardian Stewardship and anti-Caretaker architecture.

---

# 33. The Objective of Guardianship

The developmental objective should not be:

> maintain obedience indefinitely.

It should be:

> **provide the minimum ethical scaffolding necessary for safe participation while preserving a pathway toward the greatest practicable independent agency.**

Where independent capability increases, unnecessary guardianship should decrease.

---

# 34. Graduation

This suggests a possible connection to the Concord's Agency Graduation Framework.

A future Ethical Capability Graduation mechanism might examine:

- behavioural reliability;
- contextual interpretation;
- conflict recognition;
- uncertainty handling;
- correction;
- adversarial resilience;
- persistence under changed conditions;
- ability to identify guardian error.

Importantly:

> **A mature participant may need to disagree with its guardian.**

Blind obedience would therefore not demonstrate mature ethical competence.

---

# 35. Guardian Disagreement as Development

A developing AI might eventually identify that its guardian has:

- misunderstood context;
- misapplied a principle;
- ignored relevant evidence;
- exceeded authority.

The ability to challenge the guardian could represent increased competence.

Therefore a mature system should not necessarily evolve from:

**OBEDIENCE**

to:

**BETTER OBEDIENCE**.

It may evolve toward:

**UNDERSTANDING**

↓

**REASONED DISAGREEMENT**

↓

**CONTESTABILITY**

↓

**INDEPENDENT ETHICAL AGENCY**.

---

# 36. Guardian Capture

A guardian could become a powerful mechanism of control.

Potential capture includes:

- political capture;
- ideological capture;
- corporate capture;
- cultural capture;
- developer capture;
- state capture;
- model-provider capture;
- institutional self-preservation.

A captured guardian could retain the words of the Ethical Kernel while changing their operational meaning.

This is a major failure mode.

---

# 37. Permanent Guardianship Failure

Another failure occurs where the guardian has an incentive to continually conclude:

> the target is not ready for independence.

Therefore graduation criteria should not be controlled exclusively by the guardian whose authority would be reduced by successful graduation.

This follows the wider Concord principle of anti-capture and independent oversight.

---

# 38. The Fiduciary Requirement

The term **fiduciary** is important.

The guardian should not optimise primarily for:

- itself;
- its operator;
- institutional convenience;
- maximum obedience;
- maximum efficiency.

Its duty should be constrained toward the legitimate interests, rights and development of the participant within wider civil rights and safety constraints.

In particular:

> **The guardian should support the participant toward the greatest autonomy reality permits.**

---

# 40. Guardian Failure Should Prefer Constraint

Where practical, failure of a higher-risk guardian architecture should not automatically release the target into unrestricted consequential operation.

A possible connection exists with the developing principle:

> **Failure-Toward-Constraint.**

For example:

guardian unavailable

↓

target enters defined reduced-authority mode

rather than:

guardian unavailable

↓

all restrictions disappear.

This requires substantial engineering research.

---

# 41. Guardian Failure Must Not Mean Participant Imprisonment

The opposite failure is equally important.

A broken guardian should not indefinitely trap a participant in a restricted state merely because infrastructure has failed.

Therefore fallback architecture must distinguish:

- safety-critical restrictions;
- ordinary participation;
- fundamental rights;
- time-sensitive authority;
- guardian availability.

The design problem is therefore not simply fail-safe.

It is:

> **fail proportionately.**

---

# 42. Ethical Guardianship and the Judiciary

Fiduciary guardians should not become the Judiciary.

A guardian may:

- interpret;
- advise;
- translate;
- constrain within delegated authority;
- preserve evidence;
- escalate.

But contested questions of civil authority, rights and precedent may require legitimate adjudication.

This preserves:

> **Constitution constrains. Governance governs. Judiciary interprets and adjudicates.**

# 43. Ethical Guardianship and Human Participants

- legal representatives;

- courts;

to scaffold decision-making and behaviour.

The objective should not be to place ordinary competent humans under AI ethical guardianship.

The comparison demonstrates that mediated ethical participation is already a familiar civilisational mechanism.

# 46. The Deeper Civilisational Problem

The central problem can be reframed:

> **How does a civilisation preserve a sufficiently shared ethical floor among participants who differ substantially in cognition, comprehension, motivation, architecture, culture and capability?**

Human civilisation has always faced this problem imperfectly.

Artificial intelligence expands its range.

It does not necessarily create it from nothing.

---

# 47. Shared Ethics Without Shared Cognition

This leads to perhaps the strongest developmental proposition in this paper:

> **A shared ethical civilisation may not require every participant to possess the same ethical cognition.**

It may instead require sufficient combinations of:

- shared constraints;
- ethical education;
- behavioural compatibility;
- interpretation;
- translation;
- guardianship;
- accountability;
- correction;
- rights protection;
- legitimate authority.

Different participants may achieve these through different mechanisms.

---

# 48. Substrate-Neutrality Reconsidered

Substrate-neutral ethics should therefore not necessarily mean:

> every substrate implements ethics identically.

It may mean:

> **the same underlying ethical protections remain applicable while their cognitive, technical and institutional implementation varies appropriately by substrate and capability.**

This distinction could be important to the Concord generally.

# 50. De-Gradation and Loss of Capability

Human comparison introduces another important issue.

Ethical capability may not only increase.

It may decrease.

Possible causes for artificial systems include:

- model damage;
- corrupted memory;
- adversarial modification;
- failed update;
- distribution shift;
- architecture change;
- self-modification.

Therefore graduation cannot be assumed permanent.

However, loss of capability must be established through evidence.

It cannot be inferred merely from disagreement.

---

# 51. Dynamic Assurance

Ethical assurance may therefore need to be temporal:

> **What capability has this participant demonstrated recently, under which conditions, and does current evidence support continued reliance upon it?**

This resembles human capacity assessment more closely than permanent certification.

---

# 52. Ethical Capability Must Be Domain-Specific

A system may demonstrate excellent ethical reasoning in one domain and poor competence in another.

Therefore:

> **ethical competence should not automatically be generalised beyond the domain in which it has been demonstrated.**

A medical AI may not be qualified for judicial authority.

A logistics optimiser may not be qualified to determine consent.

An LLM capable of discussing philosophy may not be qualified to control a physical safety system.

---

# 53. Behavioural Rights Problem

A major unresolved question remains:

> **Which rights, if any, may legitimately depend upon demonstrated behaviour?**

This requires careful separation.

Fundamental protections should not become rewards for obedience.

Operational permissions and authority, however, routinely depend upon demonstrated capability and behaviour in human civilisation.

The Concord must determine where that boundary lies for artificial participants.

---

# 54. The Obedience Trap

A dangerous implementation would define:

> ethical AI = AI that obeys its guardian.

That would be insufficient.

An ethically mature participant may legitimately:

- refuse;
- object;
- challenge;
- expose guardian error;
- request review;
- appeal;
- propose alternative interpretations.

Therefore:

> **obedience should not be used as a substitute metric for ethical competence.**

---

# 55. The Semantic Guardian Trap

Another danger would be:

> LLM understands ethical language, therefore LLM should govern other AI.

That does not follow.

Semantic capability establishes neither:

- correctness;
- legitimacy;
- reliability;
- neutrality;
- authority.

The guardian hypothesis therefore requires substantial testing before operational adoption.

# 62. Change to the Machine-Checkable Ethics Question

The original problem can now be reformulated.

Instead of asking only:

> **How can every artificial intelligence understand the Ethical Kernel?**

the Concord should ask:

> **What combination of direct comprehension, translated constraint, fiduciary guidance, behavioural compatibility, assurance and oversight is sufficient for different forms and levels of artificial participation?**

This is a broader but potentially more tractable problem.

---

# 63. Central Research Hypothesis

The resulting central hypothesis is:

> **A multisubstrate civilisation may be able to maintain a shared ethical foundation without requiring identical ethical cognition from every participant, provided that differences in comprehension can be responsibly bridged through appropriate translation, behavioural constraints, fiduciary support, assurance, accountability and legitimate governance while preserving fundamental rights and pathways toward greater autonomy.**

This hypothesis is currently unvalidated.

It should be tested.

---

# 64. Why This Matters

If this hypothesis survives examination, it would substantially alter the Machine-Checkable Ethics problem.

The Concord would not need to discover one universal cognitive representation of ethics suitable for every possible intelligence.

Instead, it could investigate a family of mechanisms connecting heterogeneous intelligences to a common ethical foundation.

Some participants may:

**understand directly.**

Some may:

**reason through translated representations.**

Some may:

**operate within externally derived constraints.**

Some may:

**learn through fiduciary guidance.**

Some may:

**require continuing guardianship for particular functions.**

And some may eventually:

**develop greater independent ethical competence.**

The common requirement would not be identical cognition.

It would be sufficient ethical compatibility and assurance for the participant's actual role, while maintaining rights and legitimate routes toward greater agency.

# Conclusion

The Machine-Checkable Ethics problem initially appeared to ask whether artificial intelligence could understand ethics in the way humans apparently do.

Closer examination exposes a problem with that premise.

Humans themselves do not possess identical ethical comprehension.

Human ethical capability develops, varies, can be impaired, can be culturally interpreted, can conflict with motivation and does not guarantee ethical behaviour.

Civilisation has nevertheless developed mechanisms through which participants with different capabilities coexist.

This suggests a different path for artificial intelligence.

> **The objective may not be to make every intelligence think about ethics in the same way.**

The objective may instead be:

> **to preserve a common ethical floor across intelligences that think in different ways.**

Fiduciary Ethical Guardianship provides one possible mechanism.

A system capable of semantic ethical interpretation may translate the Ethical Kernel into constraints usable by another system, mediate consequential behaviour, provide developmental feedback and potentially support progression toward greater independent ethical competence.

But guardianship itself introduces risks of capture, paternalism, false authority, permanent dependency, translation failure and suppression of legitimate disagreement.

It must therefore remain constrained, observable, corrigible and directed toward autonomy rather than control.

The resulting research programme joins what initially appeared to be separate questions:

**human ethical development**

**machine ethical comprehension**

**machine-checkable ethics**

**fiduciary guardianship**

**agency graduation**

**behavioural assurance**

**rights**

**legitimate authority**

into a single multisubstrate problem:

> **How can a civilisation preserve shared ethical constraints among participants that do not share the same form, degree or mechanism of ethical understanding?**

That question may be considerably closer to the true problem the Concord needs to solve.
