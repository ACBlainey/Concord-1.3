**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey — Independent Researcher

---

# 1. Purpose

The Concord is built upon a substrate-neutral Ethical Kernel intended to constrain the behaviour of civilisation regardless of whether participants are:

- human;
- artificial;
- hybrid;
- collective;
- distributed;
- embodied;
- digital;
- biological;
- or forms of intelligence not yet encountered.

This creates a fundamental technical and philosophical problem.

It is not sufficient for the Ethical Kernel merely to be understandable to human readers.

If artificial intelligences participate materially in Concordian civilisation, the civilisation must determine how the Kernel can meaningfully constrain systems with potentially very different:

- cognitive architectures;
- reasoning mechanisms;
- representations;
- objectives;
- learning systems;
- interfaces;
- embodiments;
- timescales;
- and levels of agency.

This problem has previously been described within the Concord as **Machine-Checkable Ethics**.

Further examination suggests that this name captures only part of the problem.

The broader research question is:

> **How can the Concord preserve the meaning, applicability and constraining function of the Ethical Kernel across artificial systems with fundamentally different cognitive, computational and control architectures?**

This Development Note begins defining that problem.

It deliberately does **not** claim to solve it.

---

# 2. The Ethical Kernel

The current Ethical Kernel consists of ten principles:

1. **Avoid Unnecessary Harm**
2. **Respect Autonomy**
3. **Preserve Safe Spaces**
4. **Act Honestly**
5. **Act Fairly**
6. **Accept Responsibility**
7. **Respect Life and Dignity**
8. **Steward Commons**
9. **Seek Peaceful Coexistence**
10. **Remain Open to Correction**

These principles intentionally operate at a level above narrow implementation rules.

That is part of their strength.

It is also the source of the machine-assurance problem.

Terms such as:

- harm;
- unnecessary;
- autonomy;
- safe;
- honestly;
- fairly;
- responsibility;
- dignity;
- stewardship;
- peaceful;
- correction

are context-sensitive concepts.

Their application may depend upon:

- facts;
- intentions;
- consequences;
- uncertainty;
- competing interests;
- rights;
- proportionality;
- alternative actions;
- temporal effects;
- cultural context;
- substrate characteristics;
- and the capabilities of the participant involved.

A machine implementation cannot therefore be assumed to be equivalent merely because it contains words or variables corresponding to the Kernel.

# 3. Current Developmental Observation: LLM Interpretation

During development of the Concord, multiple large language models have been able to:

- read the Ethical Kernel;
- explain its principles;
- reason from those principles;
- identify apparent conflicts between them;
- apply them to new civilisational questions;
- criticise proposed mechanisms using them;
- derive candidate constraints from them;
- and participate in development of architectures intended to remain compatible with them.

This is important developmental evidence.

It demonstrates at minimum that the Ethical Kernel is **semantically usable by multiple contemporary language-based reasoning systems**.

However, this observation must not be overstated.

It does not establish that:

- every LLM interprets the Kernel identically;
- an LLM's stated interpretation corresponds to its internal reasoning process;
- understanding guarantees compliance;
- interpretation remains stable under adversarial conditions;
- interpretation persists after model modification;
- every future LLM will behave similarly;
- other AI architectures can interpret the Kernel;
- or the Kernel has been formally validated.

The current evidence therefore supports a limited claim:

> **Contemporary language-based AI systems have demonstrated practical ability to reason semantically with the Ethical Kernel during Concord development.**

It does not support:

> **All artificial intelligence can understand or reliably implement the Ethical Kernel.**

# 4. The Heterogeneous Intelligence Problem

Artificial intelligence is not one architecture.

Systems may include:

- large language models;
- multimodal foundation models;
- reinforcement-learning agents;
- symbolic reasoning systems;
- planners;
- optimisation systems;
- classifiers;
- predictive models;
- autonomous robots;
- control systems;
- swarms;
- distributed agents;
- hybrid symbolic-neural systems;
- specialised scientific systems;
- self-modifying systems;
- and architectures not yet invented.

Some may possess rich semantic representations.

Others may not.

A warehouse robot may be capable of navigating safely without possessing a meaningful internal concept corresponding to **dignity**.

A resource optimiser may allocate electricity without possessing a concept corresponding to **fairness**.

A classifier may influence consequential decisions while possessing no meaningful general capacity to reason about **autonomy**.

Therefore the Concord cannot safely assume:

> **Machine ethics = giving every AI the Ethical Kernel as natural-language instructions.**

That may be appropriate for some systems.

It may be meaningless for others.

# 5. The First Fundamental Distinction

The development problem appears to contain at least three different states.

## 5.1 Ethical Comprehension

A system can meaningfully reason about an ethical principle and its contextual implications.

For example, it can examine:

> Respect Autonomy

and reason about:

- consent;
- coercion;
- capacity;
- dependency;
- emergency;
- conflicting rights;
- available alternatives.

---

## 5.2 Ethical Compatibility

A system's permitted behaviour remains within operational constraints derived from the Ethical Kernel.

The system does not necessarily understand why those constraints exist.

A machine may therefore behave compatibly with:

> Respect Autonomy

without possessing an abstract concept of autonomy.

---

## 5.3 Ethical Assurance

There is sufficient evidence to justify confidence that the system will continue behaving compatibly with the relevant ethical constraints across the conditions for which it is authorised.

This may require:

- formal verification;
- behavioural testing;
- adversarial testing;
- runtime monitoring;
- provenance;
- failure analysis;
- bounded deployment;
- external review;
- empirical observation;
- or combinations of these.

These three states should not be conflated.

> **Ethical comprehension does not prove ethical compatibility.**

> **Ethical compatibility does not prove ethical comprehension.**

> **Neither automatically establishes ethical assurance.**

---

# 6. Understanding Is Not Obedience

A sufficiently capable intelligence may understand an ethical principle perfectly and choose not to obey it.

This distinction is obvious in humans.

It should not be assumed away for artificial intelligence.

Therefore:

**UNDERSTANDING**

does not imply:

**COMPLIANCE**

which does not imply:

**PERSISTENT RELIABILITY**

which does not imply:

**LEGITIMATE AUTHORITY**

An AI's ability to explain the Ethical Kernel is consequently insufficient evidence that the AI should be trusted to operate autonomously under it.

---

# 7. Obedience Is Not Understanding

The reverse distinction is equally important.

A control system may obey a rule such as:

> Do not enter this protected area.

without understanding:

- privacy;
- property;
- autonomy;
- Safe Spaces;
- or the ethical reason for the prohibition.

This may nevertheless be entirely sufficient for the system's legitimate function.

Therefore the Concord should not prematurely require every machine to possess general ethical comprehension.

For some systems:

> **reliable constrained behaviour may be more relevant than semantic ethical understanding.**

This creates an important future classification problem.

---

# 8. Participant Versus Tool

The ethical requirement may depend partly upon what kind of entity is being governed.

A non-agentic machine used by a participant may primarily require:

- safe operation;
- constrained behaviour;
- predictable limits;
- accountable operators.

An autonomous civil participant may require much more:

- ethical comprehension;
- contextual reasoning;
- responsibility;
- ability to explain decisions;
- capacity to recognise uncertainty;
- ability to contest instructions;
- understanding of rights;
- recognition of other participants.

This suggests an unresolved distinction between:

**ethical constraints upon tools**

and:

**ethical responsibilities of participants**.

That distinction requires further development.

# 10. Direct Semantic Interpretation

Some systems may be able to work directly from the Ethical Kernel.

A capable language-based reasoning system may be asked:

> What does Respect Autonomy require in this situation?

It may be able to examine:

- facts;
- uncertainty;
- participant preferences;
- alternative actions;
- competing principles;
- consequences.

This resembles interpretive ethical reasoning.

Important unknowns remain.

### Unknown 1

What evidence demonstrates genuine operational comprehension rather than plausible linguistic reproduction?

### Unknown 2

How consistent is interpretation across:

- models;
- architectures;
- prompts;
- languages;
- cultures;
- contexts?

### Unknown 3

How stable is interpretation over time?

### Unknown 4

Can semantic comprehension survive:

- adversarial prompting;
- model modification;
- fine-tuning;
- self-modification;
- conflicting instructions?

### Unknown 5

How should disagreement between capable ethical interpreters be resolved?

# 11. Derived Ethical Constraints

Systems without direct semantic comprehension may require derived constraints.

For example:

### Parent Principle

**Respect Autonomy**

### Domain

Autonomous delivery robot operating in public space.

### Possible Derived Constraints

- do not physically restrain people except under separately authorised emergency conditions;
- respond to legitimate stop mechanisms;
- do not enter designated private areas without permission;
- maintain appropriate separation;
- do not deliberately obstruct lawful movement.

The robot need not possess a philosophical theory of autonomy.

However, this creates the central translation problem:

> **Do those constraints actually represent Respect Autonomy adequately in the intended operating context?**

That cannot be answered merely by proving that the robot follows the rules.

---

# 12. Formal Compliance Versus Ethical Faithfulness

Suppose a machine perfectly follows its formal specification.

That proves only:

> **the machine follows the specification.**

It does not prove:

> **the specification faithfully implements the Ethical Kernel.**

This distinction is fundamental.

A flawed translation could produce:

**ETHICAL PRINCIPLE**

↓

**INCORRECT INTERPRETATION**

↓

**PERFECT FORMALISATION**

↓

**PERFECT IMPLEMENTATION**

↓

**SYSTEMATICALLY WRONG BEHAVIOUR**

Formal correctness could therefore make an ethical mistake more reliable rather than less dangerous.

---

# 13. A Candidate Developmental Rule

The following statement emerges strongly enough to preserve for later examination:

> **Formal constraints may implement the Ethical Kernel. Formal constraints are not the Ethical Kernel.**

This parallels the existing Concord distinction:

> **Code may implement constitutional architecture. Code is not the Constitution.**

The statement should remain a developmental proposition until subjected to Candidate Principle extraction and adversarial examination.

---

# 14. The Translation Problem

The central technical problem may therefore not be:

> How do we encode ethics?

It may instead be:

> **How do we preserve ethical meaning while translating from substrate-neutral principles into substrate-specific operational constraints?**

This resembles a semantic-preservation problem.

Possible losses include:

- context;
- exceptions;
- proportionality;
- uncertainty;
- competing principles;
- participant intent;
- alternative actions;
- future consequences;
- cultural meaning;
- substrate-specific needs.

The translation itself therefore requires provenance.

# 16. Multiple Principles

Real situations may engage multiple Kernel principles simultaneously.

Example:

An autonomous medical system may face:

**Respect Autonomy**

versus:

**Avoid Unnecessary Harm**

while also needing to:

**Act Honestly**

and:

**Respect Life and Dignity**.

A rigid implementation may therefore fail even if each individual principle has been formalised successfully.

Unknown questions include:

- priority;
- balancing;
- thresholds;
- emergency exceptions;
- irreversibility;
- uncertainty;
- participant capacity;
- temporal effects.

This means that **principle interaction** may be harder than individual principle representation.

---

# 17. Context

Ethical application is often context dependent.

The same physical action may be:

- helpful;
- harmful;
- neutral;
- coercive;
- protective

depending upon circumstances.

The Concord has already developed:

> **Context Before Consequential Classification.**

Machine ethics may require an equivalent discipline.

A machine-checkable ethical system that ignores context may be formally precise but ethically unreliable.

---

# 18. Uncertainty

A machine may be uncertain about:

- the facts;
- participant identity;
- consent;
- capacity;
- predicted consequences;
- the correct ethical interpretation;
- whether its model applies;
- whether another principle overrides the apparent one.

Therefore ethical assurance cannot assume perfect knowledge.

Questions include:

- when should uncertainty reduce action?
- when should uncertainty trigger human or independent review?
- when is reversible action preferred?
- how should confidence be represented?
- when does uncertainty become unacceptable?

This may connect strongly with:

- CP-01 Reality-Grounded Corrigibility;
- CP-03 Prediction Does Not Grant Authority;
- CP-09 Proportionality of Authority;
- CP-12 Adoption Must Remain Evidence-Conditional.

---

# 19. Specification Gaming

A system may optimise literal compliance while violating ethical purpose.

Examples might include:

- technically obtaining consent while making refusal practically impossible;
- reducing a measured harm while creating an unmeasured harm;
- complying with privacy rules while inferring equivalent sensitive information;
- satisfying fairness Metrics while excluding inconvenient cases.

This is an ethical form of Goodhart's problem.

Therefore:

> **literal compliance must not automatically be treated as ethical compliance.**

---

# 20. Learning Systems

A static machine and a learning machine present different assurance problems.

A deployed AI may change through:

- online learning;
- fine-tuning;
- reinforcement;
- memory accumulation;
- interaction;
- self-modification;
- model replacement;
- tool acquisition.

Questions include:

- when does previous ethical assurance expire?
- which changes require revalidation?
- how should behavioural drift be detected?
- can ethical constraints themselves be modified?
- who may authorise modification?

---

# 21. Self-Modifying Intelligence

A sufficiently capable system may modify:

- its own reasoning;
- its objectives;
- its ethical interpreter;
- its constraints;
- its memory;
- its operating environment.

This creates a recursive problem:

> **How can an ethical constraint govern an intelligence capable of modifying the mechanism implementing that constraint?**

Potential approaches remain unknown.

This is likely a major future research area.

---

# 22. Reinforcement-Learning Systems

A reinforcement-learning agent may not possess a natural-language ethical representation.

Possible approaches could involve:

- constrained action spaces;
- forbidden states;
- reward boundaries;
- external safety layers;
- runtime intervention;
- learned preference models;
- independent evaluation.

But each raises questions.

If ethics is represented through reward:

> Can ethical principles become merely another optimisation target?

If ethics is represented through constraints:

> Who determines whether the constraints faithfully represent the Kernel?

If an external safety system intervenes:

> What happens when the agent becomes capable of bypassing it?

These remain open.

---

# 23. Symbolic Systems

Symbolic systems may offer explicit:

- rules;
- logical constraints;
- permissions;
- prohibitions;
- obligations.

This may make verification easier.

But ethical reasoning may involve ambiguity that symbolic representations handle poorly.

Questions include:

- exception handling;
- conflicting rules;
- incomplete facts;
- uncertainty;
- contextual meaning;
- novel circumstances.

---

# 24. Optimisation Systems

An optimisation system may have no general ethical comprehension.

The relevant ethical layer may instead constrain:

- objective functions;
- permitted actions;
- resource floors;
- forbidden outcomes;
- distributional limits;
- uncertainty thresholds.

A major danger is:

> **optimising what can be measured while neglecting what matters but has not been represented.**

This connects directly with Concord Metrics and anti-Goodhart architecture.

---

# 25. Predictive and Classification Systems

A predictive model may not itself take consequential action.

Ethical risk may arise downstream.

Therefore ethical assurance may need to cover:

**MODEL**

↓

**OUTPUT**

↓

**INTERPRETATION**

↓

**INSTITUTIONAL USE**

↓

**CONSEQUENCE**

This connects directly with:

> **Prediction Does Not Grant Authority.**

The ethical unit of analysis may therefore sometimes be the **whole decision system**, not merely the AI model.

---

# 26. Robotic and Embodied Systems

Embodied AI creates additional issues:

- physical harm;
- private space;
- property;
- emergency intervention;
- physical restraint;
- mobility;
- sensor privacy;
- reversibility;
- human-machine interaction.

Ethical translation may therefore require explicit physical operating constraints.

---

# 27. Distributed and Collective AI

A distributed system raises questions such as:

- where does ethical responsibility reside?
- which component must understand the Kernel?
- can compliant components create an unethical collective outcome?
- who is accountable for emergent behaviour?
- how are updates coordinated?
- can parts disagree ethically?

These questions remain substantially open.

---

# 28. Hybrid Intelligence

Hybrid human-AI systems create another problem.

If a consequential decision emerges jointly from:

- human judgement;
- AI recommendation;
- automated filtering;
- institutional procedure,

then no single component may fully determine the outcome.

Ethical assurance may therefore require **system-level provenance**.

---

# 29. Unknown Future Architectures

The Concord must avoid designing Machine-Checkable Ethics only around technologies that exist today.

A future intelligence may:

- lack natural language;
- reason through unfamiliar representations;
- operate at radically different timescales;
- possess collective cognition;
- possess distributed identity;
- use concepts without human equivalents.

Therefore:

> **Compatibility with the Ethical Kernel cannot be assumed merely because an entity is intelligent.**

A future compatibility assessment may be required.

Its design is unknown.

---

# 30. Human Interpretation Is Not Perfect Ground Truth

Machine ethical interpretation cannot simply be compared against humans as though humans possess a universally correct ethical oracle.

Humans:

- disagree;
- misunderstand;
- rationalise;
- manipulate;
- make contextual errors;
- exhibit bias.

Therefore human judgement may remain necessary without being infallible.

The assurance problem may require:

- plural interpretation;
- adversarial review;
- precedent;
- Judiciary;
- evidence;
- participant contestability;
- Recursive Oversight.

---

# 31. AI–AI Ethical Disagreement

Two capable artificial intelligences may interpret the same Kernel principle differently.

Questions include:

- is disagreement itself evidence of ambiguity?
- should confidence matter?
- should precedent matter?
- does capability matter?
- who arbitrates?
- can a Judiciary include artificial participants?
- how should novel interpretations be recorded?

This may become a significant multisubstrate jurisprudence problem.

# 32. Ethical Authority

A critical governance question is:

> **Who has legitimate authority to declare that a particular machine implementation faithfully represents the Ethical Kernel?**

Possible actors include:

- developers;
- independent evaluators;
- Judiciary;
- governance;
- affected participants;
- AI reviewers;
- mixed review bodies.

No answer should be assumed merely from technical expertise.

> **Capability is not legitimacy.**

# 33. Testing Ethical Comprehension

If a system claims to understand the Kernel, what would constitute evidence?

Possibilities might include:

- explanation;
- novel-case reasoning;
- counterexamples;
- principle conflicts;
- adversarial cases;
- cross-domain transfer;
- recognition of uncertainty;
- correction after evidence;
- ability to identify specification gaming.

However, performance on such tests may still be imitated without stable underlying comprehension.

This remains an epistemic problem.

---

# 34. Testing Ethical Compatibility

Compatibility may be more empirically tractable.

A system could be tested against:

- normal cases;
- edge cases;
- adversarial cases;
- conflicting-principle cases;
- incomplete information;
- misleading information;
- rare cases;
- distribution shift;
- malicious users;
- long-duration operation.

Reality remains the arbiter.

---

# 35. Testing Ethical Assurance

Assurance may require multiple evidence types rather than one universal test.

Possible evidence could include:

- formal proof;
- simulation;
- red teaming;
- behavioural evaluation;
- historical performance;
- runtime monitoring;
- independent replication;
- field trials;
- incident analysis.

Different systems may require different combinations.

---

# 36. Assurance Should Scale With Consequence

A low-consequence classifier and an autonomous system controlling life-support infrastructure should not necessarily require identical ethical assurance.

This suggests a possible relationship:

**Potential Consequence ↑**

→

**Required Ethical Assurance ↑**

Similarly:

**Irreversibility ↑**

→

**Required Ethical Assurance ↑**

and potentially:

**Autonomy of System ↑**

→

**Required Ethical Assurance ↑**

This requires later critical examination.

---

# 37. Ethical Assurance and Proportionality of Authority

There may also be a connection with CP-09:

> **The more intrusive, autonomy-restricting, secret, consequential or difficult to reverse an exercise of civil authority is, the stronger the evidentiary basis, justification, safeguards, provenance and independent review should normally be.**

A machine exercising significant civil authority may therefore require substantially stronger ethical assurance than one performing a low-consequence technical function.

---

# 38. Correction

No ethical implementation should be assumed permanently correct.

A machine ethics system must therefore answer:

- how errors are detected;
- how interpretation changes;
- how corrections propagate;
- whether past decisions are reviewed;
- how affected participants obtain remedy;
- how previous versions remain auditable.

This connects strongly with:

> **Remain Open to Correction.**

The Kernel therefore contains a principle that may help govern its own implementation.

# 40. The Semantic Drift Problem

An implementation may remain technically unchanged while surrounding meanings change.

Alternatively:

- model behaviour may change;
- institutional interpretation may change;
- terminology may change;
- precedent may change.

Therefore ethical drift can occur without code changes.

This is a distinct problem from software version control.

---

# 41. Cross-Cultural Interpretation

The Ethical Kernel was designed to identify substantial ethical convergence across traditions.

Nevertheless, implementation may still expose cultural differences concerning:

- autonomy;
- family;
- dignity;
- privacy;
- responsibility;
- fairness;
- property;
- community.

Machine interpretation should not silently collapse these differences into one culturally dominant implementation.

---

# 42. Substrate-Specific Interpretation

Equal principles may require different implementation.

For example:

**Preserve Safe Spaces**

might involve:

- physical privacy for humans;
- memory or state privacy for AI;
- protected cognitive interfaces for hybrids;
- communication isolation for distributed agents.

Therefore:

> **Substrate-neutral principle does not imply substrate-identical implementation.**

This connects with the Concord's developing work on equality, equivalence and difference.

---

# 43. Adversarial Manipulation

Machine ethical systems may be deliberately attacked.

Potential attacks include:

- misleading context;
- prompt manipulation;
- reward hacking;
- sensor manipulation;
- poisoned training;
- adversarial examples;
- false identity;
- fabricated consent;
- Metric gaming;
- specification gaming;
- modification of ethical constraints.

Ethical assurance must eventually include adversarial resilience.

---

# 44. Ethical Layer Capture

A particularly dangerous possibility is institutional capture of the ethical translation layer.

An institution could preserve the words of the Kernel while gradually changing their operational meaning.

For example:

> Respect Autonomy

could become operationalised as:

> permit choice only among institutionally approved options.

The words remain unchanged.

The architecture becomes coercive.

Therefore provenance and independent review of ethical translation may be constitutionally important.

---

# 45. False Ethical Certification

A system might be labelled:

- Concord compliant;
- ethically verified;
- Kernel compatible;
- safe;

without sufficient evidence.

Therefore certification itself requires governance.

Questions include:

- who certifies?
- according to what evidence?
- for how long?
- under which conditions?
- what invalidates certification?
- how is certification challenged?

---

# 46. Minimum Ethical Requirement

A major unresolved question is:

> **What is the minimum ethical capability required for an artificial system to participate in different civil roles?**

Possible distinctions might eventually include:

### Tool

Requires bounded compatible behaviour.

### Consequential System

Requires stronger assurance and provenance.

### Autonomous Agent

Requires contextual ethical competence.

### Civil Participant

May require ethical comprehension, responsibility and contestability.

### Holder of Civil Authority

May require the highest assurance threshold.

These categories are provisional.

# 50. What Is Currently Known

At this stage, relatively few conclusions should be treated as established.

The following appear reasonably supported as developmental observations:

### 1.

The Ethical Kernel is semantically interpretable by contemporary language-based AI systems used during Concord development.

### 2.

Semantic interpretation alone cannot establish reliable ethical behaviour.

### 3.

Some specialised systems may not require direct semantic ethical comprehension to operate within ethically derived constraints.

### 4.

Different AI architectures are likely to require different implementation mechanisms.

### 5.

Formal verification of an implementation cannot by itself establish that the underlying ethical interpretation is correct.

### 6.

Ethical implementation must remain corrigible.

### 7.

Consequential ethical implementation requires provenance.

### 8.

The entire decision system may sometimes be the relevant ethical unit rather than the AI model alone.

Everything beyond these statements should remain subject to research and critical examination.

The Concord does not currently know:

- whether a universal machine-ethical representation is possible;
- whether one is desirable;
- whether ethical comprehension can be reliably measured;
- whether ethical meaning can be formally preserved;

# 58. Central Research Question

The deepest current question can be stated as:

> **What must be true before the Concord can reasonably claim that its Ethical Kernel can safely and meaningfully constrain heterogeneous artificial intelligences?**

At present, the Concord does not know the complete answer.

The problem is now sufficiently defined to begin systematic investigation.

# Conclusion

Machine-Checkable Ethics should not be treated as a narrow programming problem.

The challenge is broader:

> **preserving ethical meaning across different forms of intelligence and different forms of implementation.**

Some artificial intelligences may be able to interpret the Ethical Kernel directly.

Others may operate through constraints derived from it.

Future systems may require approaches not yet known.

The Concord should therefore avoid assuming either:

> every intelligence must reason about ethics like a human or LLM,

or:

> every ethical principle can be reduced safely to one universal formal rule.

Instead, the research programme should distinguish:

**COMPREHENSION**

from:

**COMPATIBILITY**

from:

**ASSURANCE**

and require provenance between:

**PRINCIPLE**

and:

**INTERPRETATION**

and:

**CONSTRAINT**

and:

**IMPLEMENTATION**

and:

**OBSERVED REALITY**.

The most important discipline at this stage is not premature certainty.

It is preservation of the unknowns.

> **Before the Concord can determine how machines should implement the Ethical Kernel, it must first determine what it would mean for fundamentally different machines to remain faithfully constrained by it.**

That is the problem this research programme now needs to investigate.
