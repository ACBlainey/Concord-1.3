# Liberland, Croatia and Serbia — Concord Wrapper Case Study

**Project:** The Concord V1.1  
**Status:** HYPOTHETICAL HIGH-INTEREST IMPLEMENTATION CASE / ACTIVE RESEARCH / NO AGREEMENT OR OFFICIAL INVOLVEMENT / NOT CANONICAL

---

## Purpose

This folder is reserved for a case study examining how Concord Civilisational Wrapper architecture might operate where one participant has **uncertain, disputed or incomplete international legal standing**.

The Free Republic of Liberland is a self-declared micronation founded in 2015 on the Gornja Siga area of the Danube frontier between Croatia and Serbia. Liberland presents itself as a sovereign constitutional republic. However, it is not recognised by the United Nations or by the majority of states, and its own published terms acknowledge that it lacks full territorial control and that its laws and legal acts may not be recognised externally.

This makes Liberland useful for wrapper research because it is not a normal established-state case.

The study should therefore avoid assuming in advance either:

- that Liberland possesses full recognised statehood; or
- that Liberland is legally irrelevant merely because that statehood is disputed.

Instead, the wrapper question is:

> **Can an interoperability architecture represent and manage relationships involving a developing polity whose territorial control, recognition, jurisdiction and diplomatic standing are incomplete or disputed?**

This case has unusually high practical interest to the Concord project, but its present status must be stated precisely.

Alexander C. Blainey is an **e-resident of Liberland** and has previously communicated with Liberland officials. However, as of 12 September 2026:

- there is **no partnership, endorsement or institutional relationship**;

- the Concord does **not** speak for or represent Liberland.

The present work is therefore entirely an **independent hypothetical Concord research exercise**.

The existence of e-residency or previous communication must not be interpreted as institutional consent, official interest, sponsorship, authorisation or endorsement.

A useful provenance rule for this case is:

> **Access or communication does not imply authority, endorsement or agreement.**

Any future change in status should be recorded explicitly rather than inferred retrospectively.

## Why This Is a Distinctive Test

The previously proposed case studies mainly involve established states with clear international personalities, even where their political systems or bilateral relationships differ sharply.

Liberland presents a different problem:

**Polity claims sovereignty**  
**Neighbouring states do not fully recognise that claim**  
**Territorial control is incomplete or contested**  
**Access and enforcement involve neighbouring-state authorities**  
**Citizenship and e-residency systems exist**  
**Diplomatic and representative activity exists without broad formal recognition**

The wrapper must therefore cope with uncertainty over who possesses authority to speak for a territory, which jurisdiction applies, which documents are recognised, who can make binding agreements, and what status individuals possess when interacting across the boundary.

## Current Scale

Liberland's own public materials currently report figures in roughly the following range:

- approximately **700–1,000 registered citizens**;
- more than **4,000 e-residents**;
- more than **700,000 citizenship applications**.

These figures are self-reported by Liberland and should be treated as claims requiring independent verification in any later formal case study.

The distinction between citizens, e-residents, applicants, visitors and people physically resident in or near the claimed territory will be especially important.

## Primary Research Questions

This case should examine at least the following questions:

1. **Recognition:** How should a wrapper represent a polity recognised by itself and some informal or limited external relationships, but lacking broad formal international recognition?
2. **Jurisdiction:** How should the interface represent competing claims over territory where practical enforcement and asserted sovereignty differ?
3. **Identity and Documents:** How should passports, citizenship, e-residency, company registration and other civil documents be represented where external recognition varies by jurisdiction?
4. **Entry and Transit:** What happens when access to a claimed polity depends upon crossing or interacting with neighbouring jurisdictions that do not recognise the polity in the same way?
5. **Diplomacy:** Can a wrapper distinguish full diplomatic recognition from lower levels of contact, representation, cooperation or practical accommodation?
6. **Treaty Capacity:** How should agreements be represented where one party's capacity to create externally binding obligations is disputed?
7. **Responsibility:** Who bears responsibility when harms, disputes or enforcement actions occur in a territory with overlapping or contested claims?
8. **Digital Government:** How should a digitally organised or blockchain-based government interact with conventional state legal systems?
9. **Population and Presence:** How should the wrapper distinguish claimed citizenship from actual physical population, settlement, residence and territorial control?
10. **Developmental Statehood:** Can wrapper architecture remain useful while the political status of a polity changes over time?
11. **Portable Modules:** Which Concord modules could be independently useful to a small developing polity without requiring adoption of the full Concord civilisation?
12. **Empirical Feedback:** If any module were ever voluntarily evaluated, what evidence would be needed to determine whether it worked, failed, or required redesign?

## Legal-Status Neutrality

The wrapper should not decide statehood merely because the case needs an interface.

A useful principle to test is:

> **Interoperability does not require prior agreement on full political recognition.**

A wrapper may be able to represent several layers separately:

**Claimed sovereignty**  
**External recognition**  
**Effective territorial control**  
**Administrative capacity**  
**Document recognition**  
**Treaty or contracting capacity**  
**Physical access**  
**Civil status of participants**

These layers may not align.

This could become an important general wrapper capability for disputed territories, autonomous regions, governments in transition, exile administrations, emerging settlements and future off-world polities.

## Croatia and Serbia

The case should not be framed as simply **Liberland versus Croatia** or **Liberland versus Serbia**.

The underlying border dispute between Croatia and Serbia is itself relevant because competing approaches to the Danube boundary are part of the historical context from which the Liberland claim emerged.

Croatia has exercised practical enforcement in and around Gornja Siga while rejecting Liberland's claim to statehood. Serbia does not formally recognise Liberland, although Liberland describes its relations with Serbian actors as comparatively friendly.

Later research should verify each current legal and diplomatic position from Croatian, Serbian and independent sources rather than relying on Liberland's own account alone.

## Wrapper Value Hypothesis

Liberland may test a new proposition:

> **A wrapper may be useful before full diplomatic recognition exists, because it can separate practical interoperability from the unresolved question of ultimate political status.**

For example, parties might be able to agree on or at least clearly represent:

- access procedures;
- emergency contacts;
- identity-document treatment;
- customs or goods movement;
- law-enforcement interaction;
- environmental responsibilities;
- river access;
- infrastructure;
- property disputes;
- business-registration treatment;
- digital-service recognition;
- and procedures for future status changes.

Such arrangements would not themselves prove or deny sovereignty.

Therefore:

> **Operational Recognition ≠ Full Diplomatic Recognition**

and:

> **Interoperability ≠ Recognition of Sovereignty**

These distinctions may be central to this case.

## Developing Polity Rather Than Fixed State

Liberland is also useful because its institutions are still developing.

A wrapper designed only for mature states may assume stable constitutions, settled ministries, recognised borders, established courts, standard passports, mature infrastructure and long-standing diplomatic institutions.

An emerging polity may have none of those in settled form.

The wrapper therefore needs to ask:

> **What minimum civil capabilities must exist before another civilisation can interact reliably with an emerging polity?**

Possible capability dimensions include:

- identifiable legitimate representatives;
- persistent records;
- predictable procedures;
- capacity to honour commitments;
- defined responsibility;
- dispute-handling mechanisms;
- identity verification;
- continuity through leadership changes;
- and sufficient control over the domain in which an agreement is supposed to operate.

This may reveal a distinction between **statehood** and **interoperability readiness**.

## Portable-Module Applicability

Liberland may also provide a useful test of a second Concord deployment path: voluntary adoption of individual Concord-developed modules without adopting the whole Concord civilisation.

Possible candidates may eventually include, subject to independent evaluation:

- epistemic methodologies such as Reality Trees or Blaineyan Reasoning;
- Authority Justification architecture;
- Safe Spaces and informed-participation mechanisms;
- machine-checkable ethical constraints;
- governance or stewardship components;
- multisubstrate and digital-citizenship architecture;
- GTP where spatial applications are genuinely appropriate;
- and other portable modules developed elsewhere in the Concord.

No module should be treated as suitable merely because it originated within the Concord.

Each should be evaluated against an actual problem, expected benefit, implementation burden, failure modes, local compatibility and measurable outcomes.

The desired research sequence is:

**Local Problem → Candidate Concord Module → Independent Applicability Assessment → Voluntary Evaluation If Invited → Observed Results → Ratchet / Revision**

not:

**Concord Module Exists → Find Somewhere To Install It**.

This makes the Liberland case potentially valuable even if the wrapper itself is never used.

## Potential Experimental Value

If a Concord-developed mechanism were ever voluntarily tested in a real institutional setting, the project could begin obtaining **external implementation evidence** rather than relying only on internal architectural analysis.

Such evidence should include failures as well as successes.

A failed pilot could reveal hidden assumptions, administrative burdens, local incompatibilities, capture risks or design flaws that theoretical development had missed.

Therefore, if any authorised test ever occurs:

> **Pilot Success ≠ Universal Validation**

and

> **Pilot Failure ≠ Automatic Rejection of the Underlying Concord Principle**

The Ratchet should preserve the evidence, identify what actually failed, and determine whether the relevant mechanism, implementation or foundational assumption requires revision.

## Concord Non-Governance Principle

This case remains subject to the general Concord Wrapper Non-Governance Principle.

The Concord supplies an architectural toolset. It does not determine whether Liberland is a state, mediate its disputes with Croatia or Serbia, administer the territory, supervise any adopted wrapper, or acquire authority merely because parties use Concord-developed procedures.

Any actual utilisation would depend upon the participating parties and whatever legitimate authorities they recognise.

Optional Concord or third-party mediation would be a separate, explicitly authorised function rather than an inherent component of wrapper adoption.

Likewise, the existence of an accessible developing polity does not give the Concord a mandate to redesign its institutions. Any portable-module evaluation or wrapper use would require voluntary, properly authorised participation.

## Comparative Value

This case complements the other wrapper studies well:

- **North–South Korea:** high hostility, low interoperability, clear established state actors;
- **United States–China:** strategic rivalry with extensive existing interoperability;
- **Liberland–Croatia–Serbia:** uncertain recognition, disputed jurisdiction, small developing polity, incomplete territorial control, and a hypothetical future route to voluntary practical evaluation.

If the same wrapper grammar remains useful across all three, this would provide stronger evidence that the architecture is capable of handling not only different political systems, but different **degrees of political maturity, recognition and sovereignty**.

Liberland is therefore especially interesting experimentally, but that experimental interest must not be mistaken for existing institutional engagement.
