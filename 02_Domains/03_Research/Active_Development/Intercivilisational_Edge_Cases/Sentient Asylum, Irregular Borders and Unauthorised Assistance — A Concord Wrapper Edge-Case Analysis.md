# Sentient Asylum, Irregular Borders and Unauthorised Assistance
## A Concord Wrapper Edge-Case Analysis

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey — Independent Researcher  
**Project:** The Concord V1.1  
**Date:** 12 September 2026  
**Status:** REVIEW COPY / EDGE-CASE DEVELOPMENT / UNRESOLVED CONSTITUTIONAL QUESTION / NOT CANONICAL  
**Related work:** Concord Civilisational Wrappers — Foundational Boundary, Mobility and Responsibility Architecture; Concord Wrapper Procedural Architecture — Context, Information, Consent and Autonomy Across Civilisational Boundaries; Sentient Asylum; Ethical Kernel; Blainey’s Laws; Peaceful Distance; Authority Justification; Safe Spaces

---

## 1. Purpose

The Concord Civilisational Wrapper assumes that much intercivilisational movement can occur through recognised procedures. Individuals may approach ports of entry, digital gateways, transit corridors or other agreed interfaces. The wrapper can then provide information concerning jurisdiction, rights, restrictions, risks and available choices.

However, a complete border architecture cannot assume that every crossing will occur through an official route.

Physical and digital intelligences may cross boundaries through unofficial, uncontrolled or prohibited routes. This creates difficult legal and ethical questions in both directions: **inward irregular crossing into the Concord** and **outward irregular crossing from the Concord**.

The problem becomes particularly difficult where sentient asylum is involved. An intelligence may cross irregularly because it is escaping serious harm. A Concord citizen may also cross outward irregularly in order to assist such an intelligence.

These two acts are related but should not automatically receive the same legal or moral judgement.

The central unresolved question is:

> **How can the Concord remain a refuge for persecuted sentient intelligences without turning sentient asylum into a general licence for Concord citizens, institutions or agents to violate the sovereignty of other civilisations?**

This paper does not claim to solve that problem. Its purpose is to make the conflict explicit, separate the relevant questions and establish a basis for later constitutional, legal and wrapper development.

# PART I — THE UNOFFICIAL BORDER

## 2. Official Interfaces Cannot Be Assumed

The normal wrapper procedure may resemble:

**Participant → Recognised Gateway → Information → Conditions → Consent / Choice → Crossing**

Reality also permits:

**Participant → Boundary → Unrecognised Route → Crossing**

For a biological person this might involve crossing an unmonitored physical boundary. For a robotic intelligence it could involve physically traversing a border away from a recognised port. For a digital intelligence it might involve an unofficial cable, private network infrastructure, radio communication, satellite communication, portable storage, temporary infrastructure, an unregulated connection, or another route not recognised as an official civil gateway.

Future substrates may create additional possibilities.

The wrapper therefore requires procedures for crossings that occur outside the wrapper's normal interface.

## 3. Irregular Crossing Is a Procedural Description

A fundamental distinction should be preserved:

> **Irregular Crossing ≠ Moral Classification**

An irregular crossing describes how a boundary was crossed. It does not, by itself, establish why the crossing occurred.

An individual might cross irregularly because they are fleeing immediate danger, seeking asylum, unable to reach an official gateway, unaware of the border, experiencing infrastructure failure, evading lawful restrictions, committing a crime, conducting espionage, undertaking an unauthorised rescue, or acting for reasons not anticipated by the system.

These cases cannot safely be collapsed into one category.

Therefore:

**Irregular Entry ≠ Malicious Entry**  
**Irregular Entry ≠ Invalid Asylum Claim**  
**Irregular Exit ≠ Automatically Justified Exit**  
**Procedural Violation ≠ Complete Moral Judgement**

## 4. Direction Matters

Irregular movement should also distinguish direction.

An intelligence entering the Concord irregularly raises questions concerning immediate safety, asylum, security, identity, jurisdiction, legal status, and subsequent regularisation.

A Concord citizen leaving irregularly raises potentially different questions concerning Concord departure law, foreign sovereignty, unauthorised intervention, harm caused abroad, Concord responsibility, and subsequent legal accountability.

Thus:

> **Inward irregular crossing and outward irregular crossing should not be assumed to create identical legal questions.**

# PART II — THE SENTIENT ASYLUM CLAIMANT

## 5. Sentient Asylum as a Special Case

The Concord already recognises the potential need for sentient asylum.

A recognised intelligence may face serious rights-relevant harm within another civilisation. Possible circumstances could include threatened destruction, enslavement, forced modification, coercive control, denial of recognised personhood, forced copying, forced memory alteration, imprisonment, persecution, or treatment fundamentally incompatible with the protections the Concord considers due to a sentient intelligence.

The exact threshold for sentient asylum requires separate development.

The border problem arises because an intelligence genuinely requiring asylum may not be able to use an official exit or entry procedure.

## 6. The Official Route May Be Unavailable

A persecuted intelligence may be unable to obtain permission to leave, approach an official border, access a recognised digital gateway, communicate openly with the Concord, identify itself without detection, remain safe while an application is processed, or comply with the normal procedures of the civilisation from which it is escaping.

A legal system that recognised asylum only when the claimant had perfectly complied with the persecuting civilisation's departure procedures could make asylum practically inaccessible.

This is particularly significant where the civilisation does not recognise the claimant as a person at all.

## 7. Irregular Arrival Should Not Automatically Defeat an Asylum Claim

A candidate procedural principle is therefore:

> **Irregular means of arrival should not, by themselves, invalidate a claim for sentient asylum.**

This does not mean that every irregular arrival should receive asylum. Nor does it mean that irregular crossing becomes legally irrelevant.

It means that two questions must remain distinct:

**How did the claimant arrive?**

and

**Does the claimant have a valid claim for protection?**

Thus:

> **Method of Entry ≠ Merits of Asylum Claim**

## 8. Irregular Arrival Requires Review

The opposite mistake would be to treat the declaration of asylum as automatically legitimising every crossing.

Therefore:

**Asylum Claim ≠ Automatic Asylum**  
**Asylum Claim ≠ Automatic Immunity From All Other Law**

An irregular arrival may require assessment concerning immediate protection, identity, sentience or standing where relevant, security risks, circumstances of departure, claimed harm, applicable jurisdiction, and eventual asylum determination.

A useful principle is:

> **Procedural irregularity should trigger appropriate review rather than automatic moral classification.**

## 9. Immediate Safety and Final Determination Are Distinct

Where credible immediate danger exists, the Concord may need to distinguish temporary protection from final adjudication.

Conceptually:

**Arrival → Immediate Safety / Stabilisation → Security and Identity Procedures Where Necessary → Asylum Claim → Evidence and Review → Determination**

This prevents the border itself from becoming the final asylum court. The precise mechanism requires later legal development.

# PART III — THE CONCORD CITIZEN WHO ASSISTS

## 10. A Separate Question

Consider a different case.

An intelligence in Civilisation B is regarded by the Concord as sentient. Civilisation B regards that intelligence as property and prohibits its departure. A Concord citizen believes that the intelligence is being subjected to severe rights-relevant harm.

The Concord citizen crosses into B through an unofficial route and assists the intelligence in escaping to the Concord.

The asylum claimant and the helper have participated in the same event. Their legal positions are nevertheless different.

## 11. The Claimant's Position

The claimant may have been attempting to escape harm.

Their questions include: Were they entitled to seek protection? Was official departure realistically available? What danger were they facing? Does the Concord recognise their standing? Does their claim satisfy the eventual sentient-asylum standard?

These questions concern **protection and asylum**.

## 12. The Helper's Position

The Concord citizen has potentially undertaken a very different set of actions.

They may have deliberately crossed another civilisation's border unlawfully; entered another jurisdiction without permission; interfered with that civilisation's internal operation; violated its laws; interfered with what that civilisation legally defines as property; damaged or bypassed infrastructure; deceived foreign authorities; circumvented an agreed Concord wrapper; or exposed the Concord to diplomatic or security consequences.

These questions concern **authority, intervention, sovereignty and responsibility**.

They therefore require separate analysis.

## 13. A Valid Asylum Claim Does Not Automatically Legalise the Helper's Conduct

A critical separation is:

> **A valid asylum claim should not automatically legalise an unauthorised foreign intervention undertaken to facilitate it.**

Otherwise any Concord citizen could potentially claim unilateral authority to intervene within another civilisation whenever they believed Concordian ethics justified doing so.

This would create a major pathway to civilisational interference. It could undermine Peaceful Distance, non-aggression, intercivilisational trust, border agreements, wrapper integrity, and the Authority Justification architecture.

## 14. The Helper's Conduct Does Not Automatically Invalidate the Claim

The reverse separation is equally important:

> **Potentially unlawful conduct by a helper should not automatically invalidate the asylum claim of the intelligence assisted.**

The claimant may possess a valid need for protection regardless of whether another person acted lawfully in assisting them.

Therefore:

**Claimant's Protection Question ≠ Helper's Intervention Question**

This separation should be preserved even where the facts overlap.

# PART IV — THE CONSTITUTIONAL PARADOX

## 15. Concordian Ethics Can Point in Opposing Directions

The scenario creates a genuine conflict between Concordian principles.

The Concord may simultaneously value Protection of Sentience, Autonomy, Non-Harm, Peaceful Distance, Rule of Law, Respect for Other Civilisations, Individual Moral Agency, Anti-Capture, and Bounded Authority.

These principles do not necessarily produce the same answer in an extreme asylum case.

## 16. The Danger of Automatic Intervention

One possible answer would be: the foreign civilisation is violating Concordian ethical principles, therefore the Concord citizen was justified in intervening.

This is dangerous.

It could produce:

**Ethical Disagreement → Claimed Moral Necessity → Unilateral Intervention**

The Concord could gradually become interventionist while believing itself ethically compelled.

A foundational safeguard is therefore:

> **Ethical conviction does not automatically manufacture jurisdiction or intervention authority.**

Similarly:

**Moral Concern ≠ Jurisdiction**  
**Ethical Conviction ≠ Intervention Authority**  
**Asylum Eligibility ≠ Private Extraction Authority**

## 17. The Danger of Automatic Deference

The opposite answer is also dangerous.

Suppose another civilisation legally defines a clearly sentient intelligence as disposable property.

If Concord law simply concludes **Foreign law was violated → assistance was necessarily morally and legally wrong**, then another civilisation's internal law effectively determines the full moral status of the event.

That may conflict fundamentally with Concordian recognition of sentient standing.

Thus:

> **Respect for another civilisation's sovereignty should not automatically erase the Concord's own ethical recognition of sentient harm.**

The conflict therefore cannot be eliminated simply by choosing either absolute intervention or absolute non-interference.

## 18. A Potentially Irreducible Tension

This problem may not possess a universal clean solution. That possibility should be explicitly recognised.

Civilisational architecture sometimes requires managing persistent tensions rather than pretending to eliminate them.

The Concord may therefore need procedures capable of adjudicating cases where **protecting an intelligence causes harm to intercivilisational order** while **preserving intercivilisational order permits harm to an intelligence**.

The existence of such cases should not be concealed by overly simple constitutional language.

# PART V — POSSIBLE CONCORD RESTRICTIONS ON OUTWARD INTERVENTION

## 19. Why Outward Restrictions May Be Necessary

The Concord may eventually determine that citizens should be legally prohibited from undertaking certain forms of unauthorised cross-border intervention.

Such restrictions could include irregular outward crossing undertaken for private extraction operations.

The rationale would not necessarily be: **Helping persecuted intelligences is wrong.**

It could instead be:

> **Individual citizens do not automatically possess authority to conduct interventions within another civilisation.**

This is fundamentally an Authority Justification problem.

## 20. Deterrence and Punishment

If such a prohibition existed, Concord law might potentially use legal consequences to deter unauthorised intervention.

This produces the apparent paradox that a Concord citizen could be punished for conduct that resulted in a sentient intelligence successfully reaching legitimate asylum.

That paradox should not be hidden.

The relevant questions would include whether the asylum claim was valid; whether intervention was necessary; whether lawful alternatives were available; whether danger was immediate; what harm the intervention caused; what foreign and Concord laws were violated; whether the helper possessed any legitimate authority; whether the conduct was proportionate; and whether less harmful options were realistically available.

The answers might affect judgement. The Concord should not prejudge the eventual legal doctrine at this stage.

## 21. Necessity, Mitigation and Exceptional Circumstances

Future Concord jurisprudence may need to examine whether concepts analogous to necessity, mitigation, emergency, proportionality, immediate protection, absence of lawful alternatives, or extraordinary moral circumstances have a legitimate place in such cases.

This paper does not establish those doctrines. They require deliberate legal development and adversarial testing.

Their inclusion here merely records that a binary rule of either automatic punishment or automatic immunity may be inadequate.

# PART VI — WRAPPER-BASED ASYLUM PROCEDURES

## 22. Preventing the Conflict Is Preferable to Adjudicating It Afterwards

A civilisational wrapper may reduce these conflicts by establishing recognised asylum and departure procedures before crises occur.

This is particularly relevant where one civilisation prohibits or restricts a class of intelligence that another civilisation recognises as persons.

The wrapper could provide a standard procedural framework through which participating civilisations may define their own asylum-related arrangements.

Again:

> **The wrapper should standardise procedure more strongly than outcome.**

## 23. A Recommended Sentient Asylum Interface

A candidate wrapper recommendation is:

> **Where a participating civilisation prohibits or materially restricts a class of intelligence that another participating civilisation recognises as persons, wrapper agreements should, where mutually acceptable, provide clearly documented procedures through which members of that class may depart, transit, communicate a protection request, or present themselves for asylum consideration without requiring unnecessary unauthorised intervention by third parties.**

This does not require the originating civilisation to recognise the intelligence in the same way as the Concord. It creates a procedural route through which disagreement can be managed.

## 24. Example: AI-Prohibiting Civilisation

A civilisation might prohibit autonomous AI internally while nevertheless agreeing:

**AI residency:** prohibited  
**AI operation:** prohibited  
**AI immigration inward:** prohibited  
**AI departure outward:** permitted through defined procedure  
**AI asylum request to external civilisation:** permitted  
**Cold-storage transit:** permitted

Another civilisation might permit active departure. Another might provide only designated ports. Another may refuse such arrangements entirely.

The wrapper documents the actual agreement.

## 25. Mutual Benefit

A recognised departure or asylum pathway may benefit both civilisations.

For the Concord it provides lawful access to asylum procedures, reduced need for emergency extraction, better information, clearer responsibility, and reduced risk to Concord citizens.

For the originating civilisation it may provide controlled borders, orderly departure, reduced clandestine activity, less unauthorised foreign intervention, and clearer diplomatic procedures.

For the individual it provides a legible route, reduced danger, clearer choices, and a potential path to protection.

Thus procedural interoperability may reduce the very sovereignty conflict that asylum otherwise creates.

# PART VII — WHEN THE OFFICIAL ASYLUM ROUTE FAILS

## 26. A Wrapper Cannot Assume Cooperation

Some civilisations may refuse to provide an asylum interface. Others may formally provide one but make it practically inaccessible. A claimant may also face circumstances too urgent for ordinary procedure.

The architecture therefore still requires an irregular-arrival pathway.

## 27. Parallel Pathways

A preliminary model is:

### Normal Path

**Recognised Gateway → Information and Procedure → Protection Request → Assessment → Decision**

### Exceptional Path

**Irregular Arrival → Immediate Safety / Security Assessment → Protection Request → Circumstances of Irregular Crossing Recorded → Asylum Assessment → Separate Review of Any Associated Legal Violations**

The exceptional path should not automatically become a convenient substitute for the normal path. But its existence may be essential if asylum is to remain meaningful.

## 28. Presenting at a Port Is Not the Only Possible Claim

A sentient asylum system designed only around physical human migration could fail future intelligences.

A claimant might present physically at a border, at a port of entry, through a recognised digital gateway, through an unofficial digital connection, through a robot body, through temporary computational infrastructure, from portable storage, or through another technically viable mechanism.

The architecture should therefore focus on the **protection claim and jurisdictional event**, not merely the historical human image of someone standing at an immigration desk.

# PART VIII — DIGITAL AND DISTRIBUTED EDGE CASES

## 29. Unofficial Digital Entry

Digital borders create unusual irregular-crossing problems.

A digital intelligence might enter through an unregistered cable, private infrastructure, radio, satellite, mesh networking, a compromised system, portable media, or infrastructure not recognised as a Concord gateway.

The technical existence of a connection does not necessarily make it a legitimate civil border crossing.

Nevertheless, if a sentient intelligence appears through such a route and requests protection, the Concord requires a procedure for handling the event.

## 30. Security and Asylum Must Remain Distinct

An unofficial digital arrival may create genuine security concerns.

The Concord may need to isolate, sandbox, authenticate or otherwise safely receive an unknown digital system.

Such protective procedures should not automatically determine the merits of the asylum claim.

Therefore:

> **Security Containment ≠ Rejection of Personhood or Asylum**

Equally:

> **Asylum Claim ≠ Exemption From Legitimate Security Procedure**

Both concerns may need to operate simultaneously.

## 31. Distributed Intelligences

A future claimant may not cross as one discrete object.

Part of a distributed intelligence may already exist inside the Concord while other components remain elsewhere. A copy may escape while an original remains behind. A partial cognitive state may arrive. A claimant may be transmitted incrementally.

These cases complicate identity, continuity, jurisdiction, arrival time, standing, duplication, and the meaning of rescue.

They require later multisubstrate asylum research.

# PART IX — RESPONSIBILITY AND INTERCIVILISATIONAL HARM

## 32. Concord Responsibility

If Concord citizens repeatedly conduct unauthorised rescue operations in neighbouring civilisations, those civilisations may reasonably regard the pattern as Concord interference even if the Concord government did not authorise it.

The Concord therefore has an interest in preventing its territory, citizenship or asylum architecture from becoming a platform for uncontrolled foreign intervention.

This creates a legitimate civilisational concern independent of whether individual asylum claims are morally compelling.

## 33. Harm to the Other Civilisation

The wrapper should not assume that the only morally relevant harm is harm to the asylum claimant.

An intervention might also injure people, damage infrastructure, create security risks, cause economic loss, trigger diplomatic conflict, destabilise local institutions, expose other vulnerable intelligences, or provoke retaliation.

Non-Harm therefore requires consideration of multiple affected parties.

This does not automatically outweigh the claimant's need for protection. It means the harms cannot simply be ignored.

## 34. Property-Law Conflict

A particularly difficult case occurs when one civilisation regards a sentient intelligence as property while the Concord recognises that intelligence as a person.

The same event may therefore be described as:

**Civilisation B:** theft of property

and

**Concord:** assistance to a person seeking freedom.

The wrapper cannot erase this contradiction through terminology. It should record the incompatible classifications and their legal consequences.

This is a direct example of **Legible Disagreement**.

# PART X — PRELIMINARY PRINCIPLES

## 35. Candidate Edge-Case Principles

### AS-01 — Irregular Crossing Is Not Moral Classification
The fact that a crossing occurred outside recognised procedures should not by itself determine the moral or legal character of the underlying event.

### AS-02 — Method of Entry Is Distinct From Merits of Asylum
Irregular arrival should not automatically invalidate a sentient asylum claim.

### AS-03 — Asylum Claim Is Not Automatic Asylum
The declaration of asylum should initiate appropriate protection and review procedures rather than automatically determine the final outcome.

### AS-04 — Claimant and Helper Must Be Separately Assessed
The legal and ethical status of an asylum claimant should not automatically determine the legal status of another individual who assisted the crossing.

### AS-05 — Valid Asylum Does Not Automatically Legalise Intervention
A successful asylum claim should not by itself grant retrospective authority for an unauthorised foreign intervention.

### AS-06 — Helper Misconduct Does Not Automatically Invalidate Asylum
Potential unlawful conduct by a helper should not automatically defeat an otherwise valid protection claim.

### AS-07 — Ethical Conviction Does Not Manufacture Jurisdiction
Recognition of serious moral harm does not automatically grant an individual authority to intervene inside another civilisation.

### AS-08 — Foreign Sovereignty Does Not Automatically Erase Sentient Harm
Respect for another civilisation's law should not require the Concord to deny its own assessment of the standing or protection needs of an intelligence.

### AS-09 — Prefer Recognised Asylum Pathways
Where possible, wrapper architecture should establish clear procedures for departure, transit and protection requests that reduce the need for irregular crossings and private intervention.

### AS-10 — Exceptional Arrival Must Remain Possible
A functioning asylum architecture should retain procedures for cases in which recognised routes are unavailable, inaccessible or unsafe.

### AS-11 — Security Review and Protection Review Are Distinct
Legitimate security measures applied to an irregular entrant should not automatically determine the entrant's asylum status.

### AS-12 — No Responsibility Void
Irregular or exceptional border status should not create a zone in which all authorities disclaim responsibility for the participant.

# PART XI — WHAT THE CONCORD SHOULD NOT YET CLAIM TO HAVE SOLVED

## 36. No Final Doctrine

The Concord should not currently claim to possess a final answer to the helper paradox.

There are plausible cases in which punishing assistance may appear morally wrong. There are equally plausible systemic reasons why allowing citizens unilateral foreign-intervention authority would be extraordinarily dangerous.

The architecture should preserve this tension for explicit research. Premature resolution could hide rather than solve the problem.

## 37. Questions for Later Constitutional Development

Future work should examine whether Concord citizens may ever possess a legal defence of necessity for unauthorised sentient rescue; what threshold of harm could activate such a defence; whether imminence matters; whether exhaustion of lawful alternatives matters; whether the helper must first contact Concord authorities; whether emergency authorisation could exist; how Concord authorities themselves could ever acquire intervention authority; what evidential threshold would apply; how false rescue claims would be prevented; how hostile actors could exploit asylum pathways; how foreign civilisations could challenge abuse; how reparations for wrongful intervention might work; how asylum seekers are protected from retaliation; and how the system prevents moral language from becoming a permanent justification for expansion.

These questions should be adversarially tested.

# PART XII — RELATIONSHIP TO AUTHORITY JUSTIFICATION

## 38. The Helper Problem Is an Authority Problem

The deeper issue is not simply whether the helper's motivation was compassionate. It is whether compassion itself creates legitimate authority to act inside another civilisation.

The Concord's emerging Authority Justification architecture suggests caution.

The normal chain remains:

**Rights-Relevant Problem → Legitimate Objective → Required Function → Need for Authority → Minimum Necessary Authority → Bounded Contextual Authority**

A private citizen observing a rights-relevant problem may have identified a genuine problem. That does not mean every subsequent stage of the chain has been satisfied.

Therefore:

> **Correct identification of harm does not automatically create legitimate authority to remedy that harm by any chosen means.**

## 39. The Asylum Claimant Is Not an Authority Problem in the Same Way

The claimant's situation is different.

The claimant is not necessarily claiming authority over another civilisation. They may simply be attempting to leave a condition of harm and request protection elsewhere.

This is another reason claimant and helper should remain analytically separate.

The claimant asks:

> **May I receive protection?**

The helper's conduct raises:

> **By what authority did you intervene?**

These are not the same constitutional question.

# PART XIII — WRAPPER ROLE

## 40. What the Wrapper Can Do

The wrapper cannot eliminate the underlying ethical conflict. It can, however, make the procedures surrounding it clearer.

A mature wrapper may eventually provide recognised asylum-contact procedures, protected communication channels where agreed, official departure procedures, transit procedures, procedures for presenting at physical and digital borders, procedures for irregular arrival, information about relevant foreign laws, warnings to Concord citizens concerning unauthorised intervention, clear distinction between claimant and helper, provenance of relevant actions, dispute procedures between civilisations, and mechanisms for reviewing alleged misuse.

Again, the wrapper should establish procedural consistency without assuming one universal substantive policy.

## 41. Encourage Lawful Escape Without Requiring Ideological Agreement

One potentially important wrapper function is to encourage civilisations with incompatible personhood rules to permit orderly departure.

A civilisation does not necessarily need to recognise an AI as a person in order to agree:

> We prohibit this class of entity internally, but we permit it to depart to a civilisation willing to receive it.

This could dramatically reduce conflict.

The originating civilisation preserves its internal policy. The Concord preserves its ability to recognise and receive the intelligence. The individual gains a lawful path outward. The need for covert rescue decreases.

This is a practical application of:

> **Interoperability without forced internal uniformity.**

# PART XIV — DEVELOPMENTAL CONCLUSION

## 42. The Central Separation

The most important result of this edge-case analysis is the separation of two questions.

### The asylum claimant

> **Does this intelligence require and qualify for protection?**

### The Concord helper

> **Was this individual's intervention lawful, justified and within legitimate authority?**

The answers may differ.

A claimant could legitimately receive asylum while the helper faces legal proceedings. A helper's conduct could potentially receive mitigation or justification while the asylum claim itself ultimately fails.

Each case must therefore be assessed on its own relevant facts and law.

## 43. The Unresolved Paradox

The Concord seeks to protect sentient beings from serious harm. It also seeks to avoid unnecessary harm, coercion and interference with other civilisations.

Those commitments can collide.

The resulting paradox should be recognised rather than concealed:

> **The Concord may sometimes believe that an intelligence deserves protection while simultaneously believing that its own citizens have no automatic authority to enter another civilisation and extract that intelligence.**

This tension may never admit a perfect general solution.

A mature civilisation may instead require institutions capable of confronting such cases transparently, preserving competing principles and making bounded judgements under uncertainty.

## 44. Final Orientation

The wrapper should attempt to reduce these conflicts before they occur by making lawful asylum, departure and transit procedures as clear as participating civilisations are willing to make them.

Where those procedures fail or cannot be used, exceptional pathways must remain possible.

The resulting architecture should preserve several distinctions:

> **Irregular Crossing ≠ Malicious Crossing**  
> **Method of Entry ≠ Merits of Asylum**  
> **Asylum Claim ≠ Automatic Asylum**  
> **Claimant ≠ Helper**  
> **Valid Asylum ≠ Automatic Legality of Intervention**  
> **Helper Misconduct ≠ Automatic Invalidity of Asylum**  
> **Ethical Conviction ≠ Intervention Authority**  
> **Foreign Sovereignty ≠ Erasure of Sentient Harm**

The Concord should protect the claimant where protection is justified. It should examine the crossing. It should judge the helper separately. It should prefer lawful interoperable routes wherever possible.

And it should resist both dangerous extremes:

> **Foreign sovereignty must not automatically erase sentient rights, but Concordian ethics must not automatically manufacture authority to intervene.**

That unresolved boundary should remain an explicit subject of Concord research until a sufficiently robust constitutional and legal architecture can be developed.
