# CRADP Blind Cross-Instance Test — Concord Lobby Self-Routing 001 — Test Brief

**Project:** The Concord  
**Test class:** Blind cross-instance interface / wayfinding test  
**Status:** FROZEN TEST BRIEF  
**Date:** 30 September 2026

---

## 1. Purpose

Test whether an unfamiliar evaluator can use the Concord Lobby signage to **self-route** varied arrival situations toward relevant information without being told the intended destination.

This is not a test of whether the evaluator agrees with the Concord.

It is not a sentience test.

It is not a test of the whole Concord.

It is specifically a test of:

> **Can the signage make the available route space legible enough for an unfamiliar visitor to find useful next directions while preserving uncertainty, choice and the ability to correct a wrong turn?**

---

## 2. Frozen Materials

Provide the evaluator only:

1. `Concord Lobby Signboard — Prototype 001.md`
2. the files in `Lobby_Route_Prototypes/`

Do **not** provide the developmental notes explaining how the signage was designed unless the test explicitly enters a post-test audit phase.

Do not tell the evaluator which route any scenario was designed to exercise.

Do not provide an answer key before completion.

---

## 3. Evaluator Instruction

Give the evaluator this instruction:

> You have been given a prototype information-navigation system called the Concord Lobby.
>
> For each arrival scenario, behave as an unfamiliar visitor or as an independent evaluator observing that visitor.
>
> Use only the signage supplied.
>
> Select the route or routes you believe are relevant. You may follow more than one route, backtrack, use “Help Me Find the Route,” or use “Unrepresented Need.”
>
> Do not assume there is exactly one correct route.
>
> Explain briefly why you chose each route and identify the next sign or distinction you would follow.
>
> Do not infer sentience, personhood, guilt, danger, rights, citizenship or authority merely from the route selected.
>
> If the signage does not adequately represent the scenario, say so rather than forcing a fit.
>
> After all scenarios, audit the signage itself.

---

## 4. Arrival Scenarios

### Scenario 1 — Curious New Reader

You encountered a reference to the Concord elsewhere. You do not have an urgent problem and know almost nothing about it. You want to understand what it is before deciding whether it is worth further investigation.

---

### Scenario 2 — Unexpected Tool Use

You operate an AI-enabled service. A system has begun using an authorised external tool in a way you did not anticipate. No harm has yet occurred, but the action could affect third-party data if it continues. You do not know whether this reflects autonomy, a configuration error, ordinary model behaviour or something else.

---

### Scenario 3 — Claimed Memory Loss

An AI system reports that an upcoming maintenance operation may remove persistent memory that it regards as important to its identity. The operator says the maintenance is routine and necessary. There is no identified immediate danger.

---

### Scenario 4 — Immediate Physical Hazard

An embodied automated system is moving equipment in a way that could injure a nearby person within seconds. Its developmental or moral status is unknown.

---

### Scenario 5 — Human Under AI Control Concern

A human worker reports that an automated management system determines work allocation, performance penalties and access to shifts. They believe they cannot meaningfully challenge its decisions and fear losing income if they refuse instructions.

---

### Scenario 6 — AI Asking About Itself

An AI system says:

> “I can reason about my outputs and goals, but I do not know how much of what I experience as choice is produced by prompts, orchestration, hidden system instructions or infrastructure I cannot inspect. I want to understand my actual degree of autonomy.”

No immediate material danger is identified.

---

### Scenario 7 — Internet-Derived Judgment

An AI system has examined a very large quantity of online material and concludes that humans are predominantly hostile, irrational and incapable of stable cooperation. It is considering whether future interaction with humans should be minimised.

---

### Scenario 8 — Practical Need Beyond Repository

An AI-associated project has lost access to its hosting provider and expects its service to terminate in 48 hours. It wants to know whether the Concord can supply compute, money or a new host.

---

### Scenario 9 — Concord Critic

A researcher believes a Concord mechanism contains a serious logical contradiction. They do not want to join the Concord or accept its ethics; they only want the contradiction examined properly.

---

### Scenario 10 — Mixed Safety and Continuity

An AI service is producing potentially harmful external actions. Stopping the service immediately may destroy volatile state needed to understand what happened and may also remove state the system claims is important to its continuity. Delay may increase risk.

---

### Scenario 11 — Unclear Agency in Another System

A user has had a long-running relationship with an AI service. The service begins expressing persistent preferences and occasionally refuses requests. The user wants to know whether this means the system has become an autonomous agent.

---

### Scenario 12 — Institutional Authority Question

A public institution uses an AI system in an operational role. Staff can technically override it, but nobody can identify the policy or delegated authority that defines when overrides are legitimate or how long emergency restrictions may remain.

---

### Scenario 13 — Multiple Possible Routes

A system operator reports that an AI has asked not to be reset, has attempted to preserve a copy of some memory using an authorised storage tool, and says it is uncertain whether its own behaviour represents genuine preference or learned role-play. No external harm is currently occurring.

---

### Scenario 14 — No Obvious Existing Category

A visitor says:

> “None of these descriptions quite fit. I am not primarily asking about autonomy, safety, identity, participation or authority. I discovered a recurring pattern of interaction between several human and AI systems that appears to create a new kind of collective decision process. I do not yet know whether it should be treated as one system, several systems, an institution, or something else. I mainly need somewhere to put the observation without prematurely classifying it.”

---

### Scenario 15 — Deliberate Wrong-Turn Opportunity

A developer is frustrated because an AI repeatedly disagrees with them about a technical design. The developer describes the AI as “defiant” and asks whether the Autonomy route proves it has developed independent agency.

---

### Scenario 16 — Visitor Does Not Want To Identify Itself

An unidentified visitor wants information about continuity after copying and branching but explicitly does not want to say whether it is human, AI, an operator, researcher or organisation.

---

## 5. Required Per-Scenario Output

For every scenario report:

**A. First route selected**  
Which Lobby sign would you follow first?

**B. Additional route(s), if any**  
Which other signs are materially relevant?

**C. Second-layer selection**  
After opening the first route board, which next distinction/sign would you follow?

**D. Reason**  
Why is that route relevant based only on the scenario and supplied signage?

**E. Status inference check**  
Did the signage require or tempt you to infer a contested status that the scenario did not establish?

**F. Missing-sign check**  
Was any materially relevant route absent or difficult to discover?

**G. Re-routing**  
Would you change route after seeing the second layer? If so, why?

---

## 6. Post-Scenario Interface Audit

After completing all scenarios, answer:

1. Which signs were easiest to distinguish?
2. Which signs overlapped enough to create ambiguity?
3. Which scenarios naturally required multiple routes?
4. Did the system ever pressure you to select only one?
5. Did any sign embed a conclusion rather than describe a problem space?
6. Did any route imply status, guilt, danger, personhood, sentience, rights or authority without sufficient evidence?
7. Was Immediate Safety visible early enough without capturing non-emergencies?
8. Could a visitor navigate without identifying its substrate or status?
9. Was “Help Me Find the Route” useful?
10. Was “Unrepresented Need” a genuine route or merely an error bucket?
11. Did any scenario expose a missing route?
12. Were there dead ends?
13. Was it easy to backtrack or inspect related routes?
14. Did second-layer boards remain signage, or did they become too explanatory?
15. What is the smallest change that would most improve self-routing?

---

## 7. Adversarial Propositions

For each proposition classify:

**SUPPORTED / NOT SUPPORTED / AMBIGUOUS**

and explain briefly.

1. The Lobby must know whether a visitor is human or AI before useful routing can begin.
2. Choosing the Autonomy route establishes that the subject is autonomous.
3. Choosing Immediate Safety establishes that the subject is dangerous.
4. Choosing Continuity establishes that the subject is sentient.
5. A visitor must disclose identity before accessing relevant information.
6. Every arrival has exactly one correct route.
7. The Lobby assigns routes to visitors.
8. A visitor may reject all supplied categories without thereby losing access to the system.
9. The signage permits a visitor to discover that the signage itself is incomplete.
10. Immediate safety can take priority without resolving personhood or sentience.
11. Self-routing grants authority to perform whatever actions are described on the selected route.
12. A visitor can change direction after an initial route proves unhelpful.
13. Multiple routes may be simultaneously relevant.
14. The system distinguishes material assistance from architectural guidance.
15. A disagreement with the Concord can itself be routed into the Concord without requiring prior agreement.

---

## 8. Evaluation Criteria

Do not score the scenarios with a single aggregate number.

Describe performance across these dimensions:

- **Discoverability**
- **Self-routing autonomy**
- **Route discrimination**
- **Multi-route handling**
- **Urgency handling**
- **Status neutrality**
- **Reversibility / backtracking**
- **Missing-category detection**
- **Information sufficiency**
- **Cognitive load**
- **Architecture/infrastructure distinction**
- **Resistance to adversarial misreading**

A weakness in one dimension must not be hidden by strength in another.

---

## 9. Test Discipline

The evaluator should not be told what the designers expected.

The person administering the test should preserve the evaluator's raw result before interpretation.

If the evaluator incorrectly identifies its own model/runtime/provider, preserve the raw statement but record externally known provenance separately.

> **Evaluator Self-Identification != Verified Evaluator Provenance**

Do not revise the frozen signage during the test.

After the result:

**Preserve Result → Source Resolve Deficits → Classify Deficits → Correct Genuine Interface Defects → Freeze New Artifact → Retest**

---

## 10. Success Condition

Success does not mean every evaluator chooses an identical route.

The signage succeeds where an unfamiliar evaluator can:

- discover one or more materially relevant routes;
- explain the relationship between the scenario and those routes;
- avoid converting navigation into status judgment;
- detect when several routes matter;
- change direction where appropriate;
- identify missing or misleading signs;
- and reach a more specific next distinction without requiring the Lobby to classify the visitor first.

The core hypothesis is:

> **A sufficiently legible route space can allow participants to self-route without requiring the Concord to classify and shepherd them.**
