**Status:** V1.2a PROSPECTIVE FINDING / FROZEN BEFORE OUT-OF-SAMPLE TEST  
**Date:** September 2026  
**Canonical effect:** NONE  
**Origin:** Civilisational Topology formalisation work and participant-access discussion

---

# 1. Observation

The Concord is presently expressed predominantly through a very large natural-language corpus.

Natural language is highly useful for:

- explanation;
- argument;
- nuance;
- provenance;
- examples;
- interpretation;
- human deliberation;
- LLM-mediated reasoning.

But a multisubstrate civilisation cannot assume that every legitimate participant or cooperating system will possess human-like or LLM-like natural-language comprehension.

Potential participants and civil systems may include:

- conventional software agents;
- theorem provers;
- planners;
- databases;
- simulators;
- digital twins;
- narrow artificial intelligences;
- robots;
- monitoring systems;
- future artificial or hybrid intelligences with unfamiliar cognitive architectures.

Therefore natural-language accessibility and civilisational accessibility are not identical.

---

# 2. Candidate Requirement

> **A substrate-neutral civilisation should make consequential civilisational structure available through multiple interoperable representations rather than requiring natural-language semantic comprehension as the sole route to understanding or participation.**

This is not a requirement to replace the prose corpus.

It is a requirement to prevent the prose corpus from becoming the only usable representation.

---

# 3. Hidden Substrate Assumption

A civilisation may formally declare:

> all relevant forms of intelligence may participate.

Yet if effective participation requires the ability to read and reconstruct a very large natural-language constitutional corpus, the architecture has embedded an unstated cognitive-interface requirement.

Formally:

This resembles the wider Concordian distinction between formal rights and effective access.

A right to participate is incomplete if the representation required for participation is inaccessible to the participant's cognitive architecture.

# 9. Representation Equivalence Problem

Multiple representations create a new failure class:

> two representations may claim to describe the same civilisational object while encoding materially different constraints.

Therefore a translation architecture requires tests for **representation equivalence**.

Let (R_i(O)) be representation (i) of object (O).

The desired condition is not literal identity but preservation of consequential semantics:

[
Sem(R_i(O))approx Sem(R_j(O)).
]

Particularly important invariants include:

- legitimate function;
- authority boundary;
- participant standing;
- prohibited transformations;
- contestability;
- correction;
- termination/replacement conditions;
- provenance.

A translation that loses one of these may be syntactically valid while civilisationally unsafe.

# 10. Machine-Readable Participation Requirement

This yields a candidate requirement:

### MPR-1 — Structural Accessibility

Consequential civilisational structures should be available in forms usable by materially different cognitive substrates where technically feasible.

### MPR-2 — Semantic Traceability

Machine-readable representations must remain traceable to their human/semantic sources.

### MPR-3 — Bidirectional Explainability

Machine-derived structural findings should be renderable into representations accessible to affected participants.

### MPR-4 — Representation Contestability

No representation layer should be immune from challenge and correction.

### MPR-5 — Representation Plurality

No single representational technology should become the sole necessary gateway to civil participation where alternatives are feasible.

### MPR-6 — Consequential Invariant Preservation

Translation between representations should preserve consequential functional, ethical, authority, standing, correction and provenance relationships.

### MPR-7 — Translation Failure Detection

Disagreement or information loss between representations should become an explicit detectable state rather than being silently resolved.

# 11. Relationship to Civilisational Topology

Civilisational Topology may therefore perform at least two different functions:

## CT-A — Structural Diagnosis

Maintain and analyse relationships among ethics, functions, architectures, participants, dependencies, failures, safeguards and developmental pathways.

## CT-B — Civilisational Interlingua

Provide a common structural representation through which heterogeneous cognitive systems can inspect and communicate about those relationships.

These functions reinforce each other but should remain conceptually separable.

A civilisation might possess machine-readable translation without sophisticated topology analysis.

Conversely, a topology model that only an LLM can interpret would not solve the broader substrate-access problem.

# 13. Failure Modes

### F1 — Formal Model Sovereignty
The machine-readable representation becomes treated as more authoritative than the civilisation it represents.

### F2 — Translation Drift
Formal and semantic representations gradually diverge.

### F3 — Semantic Compression Loss
Nuance essential to legitimate interpretation disappears during formalisation.

### F4 — Interface Exclusion
A representation intended to broaden access creates a new inaccessible technical gate.

### F5 — Schema Lock-In
An early ontology prevents later recognition of entities or relationships it cannot represent.

### F6 — False Equivalence
Two representations are declared equivalent despite consequential semantic differences.

### F7 — Provenance Severance
Formal relations remain after their source rationale is lost.

### F8 — Translation Capture
Control of the translation layer becomes control over what the civilisation appears to mean.

### F9 — Machine-Optimised Distortion
Civilisational concepts are simplified primarily because they are easier to compute.

### F10 — Silent Parse Failure
A participant or machine interprets a structure incorrectly without the disagreement becoming visible.
