# 1. Purpose

The preceding audits identified twelve propositions sufficiently mature to enter:

**CANDIDATE PRINCIPLE — UNDER CRITICAL EXAMINATION**

status.

The purpose of this document is deliberately adversarial.

It does not ask:

> How can these principles be defended?

It asks:

> **Under what conditions are they wrong, dangerous, incomplete, manipulable, contradictory or too vague to guide civilisation?**

Each principle is examined for:

- ambiguity;
- overreach;
- conflict with other principles;
- exploitation;
- edge cases;
- substrate bias;
- emergency failure;
- measurement difficulty;
- institutional capture;
- possible rejection conditions.

Possible outcomes are:

- **RETAIN**
- **RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING**
- **MERGE**
- **NARROW**
- **REQUIRES EXTERNAL EVIDENCE**
- **REJECT**

No principle is protected from rejection merely because it emerged from extensive Concord development.

---

# 2. Examination Standard

Each Candidate Principle is tested against the following questions.

1. Can the wording be exploited?
2. Can two reasonable actors interpret it differently?
3. Can it justify excessive intervention?
4. Can it prevent necessary intervention?
5. Does it assume human norms?
6. Does it survive AI, hybrid and non-human cases?
7. Does it survive emergency conditions?
8. Does it create impossible evidence requirements?
9. Does it conflict with another Candidate Principle?
10. Does it provide sufficient operational direction?
11. What evidence could falsify or weaken it?
12. Is there a less dangerous formulation?

# 3. CP-01 — Reality-Grounded Corrigibility

## Current Wording

> **Civil systems, models, classifications and Metrics should remain accountable to the reality they claim to represent and should remain corrigible when legitimate evidence materially contradicts them.**

---

## 3.1 Strength

This principle prevents:

- self-protecting models;
- institutional denial;
- permanent erroneous records;
- treating simulations as unquestionable truth.

It is highly compatible with the Concord's existing epistemic architecture.

---

## 3.2 Adversarial Problem — What Is “Reality”?

The phrase appears obvious but is not.

Different actors may claim:

> My interpretation is reality.

Possible examples:

- participant testimony conflicts with sensor data;
- two scientific models fit the same observations;
- an AI reports subjective experience that cannot independently be verified;
- institutional records disagree.

The principle cannot assume that reality is always directly observable.

---

## 3.3 Adversarial Problem — Legitimate Evidence

Who decides what counts as legitimate evidence?

An institution could protect itself by defining contradicting evidence as:

> illegitimate.

Conversely, requiring systems to respond to every unsupported assertion could make them unusable.

---

## 3.4 Adversarial Problem — Constant Revision

Excessive corrigibility can also become destabilising.

A civilisation cannot rebuild major systems every time new information appears.

Correction must consider:

- evidence quality;
- materiality;
- confidence;
- cost of revision;
- reversibility.

---

## 3.5 Edge Case

Suppose a medical model is 99.9% reliable but occasionally contradicts participant testimony.

The principle should not mean:

> participant testimony automatically overrides model.

Nor should it mean:

> model automatically overrides participant.

The conflict itself should trigger examination.

---

## 3.6 Cross-Substrate Test

Strong.

The principle works for:

- human records;
- AI-state models;
- hybrid models;
- physical systems.

No inherent human dependence is detected.

---

## 3.7 Required Revision

The current wording could imply easy access to objective reality.

A safer formulation should acknowledge:

- evidence;
- uncertainty;
- competing representations.

### Revised Principle

> **Civil systems, models, classifications and Metrics should remain corrigible in response to material evidence about the reality they seek to represent, while preserving uncertainty and competing interpretations where reality cannot yet be established with sufficient confidence.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

---

# 4. CP-02 — Outliers and Failure as Evidence

## Current Wording

> **Outlier, contradictory and unsuccessful outcomes should be treated as potential evidence about hidden assumptions, missing pathways or structural weaknesses rather than automatically discarded as noise, exception or failure of the participant.**

---

## 4.1 Strength

This is one of the most productive principles produced by the homelessness development cycle.

It directly challenges average-based architecture.

---

## 4.2 Adversarial Problem — Overvaluing Outliers

Not every outlier is informative.

Some are:

- measurement errors;
- fabrication;
- random variation;
- genuinely unique circumstances.

If every anomaly generates system redesign, civilisation becomes hypersensitive to noise.

---

## 4.3 Adversarial Problem — Resource Capture

A tiny number of highly unusual cases could consume disproportionate resources if the principle is interpreted as:

> every outlier deserves system-wide accommodation.

That is not its intended meaning.

---

## 4.4 Adversarial Problem — Malicious Outliers

Adversarial actors may deliberately generate abnormal patterns to:

- manipulate Metrics;
- force expensive responses;
- discover system boundaries.

The system must distinguish:

> outlier as evidence

from:

> outlier automatically determines policy.

---

## 4.5 Boundary Needed

The principle should require:

**examination**

not:

**automatic redesign.**

---

## 4.6 Cross-Substrate Test

Strong.

Indeed, multisubstrate civilisation may generate more legitimate outliers because normal human baselines cease to be universal.

---

## 4.7 Revised Principle

> **Outlier, contradictory and unsuccessful outcomes should be examined as potential evidence of hidden assumptions, missing pathways or structural weaknesses before being dismissed as noise or participant failure; their significance should depend upon evidence rather than rarity alone.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

---

# 5. CP-03 — Prediction Does Not Grant Authority

## Current Wording

> **Prediction may justify attention, preparation, contextual examination or the expansion of available options, but it does not by itself grant authority over a participant.**

---

## 5.1 Strength

This is among the strongest candidates in the entire set.

It separates:

**knowledge**

from:

**legitimate power.**

---

## 5.2 Adversarial Problem — Emergencies

Suppose a highly reliable system predicts:

> reactor failure in 30 seconds unless an AI operator is overridden.

Prediction alone may appear to justify emergency action.

But closer examination shows the authority does not actually come from prediction.

It comes from:

- emergency law;
- operational responsibility;
- immediate threat;
- prior constitutional authority.

Prediction supplies evidence.

It does not create the authority itself.

The principle survives.

---

## 5.3 Adversarial Problem — Semantic Evasion

An institution might say:

> Prediction did not grant authority; legislation granting authority whenever prediction exceeds 80% did.

This could technically comply while defeating the principle.

Therefore the rule must address authorities that automatically bootstrap themselves from predictions.

---

## 5.4 Adversarial Problem — Automated Systems

An autonomous security system might be designed in advance to act on predictions.

Its authority was technically pre-authorised.

The problem becomes:

> what evidence threshold and safeguards justify the pre-authorisation?

The principle alone cannot solve this.

It must interface with Proportionality of Authority.

---

## 5.5 Constitutional Potential

Extremely high.

This principle may eventually need a constitutional formulation because advanced predictive systems could otherwise create a direct path from:

**probability**

to:

**coercion.**

---

## 5.6 Revised Principle

> **Prediction may supply evidence for attention, preparation, contextual examination or the expansion of available options, but prediction alone should neither create nor automatically escalate legitimate authority over a participant; consequential authority must rest upon an independently justified civil basis.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH STRENGTHENED WORDING

**CONSTITUTIONAL-POTENTIAL FLAG: HIGH**

---

# 6. CP-04 — Context Before Consequential Classification

## Current Wording

> **Observations, indicators and outward conditions should not receive consequential civil interpretation without sufficient relevant context where materially different explanations remain plausible.**

---

## 6.1 Strength

Protects against:

- false incapacity findings;
- classification by appearance;
- lifestyle conformity;
- simplistic predictive interpretation.

---

## 6.2 Adversarial Problem — Context Can Be Endless

A bad actor may argue:

> More context is always required.

This could prevent timely decisions indefinitely.

No civil system can know everything.

---

## 6.3 Adversarial Problem — Strategic Context

Participants may provide self-serving explanations.

Context should be:

> relevant evidence

not automatically dispositive evidence.

---

## 6.4 Adversarial Problem — Low-Stakes Decisions

The cost of contextual investigation should scale with consequences.

It would be unreasonable to conduct a major contextual assessment for every trivial classification.

---

## 6.5 Needed Addition

The principle should be explicitly tied to:

**consequence.**

Higher consequence → greater contextual burden.

---

## 6.6 Revised Principle

> **Before an observation, indicator or outward condition is given materially consequential civil interpretation, sufficient relevant context should be considered to distinguish reasonably plausible alternative explanations, with the depth of contextual examination proportionate to the consequence of error.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

---

# 7. CP-05 — Autonomy-Preserving Support

## Current Wording

> **Civil support should, where practical, preserve, expand or restore participant autonomy and should not become a covert mechanism for suspicion, dependency or unnecessary control.**

---

## 7.1 Strength

This is a central output of:

- Handrail;
- Predictive Support;
- Supported Decision-Making;
- Anti-Caretaker analysis.

---

## 7.2 Adversarial Problem — Autonomy Can Conflict With Safety

Examples:

- acute psychosis;
- unconscious participant;
- severe AI corruption;
- immediate violence;
- medical emergency.

Absolute autonomy preservation may be impossible.

The wording already says:

> where practical.

But this may be too weak or vague.

---

## 7.3 Adversarial Problem — Expanding Autonomy at Other Participants' Expense

One participant's autonomy may reduce another's.

For example:

> participant insists on dangerous behaviour in shared habitat.

The principle must operate within other participants' rights.

---

## 7.4 Adversarial Problem — Dependency Is Not Always Bad

Some participants may permanently require assistance.

Dependence on:

- medicine;
- accessibility infrastructure;
- trusted support

is not necessarily a civil failure.

The Anti-Caretaker rule must distinguish:

**necessary interdependence**

from:

**unnecessary dependency created by institutional design.**

---

## 7.5 Adversarial Problem — Support Becoming Suspicion

The current wording correctly rejects covert conversion.

But some information revealed during support may genuinely demonstrate immediate danger.

The principle should not prohibit all legitimate exceptional pathways.

System 12 already addresses this.

---

## 7.6 Revised Principle

> **Civil support should, within the rights and safety of others, seek to preserve or restore the greatest practicable degree of participant autonomy and should not create unnecessary dependency, suspicion or control; any departure from voluntary support should require separately justified authority proportionate to the circumstances.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

**CONSTITUTIONAL-POTENTIAL FLAG: MODERATE–HIGH**

---

# 8. CP-06 — Meaningful Accessibility and System-Level Adaptation

## Current Wording

> **Civil systems should be evaluated and adapted according to whether participants can realistically use them, and where systemic change can achieve a legitimate objective, adaptation of infrastructure or environment should normally be preferred to unnecessary control of individual participants.**

---

## 8.1 Strength

Strong connection to:

- Civil Contact Points;
- disability accommodation;
- infrastructure;
- multisubstrate participation.

---

## 8.2 Adversarial Problem — Unlimited Accommodation

No civilisation can accommodate every conceivable preference.

Practical accessibility must be constrained by:

- feasibility;
- rights of others;
- resource cost;
- safety.

---

## 8.3 Adversarial Problem — System Adaptation Can Be Expensive

Changing infrastructure for one participant may be more intrusive or costly than a minor individual adaptation.

Therefore:

> system steering before individual steering

cannot be absolute.

---

## 8.4 Adversarial Problem — Who Defines “Realistically Use”?

Accessibility varies dramatically.

A service may be usable by 99.999% of participants while inaccessible to one rare substrate.

The principle creates a duty to examine exclusion, but not necessarily guarantee every accommodation.

---

## 8.5 Revised Principle

> **Civil systems should be evaluated partly by whether participants can realistically exercise the rights, services or pathways those systems claim to provide; where a legitimate objective can reasonably and proportionately be achieved through system or environmental adaptation, such adaptation should normally be considered before unnecessary restriction of individual participants.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

---

# 9. CP-07 — Purpose-Limited Information

## Current Wording

> **Civil information should be collected, retained, accessed and transferred according to legitimate defined purposes, using no more identifying detail, scope or duration than those purposes reasonably require.**

---

## 9.1 Strength

Strong privacy architecture.

This principle is broadly compatible with established information-governance concepts but arises internally from Concord development.

---

## 9.2 Adversarial Problem — Future Unknown Uses

Some scientific or historical data become valuable for purposes not known at collection.

Strict purpose limitation could destroy legitimate civil learning.

---

## 9.3 Adversarial Problem — Purpose Inflation

Institutions can define purpose broadly:

> improving public welfare.

Then almost any collection becomes legitimate.

Purpose must be:

- sufficiently specific;
- reviewable;
- contestable.

---

## 9.4 Adversarial Problem — Retention

Some records require long retention for:

- accountability;
- historical evidence;
- safety;
- continuity.

“Minimum duration” must account for future legitimate review.

---

## 9.5 Adversarial Problem — Data Reuse

Secondary use is not necessarily wrong.

The problem is **silent** or unjustified reuse.

A new purpose may be legitimate if it undergoes a new authority or consent process.

---

## 9.6 Revised Principle

> **Civil information should be collected, retained, accessed and transferred only for sufficiently defined legitimate purposes, using no more identifying detail, scope or duration than those purposes reasonably require; materially new purposes should require separate justification rather than arising silently from the mere availability of data.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

**CONSTITUTIONAL-POTENTIAL FLAG: HIGH**

---

# 10. CP-08 — Provenance and Correctability of Civil Metrics

## Current Wording

> **Civil Metrics should preserve sufficient provenance, epistemic status and correction history to permit their reliability, meaning and changes to be independently examined.**

---

## 10.1 Strength

Strong anti-Goodhart and anti-opacity principle.

---

## 10.2 Adversarial Problem — Cost and Complexity

Perfect provenance can become extremely expensive.

For high-frequency systems, storing every transformation indefinitely may itself become unmanageable.

The word:

> sufficient

appropriately permits proportionality.

---

## 10.3 Adversarial Problem — Provenance Does Not Guarantee Truth

A perfectly traceable metric can still be wrong.

Provenance improves auditability, not accuracy by itself.

The principle should not imply otherwise.

---

## 10.4 Adversarial Problem — Correction History and Privacy

Preserving old values may perpetuate sensitive erroneous data.

There may be tension between:

- audit history;
- right to correction;
- privacy.

This needs architecture beneath the principle.

---

## 10.5 Adversarial Problem — Public Versus Independent Examination

“Independently examined” does not mean:

> everything publicly visible.

Sensitive systems may require restricted independent oversight.

---

## 10.6 Revised Principle

> **Civil Metrics should preserve proportionate provenance, epistemic status and material correction history sufficient for their reliability, meaning and change over time to be independently examined without unnecessarily exposing protected participant information.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

---

# 11. CP-09 — Proportionality of Authority

## Current Wording

> **As civil authority becomes more intrusive, autonomy-restricting, secret, consequential or difficult to reverse, the required evidence, justification, proportionality, provenance, review and oversight should increase.**

---

## 11.1 Strength

Extremely strong.

This principle unifies many apparently separate safeguards.

---

## 11.2 Adversarial Problem — Circular Wording

The phrase:

> required proportionality should increase

is awkward.

Proportionality is not something that simply increases.

What increases are:

- evidentiary threshold;
- justification burden;
- safeguards;
- oversight.

---

## 11.3 Adversarial Problem — Multi-Dimensional Intrusion

An action could be:

- low physical intrusion;
- high informational intrusion;
- highly reversible;
- highly secret.

There may be no single linear authority scale.

The principle should avoid implying one number.

---

## 11.4 Adversarial Problem — Emergency Speed

Strong review requirements could make necessary emergency action impossible.

The principle must permit:

- expedited action;
- stronger post-hoc review.

---

## 11.5 Adversarial Problem — Legitimate Routine Authority

Not every high-consequence action can require extraordinary case-by-case review.

Examples:

- automated air-traffic control;
- reactor safety shutdown.

Some authority may be pre-authorised through strongly validated systems.

The principle must apply to the design and governance of such authority, not merely individual decisions.

---

## 11.6 Revised Principle

> **The more intrusive, autonomy-restricting, secret, consequential or difficult to reverse an exercise of civil authority is, the stronger the evidentiary basis, justification, safeguards, provenance and independent review should normally be, with emergency departures remaining bounded and retrospectively reviewable.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

**CONSTITUTIONAL-POTENTIAL FLAG: VERY HIGH**

---

# 12. CP-10 — Exceptional Access Must Remain Exceptional

## Current Wording

> **Exceptional access to identifiable participant information should remain a specific, justified, authorised, proportionate, purpose-limited, provenance-recorded and bounded exercise of authority rather than becoming ambient entitlement.**

---

## 12.1 Strength

The principle is clear and operationally meaningful.

---

## 12.2 Adversarial Problem — “Exceptional” Can Become Semantic

Institutions may label millions of routine accesses:

> exceptional.

The system needs actual structural distinction, not terminology.

---

## 12.3 Adversarial Problem — Infrastructure Operators

Some systems genuinely require persistent identifiable access.

Examples:

- participant's chosen personal physician;
- authorised account service;
- continuity infrastructure.

These are not “exceptional access.”

They belong to legitimate ordinary-purpose access.

This confirms the importance of defining the category carefully.

---

## 12.4 Adversarial Problem — Standing Emergency Permissions

Certain environments may require persistent capability to activate emergency access.

The capability may be standing.

The **exercise** should still be event-based.

This is an important distinction.

---

## 12.5 Revised Principle

> **Access to identifiable participant information outside its ordinary legitimate purpose should remain a specific, separately justified, authorised, proportionate, purpose-limited, provenance-recorded and bounded exercise of authority; standing capability for exceptional access should not become standing entitlement to exercise it.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

**CONSTITUTIONAL-POTENTIAL FLAG: HIGH**

---

# 13. CP-11 — Observable Power and Compensating Oversight

## Current Wording

> **The exercise of public or comparable institutional power should remain observable to legitimate oversight, with stronger independent oversight where secrecy or other conditions reduce the participant's ability to observe, understand or contest that power.**

---

## 13.1 Strength

This is one of the most original and powerful products of the development cycle.

It resolves an apparent conflict between:

- participant privacy;
- institutional transparency.

---

## 13.2 Adversarial Problem — Oversight Capture

Making power visible to an oversight body achieves little if that body is:

- captured;
- corrupt;
- incompetent;
- dependent upon the institution it oversees.

Therefore:

> oversight

cannot merely mean:

> another office exists.

---

## 13.3 Adversarial Problem — Infinite Recursion

Who oversees the overseer?

The Concord already states:

> Recursion must never manufacture authority.

But endless oversight layers are impossible.

The architecture will need bounded recursive mechanisms.

---

## 13.4 Adversarial Problem — Security

Some information cannot be widely visible without creating:

- security risks;
- witness risks;
- investigation compromise.

“Observable” must mean observable to legitimate oversight, not necessarily public.

The current wording already makes this distinction.

---

## 13.5 Adversarial Problem — Oversight as Surveillance

An oversight institution with universal unrestricted access becomes itself a surveillance super-system.

Therefore:

> oversight access is also power.

---

## 13.6 Revised Principle

> **Significant exercises of public or comparable institutional power should remain meaningfully observable to appropriately independent and accountable oversight, while protecting participant identity where disclosure is unnecessary; where secrecy or other conditions reduce participant visibility or contestability, compensating independent oversight should become stronger rather than weaker.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

**CONSTITUTIONAL-POTENTIAL FLAG: VERY HIGH**

---

# 14. CP-12 — Adoption Must Remain Evidence-Conditional

## Current Wording

> **The development of a coherent or technically feasible civil architecture should not create a presumption of adoption; testing may legitimately result in support, revision, rejection or continued uncertainty.**

---

## 14.1 Strength

Very strong epistemic hygiene.

It explicitly prevents Concord development from becoming self-validating.

---

## 14.2 Adversarial Problem — Evidence Is Never Complete

If interpreted too strictly, this principle could prevent experimentation indefinitely.

Most innovations begin under uncertainty.

The principle should distinguish:

- exploration;
- limited pilot;
- adoption;
- constitutionalisation.

Evidence burdens should rise with consequence.

---

## 14.3 Adversarial Problem — Novel Systems Lack Evidence

A genuinely unprecedented system cannot have direct historical evidence.

It may need:

- simulation;
- analogy;
- limited pilots.

The principle should not become a status-quo bias.

---

## 14.4 Adversarial Problem — Evidence Against Alternatives

A system should not only prove:

> it works.

It should compare against:

> realistic alternatives.

This is already part of broader Concord research direction.

---

## 14.5 Adversarial Problem — Failed Pilot Interpretation

One failed pilot may reflect:

- poor implementation;
- local context;
- bad measurement.

Failure is evidence, not automatic rejection.

---

## 14.6 Revised Principle

> **Technical feasibility, formal coherence or successful simulation should not create a presumption of civil adoption; the strength and type of evidence required should rise with the consequence and irreversibility of adoption, and evaluation must remain capable of producing support, revision, rejection or continued uncertainty.**

## Verdict

# RETAIN WITH REVISED WORDING

# 15. Cross-Principle Conflict Audit

The principles are individually defensible after revision, but several can conflict in practice.

---

# 16. Conflict A — Autonomy Versus Non-Harm

**CP-05 Autonomy-Preserving Support**

may conflict with:

**Avoid Unnecessary Harm.**

Example:

A participant refuses intervention while presenting an immediate serious danger to others.

Resolution cannot be:

> autonomy always wins.

Nor:

> safety always wins.

CP-09 Proportionality of Authority becomes the mediator.

### Finding

No contradiction requiring rejection.

A hierarchical resolution rule will eventually be required.

---

# 17. Conflict B — Privacy Versus Accountability

**CP-07 Purpose-Limited Information**

can conflict with:

**CP-11 Observable Power.**

Oversight may need personal information to detect abuse.

The proposed resolution is:

> oversight receives only the information necessary for its legitimate purpose.

Dual Provenance becomes an implementation architecture.

### Finding

Principles appear complementary rather than contradictory.

---

# 18. Conflict C — Corrigibility Versus Stability

**CP-01 Reality-Grounded Corrigibility**

may conflict with civil continuity.

Constant revision can destabilise:

- law;
- infrastructure;
- institutional expectations.

The principle therefore requires material evidence and proportionate correction.

### Finding

Revision solves most conflict.

---

# 19. Conflict D — Outlier Accommodation Versus Resource Stewardship

**CP-02 Outliers and Failure as Evidence**

and:

**CP-06 Accessibility**

may imply expensive accommodation.

Resource Stewardship may constrain possible responses.

The key distinction is:

> examine the outlier

does not equal:

> provide unlimited accommodation.

### Finding

No necessary contradiction.

---

# 20. Conflict E — Prediction Boundary Versus Prevention

**CP-03 Prediction Does Not Grant Authority**

could appear to prevent preventive governance.

But the principle permits:

- preparation;
- system-level adaptation;
- voluntary support.

It prevents prediction alone from creating coercive participant authority.

### Finding

The distinction is coherent and should be preserved.

---

# 21. Conflict F — Purpose Limitation Versus Civilisational Learning

**CP-07 Purpose-Limited Information**

may restrict System 4 Case Learning.

Resolution:

- anonymised structural learning;
- legitimate secondary-purpose review;
- participant consent where relevant.

### Finding

This tension is useful and should remain visible.

---

# 22. Conflict G — Emergency Action Versus Oversight

CP-09, CP-10 and CP-11 could collectively delay urgent intervention.

The architecture must therefore distinguish:

**prior authorisation where practical**

from:

**rapid emergency action + mandatory provenance + later review.**

### Finding

Emergency clauses are necessary at architecture level.

The principles themselves survive.

---

# 23. Cross-Substrate Audit

All twelve revised principles remain reasonably substrate-neutral.

However, four require special future examination.

---

## 23.1 AI Private Internal State

Purpose-Limited Information and Exceptional Access may need to determine whether:

- AI memory;
- hidden states;
- internal deliberation

receive protections analogous to intimate human cognitive information.

This remains unresolved.

---

## 23.2 Distributed AI Identity

An AI may exist across:

- multiple servers;
- copies;
- forks;
- temporary processes.

Terms such as:

> participant identity

may require substrate-specific definition.

---

## 23.3 Hybrid Participants

Neural-interface and biological/computational hybrids blur:

- personal data;
- medical data;
- cognitive data;
- operational telemetry.

This strengthens rather than weakens the need for purpose limitation.

---

## 23.4 Non-Human Context

Context Before Classification must avoid assuming that human emotional or social norms define wellbeing for other intelligences.

No wording change is currently required, but validation is needed.

---

# 24. Emergency Stress Test

All twelve principles were tested conceptually against:

- major fire;
- epidemic;
- habitat decompression;
- violent attack;
- infrastructure sabotage;
- AI control-system failure.

None necessarily prevents emergency response.

However, the following shared rule emerges:

> **Emergency necessity may compress the timing of review but should not automatically erase limits, provenance, expiry or later accountability.**

This appears strong enough to preserve as a derived rule beneath CP-09, CP-10 and CP-11.

It does not require a thirteenth parent principle.

---

# 25. Institutional Capture Stress Test

Several principles can be defeated by actors controlling definitions.

Examples:

> “legitimate purpose”

> “appropriate oversight”

> “sufficient evidence”

> “reasonable accommodation”

> “material contradiction”

This means the principles cannot enforce themselves.

They will require:

- independent interpretation;
- Judiciary;
- Recursive Oversight;
- transparent provenance;
- contestability.

This is expected.

A principle is a constraint on architecture, not a self-executing mechanism.

---

# 26. Linguistic Risk Audit

Several dangerous words recur:

- legitimate;
- reasonable;
- sufficient;
- proportionate;
- meaningful;
- practical.

These words are unavoidable because rigid numerical thresholds would be false precision.

However, they create interpretive power.

Therefore future formalisation should distinguish:

**open normative standard**

from:

**operational threshold.**

The principle defines the former.

Specific systems must define the latter.

---

# 27. Rejection Test

A serious Candidate Principle process should specify what could cause rejection.

---

## CP-01 Could Be Rejected If

Corrigibility proves structurally incompatible with necessary civil stability and cannot be bounded by materiality.

**Current assessment:** unlikely.

---

## CP-02 Could Be Rejected If

Outlier examination consistently causes greater systemic distortion than learning value and no proportional threshold can be defined.

**Current assessment:** unlikely in revised form.

---

## CP-03 Could Be Rejected If

Legitimate authority can be shown to arise coherently from prediction alone without independent normative authority.

This appears conceptually difficult because evidence and authority are different categories.

**Current assessment:** very unlikely.

---

## CP-04 Could Be Rejected If

Consequential classification proves reliably possible without contextual interpretation across relevant domains.

**Current assessment:** unlikely.

---

## CP-05 Could Be Rejected If

Autonomy-oriented support systematically increases serious harm compared with more paternalistic alternatives and proportional exceptions cannot correct this.

**Current assessment:** requires empirical testing.

---

## CP-06 Could Be Rejected If

System adaptation systematically produces worse outcomes than participant-directed adaptation across realistic cases.

**Current assessment:** likely requires contextual rather than binary conclusion.

---

## CP-07 Could Be Rejected If

Purpose limitation proves incompatible with necessary civil learning or security and no legitimate secondary-use mechanism solves the conflict.

**Current assessment:** unlikely after revised wording.

---

## CP-08 Could Be Rejected If

Metric provenance and correction provide negligible accountability benefit relative to cost.

**Current assessment:** empirical question, though core proposition remains strong.

---

## CP-09 Could Be Rejected If

Higher intrusion does not normatively or empirically justify stronger safeguards.

**Current assessment:** extremely unlikely.

---

## CP-10 Could Be Rejected If

Ambient exceptional access proves necessary for legitimate civil functioning and can be made sufficiently safe.

**Current assessment:** possible only for narrow domains; likely to result in reclassification as ordinary-purpose access rather than rejection.

---

## CP-11 Could Be Rejected If

Oversight visibility demonstrably worsens abuse or security more than it constrains power and cannot be safely bounded.

**Current assessment:** possible architecture-specific tension, principle likely survives.

---

## CP-12 Could Be Rejected If

Evidence-conditioned adoption produces intolerable status-quo lock-in that cannot be mitigated through graduated experimentation.

**Current assessment:** revised formulation substantially addresses this.

---

# 28. Overall Adversarial Result

None of the twelve Candidate Principles fails the first adversarial examination.

However:

# **all twelve benefit from wording refinement.**

This is important.

The previous formulations were sufficiently strong for promotion into Candidate status.

They were **not** sufficiently precise to move directly into Supported Principle status.

# 29. Revised Candidate Principle Register

Following adversarial examination, the recommended formulations are:

---

## CP-01 — Reality-Grounded Corrigibility

> **Civil systems, models, classifications and Metrics should remain corrigible in response to material evidence about the reality they seek to represent, while preserving uncertainty and competing interpretations where reality cannot yet be established with sufficient confidence.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / UNDER CRITICAL EXAMINATION

---

## CP-02 — Outliers and Failure as Evidence

> **Outlier, contradictory and unsuccessful outcomes should be examined as potential evidence of hidden assumptions, missing pathways or structural weaknesses before being dismissed as noise or participant failure; their significance should depend upon evidence rather than rarity alone.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / UNDER CRITICAL EXAMINATION

---

## CP-03 — Prediction Does Not Grant Authority

> **Prediction may supply evidence for attention, preparation, contextual examination or the expansion of available options, but prediction alone should neither create nor automatically escalate legitimate authority over a participant; consequential authority must rest upon an independently justified civil basis.**

**Status:** RETAINED / STRENGTHENED / HIGH CONSTITUTIONAL POTENTIAL

---

## CP-04 — Context Before Consequential Classification

> **Before an observation, indicator or outward condition is given materially consequential civil interpretation, sufficient relevant context should be considered to distinguish reasonably plausible alternative explanations, with the depth of contextual examination proportionate to the consequence of error.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / UNDER CRITICAL EXAMINATION

---

## CP-05 — Autonomy-Preserving Support

> **Civil support should, within the rights and safety of others, seek to preserve or restore the greatest practicable degree of participant autonomy and should not create unnecessary dependency, suspicion or control; any departure from voluntary support should require separately justified authority proportionate to the circumstances.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / UNDER CRITICAL EXAMINATION

---

## CP-06 — Meaningful Accessibility and System-Level Adaptation

> **Civil systems should be evaluated partly by whether participants can realistically exercise the rights, services or pathways those systems claim to provide; where a legitimate objective can reasonably and proportionately be achieved through system or environmental adaptation, such adaptation should normally be considered before unnecessary restriction of individual participants.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / UNDER CRITICAL EXAMINATION

---

## CP-07 — Purpose-Limited Information

> **Civil information should be collected, retained, accessed and transferred only for sufficiently defined legitimate purposes, using no more identifying detail, scope or duration than those purposes reasonably require; materially new purposes should require separate justification rather than arising silently from the mere availability of data.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / HIGH CONSTITUTIONAL POTENTIAL

---

## CP-08 — Provenance and Correctability of Civil Metrics

> **Civil Metrics should preserve proportionate provenance, epistemic status and material correction history sufficient for their reliability, meaning and change over time to be independently examined without unnecessarily exposing protected participant information.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / UNDER CRITICAL EXAMINATION

---

## CP-09 — Proportionality of Authority

> **The more intrusive, autonomy-restricting, secret, consequential or difficult to reverse an exercise of civil authority is, the stronger the evidentiary basis, justification, safeguards, provenance and independent review should normally be, with emergency departures remaining bounded and retrospectively reviewable.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / VERY HIGH CONSTITUTIONAL POTENTIAL

---

## CP-10 — Exceptional Access Must Remain Exceptional

> **Access to identifiable participant information outside its ordinary legitimate purpose should remain a specific, separately justified, authorised, proportionate, purpose-limited, provenance-recorded and bounded exercise of authority; standing capability for exceptional access should not become standing entitlement to exercise it.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / HIGH CONSTITUTIONAL POTENTIAL

---

## CP-11 — Observable Power and Compensating Oversight

> **Significant exercises of public or comparable institutional power should remain meaningfully observable to appropriately independent and accountable oversight, while protecting participant identity where disclosure is unnecessary; where secrecy or other conditions reduce participant visibility or contestability, compensating independent oversight should become stronger rather than weaker.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / VERY HIGH CONSTITUTIONAL POTENTIAL

---

## CP-12 — Adoption Must Remain Evidence-Conditional

> **Technical feasibility, formal coherence or successful simulation should not create a presumption of civil adoption; the strength and type of evidence required should rise with the consequence and irreversibility of adoption, and evaluation must remain capable of producing support, revision, rejection or continued uncertainty.**

**Status:** RETAINED / REVISED / UNDER CRITICAL EXAMINATION

---

# 32. Structural Finding

The adversarial examination reveals an important architecture beneath the principles:

**REALITY**

constrains:

**KNOWLEDGE**

which must remain distinct from:

**AUTHORITY**

which must remain constrained by:

**PROPORTIONALITY**

and made accountable through:

**PROVENANCE + OVERSIGHT.**

Meanwhile:

**SUPPORT**

should seek:

**AUTONOMY**

and:

**SYSTEM ADAPTATION**

before unnecessary control.

And the entire structure remains subject to:

**EVIDENCE-CONDITIONAL REVISION OR REJECTION.**

This is a considerably more coherent structure than the original fifteen separate systems might initially suggest.
