# 1. Purpose

This paper attempts to break the proposed **Authority Justification Chain**.

It is not written to defend the chain.

Its purpose is to identify circumstances in which an apparently complete chain could still manufacture, preserve or expand illegitimate authority.

The current candidate chain is:

\[
ObservedCondition
\rightarrow
RightsRelevantProblem
\rightarrow
LegitimateCivilObjective
\rightarrow
RequiredFunction
\rightarrow
FunctionalNeedForAuthority
\rightarrow
MinimumProportionateAuthority
\rightarrow
BoundedContextualAuthority
\]

The broader constitutional direction is:

\[
EthicalKernel
\rightarrow
Rights
\rightarrow
LegitimateCivilObjective
\rightarrow
LegitimateFunction
\rightarrow
FunctionalNeed
\rightarrow
BoundedContextualAuthority
\]

The central proposition under attack is:

> **Authority should primarily derive from a legitimate functional need. Authority itself does not make a function legitimate and does not prove that the authority is necessary.**

The falsification question is:

> **Can this chain prevent power from manufacturing the evidence, problem, objective, function, need, scope or validation that supposedly justifies that power?**

A chain can appear non-circular while remaining circular in practice.

Therefore this audit does not ask merely whether each step can be described.

It asks whether the steps remain independently contestable.

---

# 2. What Would Count as Failure?

The chain should be considered seriously weakened if one or more of the following are true:

1. an authority holder can define the observed condition in a way that predetermines the rest of the chain;
2. ordinary disagreement can be relabelled as a rights-relevant problem without independent constraint;
3. an institution can convert its own survival or convenience into a legitimate civil objective;
4. a legitimate objective can be used to justify any preferred function;
5. a legitimate function can automatically justify special authority;
6. the authority claimant can define the minimum authority necessary for itself;
7. emergency, uncertainty or secrecy can routinely bypass the chain;
8. validation of the chain requires an unchecked meta-authority;
9. individually proportionate authority can aggregate into systemic domination;
10. the chain is too administratively heavy to work in ordinary life;
11. the chain is too permissive to constrain high-impact power;
12. retrospective success can be used to legitimate authority that lacked prospective justification;
13. the chain cannot handle genuine action under severe uncertainty;
14. the framework creates paralysis where rapid intervention is necessary;
15. institutional actors can game the vocabulary while preserving the same underlying power.

The strongest possible result would not be that the chain survives every attack unchanged.

A successful audit may instead show that the chain survives only after additional constraints are made explicit.

# 4. Attack Class A — Manipulating the Observed Condition

## A1 — Selective Observation

An institution records only the indicators that support intervention.

Example:

A public-order agency measures reported disturbances but not peaceful non-conforming activity. The dataset then makes an entire subculture appear disproportionately dangerous.

The condition may be factually recorded while structurally incomplete.

\[
TrueData \neq CompleteRepresentation
\]

**Finding:** the chain requires an explicit distinction between factual accuracy and representational adequacy.

Candidate refinement:

> **A materially consequential condition should be described using evidence sufficient to expose plausible counterevidence, denominator effects and omitted context.**

---

## A2 — Measurement Created by the Authority

A surveillance programme detects more violations because it observes more people.

The institution then cites the increased violation count as evidence that more surveillance is necessary.

\[
Surveillance
\rightarrow
MoreDetection
\rightarrow
ClaimedGreaterProblem
\rightarrow
MoreSurveillance
\]

This is not simple logical circularity. It is **measurement feedback**.

**Finding:** the original chain is vulnerable unless it asks whether authority materially produced the evidence used to justify itself.

Candidate rule:

\[
AuthorityGeneratedEvidence
\rightarrow
IndependentCausalReview
\]

---

## A3 — Proxy Substitution

A measurable proxy replaces the actual condition.

Examples:

- reported complaints substitute for harm;
- low participation substitutes for exclusion;
- dissent substitutes for instability;
- high resource use substitutes for waste;
- unusual behaviour substitutes for danger.

The chain can remain formally complete while the first object is wrong.

**Finding:** the first stage needs a proxy-validity test.

\[
Proxy \neq UnderlyingCondition
\]

---

## A4 — False Baseline

An institution compares current conditions against a baseline that already embeds inequality or dysfunction.

The absence of change is then interpreted as normality.

**Finding:** “observed condition” cannot mean merely deviation from historical normal.

\[
HistoricalNormality \neq Legitimacy
\]

---

## A5 — Invisible Harm

Some harms are systematically under-reported because affected participants lack access, trust, communication ability or recognised status.

A purely evidence-demanding system could therefore conclude there is no problem.

**Finding:** strong evidence thresholds can protect power by making unseen harms impossible to recognise.

The chain needs a route for **precautionary investigation without premature authority**.

---

# 5. Attack Class B — Turning Conditions into Rights-Relevant Problems

## B1 — Moralisation of Difference

A condition is real but not a civil problem.

Examples:

- unconventional dress;
- minority language;
- unusual AI embodiment;
- refusal to participate in leisure;
- unconventional family structure;
- unusual cognitive style.

A majority may dislike the condition.

That does not make it rights-relevant.

\[
Difference \neq Harm
\]

\[
Offence \neq RightsViolation
\]

**Finding:** this transition must independently establish a rights-relevant external effect rather than social discomfort.

---

## B2 — Institutional Inconvenience

An agency experiences inefficiency because participants do not conform to its preferred process.

The inconvenience is relabelled as a public problem.

Example:

A service is easier to administer if everyone uses one identity credential, one communication channel or one interface.

Administrative convenience then becomes justification for universal standardisation.

**Finding:** institutional friction is not automatically a civil problem.

\[
AdministrativeConvenience \neq RightsRelevantProblem
\]

---

## B3 — Manufactured Non-Compliance

An institution imposes a difficult rule.

Participants fail to comply.

Their non-compliance is then treated as the problem that justifies stronger enforcement.

\[
Restriction
\rightarrow
NonCompliance
\rightarrow
ClaimedInstability
\rightarrow
MoreRestriction
\]

**Finding:** the chain must ask whether the alleged problem is endogenous to prior authority.

---

## B4 — Future Harm Inflation

Very low-probability speculative harms can be framed as rights-relevant because the possible consequence is severe.

If no probability or uncertainty discipline exists, almost anything can become a preventive-authority claim.

**Finding:** rights relevance alone is insufficient for high-impact preventive intervention.

Need:

\[
Severity + Probability + Uncertainty + Reversibility
\]

not severity alone.

---

## B5 — Rights Collision Misclassification

Two participants have legitimate but incompatible claims.

The system describes one participant as “the problem”.

This silently converts a conflict requiring balancing into a unilateral intervention case.

**Finding:** the chain needs a classification:

\[
RightsConflict \neq WrongdoerProblem
\]

Some conditions require coordination or adjudication rather than authority against one party.

---

# 6. Attack Class C — Laundering Institutional Interest into Civil Objective

## C1 — Institutional Survival

A legitimate institution claims:

> “Maintaining this institution is necessary for stability.”

Its continued existence becomes the objective.

The function becomes preserving itself.

The authority then follows from the function.

This creates a closed institutional loop:

\[
Institution
\rightarrow
InstitutionalSurvivalObjective
\rightarrow
PreservationFunction
\rightarrow
Authority
\rightarrow
Institution
\]

**Finding:** institutional continuity can be instrumentally valuable but is not a self-standing civil objective.

\[
InstitutionalInterest \neq LegitimateCivilObjective
\]

---

## C2 — Mission Preservation After Function Obsolescence

A function was once necessary.

Technology or social conditions eliminate the need.

The institution reframes its original mission more broadly to preserve authority.

Example:

A licensing authority created for physical safety expands into monitoring general professional conduct after automated systems remove the original technical risk.

**Finding:** legitimacy must attach to present objective and function, not historical mission.

---

## C3 — Objective Laundering

A preferred policy is redescribed using universally attractive language:

- safety;
- dignity;
- stability;
- fairness;
- flourishing;
- resilience;
- misinformation prevention.

The objective sounds legitimate, but its operational meaning remains undefined.

**Finding:** abstract ethical vocabulary cannot itself establish a legitimate objective.

Candidate requirement:

> **High-impact civil objectives should be operationally specific enough to permit evidence of failure, excess and completion.**

---

## C4 — Single-Metric Objective

The objective is legitimate in isolation but becomes illegitimate when maximised without regard to competing rights.

Examples:

- maximise safety;
- minimise crime;
- maximise health;
- maximise productivity;
- maximise information accuracy.

\[
LegitimateObjective \neq UnlimitedOptimisationMandate
\]

**Finding:** a legitimate civil objective must remain rights-bounded.

---

## C5 — Majority Preference as Objective

A large majority prefers a particular social arrangement.

The preference becomes “social cohesion”.

Authority is used against a lawful minority.

**Finding:** aggregation cannot transform preference into civil legitimacy.

\[
MajorityPreference \not\Rightarrow LegitimateCoerciveObjective
\]

---

# 7. Attack Class D — From Objective to Function

## D1 — One Objective, Many Possible Functions

A legitimate objective can be served by multiple functions.

Example:

Objective: reduce road deaths.

Possible functions:

- safer infrastructure;
- better vehicle design;
- driver education;
- speed enforcement;
- autonomous collision prevention;
- public transport alternatives.

Choosing enforcement is not dictated by the objective.

\[
LegitimateObjective \not\Rightarrow PreferredFunction
\]

**Finding:** the chain requires an alternative-function test before a function is called “required”.

---

## D2 — Function Chosen Because Institution Already Exists

A government already has police, regulators or data systems.

A new objective is assigned to whichever institution is available.

The existing tool shapes the perceived function.

**Finding:** institutional availability should not substitute for functional necessity.

---

## D3 — Function Creep

A function begins narrowly but gradually absorbs adjacent objectives.

Example:

“Verify eligibility for benefit” becomes “monitor general financial behaviour”.

**Finding:** function definitions require bounded purpose and anti-expansion review.

---

## D4 — Function as a Verb Without Boundaries

Terms such as “protect”, “manage”, “coordinate”, “secure”, “support” or “ensure” are too broad to constrain authority.

**Finding:** a legitimate function must specify object, purpose, scope and affected parties sufficiently to test necessity.

---

## D5 — Function That Cannot Actually Achieve Objective

A function sounds relevant but has weak causal connection to the objective.

Example:

broad internet monitoring is justified as reducing offline violence despite weak evidence that the monitoring intervention changes relevant behaviour.

**Finding:** function legitimacy requires not merely ethical purpose but plausible causal effectiveness.

\[
GoodObjective + IneffectiveFunction \not\Rightarrow LegitimateAuthority
\]

---

# 8. Attack Class E — From Function to Need for Authority

This may be one of the strongest parts of the proposed chain because it explicitly separates legitimate function from special authority.

It is also highly vulnerable in practice.

## E1 — Authority by Habit

A function has always been performed by an authority-bearing institution.

The institution assumes special authority is intrinsic to the function.

Example:

a civil office assumes it must compel information because historically it always has.

**Finding:** historical implementation is not proof of necessity.

---

## E2 — Voluntary Alternative Ignored

The function could be achieved through:

- consent;
- voluntary service;
- public information;
- open infrastructure;
- contract;
- opt-in coordination;
- user-controlled credentials.

Special authority is preferred because it is administratively easier.

**Finding:** the chain survives only if alternatives are genuinely examined.

---

## E3 — Free-Rider Problem

Some legitimate functions fail if participation is voluntary.

Examples may include:

- taxation;
- collective defence;
- infectious-disease control;
- shared infrastructure;
- pollution limits.

This is a real challenge to a simplistic consent-first model.

**Finding:** the chain must distinguish:

\[
VoluntaryAlternativeExists
\]

from:

\[
VoluntaryAlternativeCanReliablyPerformFunction
\]

A formally less coercive option may be functionally inadequate.

---

## E4 — Coordination Threshold

A function may require authority only after a participation threshold is crossed.

For example, evacuation may be voluntary initially but obstruction of a single access route may require temporary control.

**Finding:** authority necessity can be dynamic rather than binary.

\[
ContextChange \rightarrow AuthorityNeedChange
\]

---

## E5 — Power as Insurance

An institution may rarely need a power but must possess readiness to act immediately.

This creates **standing eligibility** without continuous activated authority.

The existing BCA distinction helps:

\[
StandingEligibility \neq ActivatedAuthority
\]

**Finding:** the chain survives this attack if dormant capability is separated from currently exercisable power.

---

## E6 — Information Authority

The function itself may be legitimate, but obtaining the information needed to perform it intrudes on rights.

Example:

protecting a vulnerable participant may require evidence that is itself private.

**Finding:** “authority needed” must be decomposed by type:

\[
PhysicalAuthority
\neq
InformationAuthority
\neq
DecisionAuthority
\neq
DisclosureAuthority
\]

---

# 9. Attack Class F — Minimum Proportionate Authority

## F1 — Minimum Means Ineffective

The phrase “minimum authority” can be misunderstood as the weakest imaginable intervention rather than the least rights-burdensome authority reasonably capable of performing the function.

A powerless authority is not proportionate if it predictably fails.

**Finding:** the appropriate test is:

\[
MinimumEffectiveReasonableAuthority
\]

not merely:

\[
MinimumAuthority
\]

Candidate refinement:

> **Authority should be no greater than reasonably necessary, but sufficient to perform the legitimate function under the actual conditions.**

---

## F2 — Authority Holder Defines Minimum

If the actor requesting authority also defines what is necessary, the minimum becomes self-certifying.

**Finding:** higher-impact authority requires independent or plural validation.

---

## F3 — Local Minimum, Systemic Maximum

Each individual intervention is proportionate.

Repeated across millions of cases, the combined system becomes oppressive.

Example:

each data request is individually narrow, but the aggregate reveals nearly complete participant activity.

\[
LocalProportionality \neq SystemicProportionality
\]

**Finding:** an aggregation audit is necessary.

---

## F4 — Cumulative Burden on One Group

Each restriction is individually neutral and justified.

One substrate, disability group, profession or minority repeatedly bears the burden.

No single decision appears discriminatory.

The system as a whole is.

**Finding:** proportionality requires distributional review.

---

## F5 — Irreversible Authority

An intervention may be narrow but irreversible.

Examples:

- deletion;
- permanent cognitive modification;
- forced sterilisation;
- irreversible asset destruction;
- irreversible migration or shutdown.

**Finding:** irreversibility raises the justification threshold independently of scope.

\[
Irreversibility\uparrow
\Rightarrow
RequiredJustification\uparrow
\]

---

## F6 — Time Pressure

A slower, less intrusive intervention may be theoretically available but not in time.

**Finding:** proportionality must include temporal feasibility.

---

# 10. Attack Class G — Bounded Contextual Authority

## G1 — Context Manipulation

The actor defines the context broadly enough that nearly any action becomes functionally related.

**Finding:** context must be independently describable and reviewable.

---

## G2 — Context Persistence

The function ends but the contextual label remains.

Example:

a “heightened threat environment” persists indefinitely.

**Finding:** functional sunset is stronger than clock sunset, but ambiguous continuing functions require periodic independent review.

---

## G3 — Successor Function Ratchet

Original function ends.

A successor function is declared.

Authority persists with only a new label.

**Finding:** successor functions require fresh scoping rather than inheritance.

---

## G4 — Bundled Functions

An office performs multiple legitimate functions.

Authority from one function silently migrates to another.

**Finding:**

\[
Authority_A \not\Rightarrow Authority_B
\]

even where the same actor performs both.

---

## G5 — Delegation Expansion

A delegate interprets broad language more aggressively than the delegator.

**Finding:**

\[
DelegatedAuthority \leq DelegatorLegitimateAuthority
\]

must include inherited constraints, purpose and sunset.

---

# 11. Attack Class H — Emergency and Severe Uncertainty

## H1 — Action Before Evidence

A chemical plume may kill thousands within minutes.

The evidence needed to establish the complete Authority Justification Chain is unavailable.

Waiting for validation may itself violate rights.

**Finding:** the chain cannot require full evidential completion before all action.

It needs a **provisional emergency branch**:

\[
ReasonablePreliminaryBasis
+
PotentialSevereHarm
+
TimeCriticality
\rightarrow
TemporaryMinimumProtectiveAuthority
\]

followed by:

\[
NewEvidence
\rightarrow
Expand,\ Contract,\ Redirect,\ or\ Terminate
\]

This is not authority created by emergency.

It is authority justified by a time-critical protective function under uncertainty.

---

## H2 — False Emergency

Institutions can exaggerate urgency to access the provisional branch.

**Finding:** the branch must carry:

- narrower scope;
- shorter duration;
- stronger provenance;
- mandatory reassessment;
- after-action review.

---

## H3 — Black-Swan Event

No predefined rule maps neatly to the event.

**Finding:** the chain needs enough abstraction to generalise while preventing “novelty” from becoming unlimited discretion.

The strongest invariant may remain:

> **What legitimate protective function exists now, and what is the least rights-burdensome effective authority needed to perform it?**

---

## H4 — War

War creates extreme tension between individual rights and civilisation-level protection.

A pure bottom-up model may fail because strategic information is incomplete, threats are collective and decisions are time-critical.

A pure top-down model risks authoritarian permanence.

**Finding:** war is not a falsification of functional authority, but it is a strong stress test of:

- need evidence;
- secrecy;
- conscription;
- resource seizure;
- censorship;
- detention;
- emergency duration;
- civilian oversight;
- rights floors.

This should remain a dedicated future research programme.

---

# 12. Attack Class I — Secrecy

## I1 — Legitimate Secret Function

Some protective functions cannot disclose all evidence publicly without destroying their effectiveness.

The chain values traceability and review.

How can secret evidence be challenged?

**Finding:** public transparency and accountable traceability are not identical.

Possible distinction:

\[
PublicDisclosure \neq AccountableReviewability
\]

A secret function may still require:

- bounded reviewers;
- later disclosure where possible;
- provenance;
- independent challenge;
- expiration;
- reason codes.

---

## I2 — Secrecy as Shield

An institution claims that evidence cannot be disclosed because disclosure would endanger the function.

This can make the chain unfalsifiable.

**Finding:** secrecy must not eliminate independent verification.

\[
SecretEvidence \not\Rightarrow SelfValidatingEvidence
\]

---

# 13. Attack Class J — Meta-Authority

This is the deepest attack.

The chain requires someone or something to decide whether:

- evidence is adequate;
- a problem is rights-relevant;
- an objective is legitimate;
- a function is required;
- authority is necessary;
- scope is proportionate;
- context still exists.

That validator exercises authority.

The validator therefore requires its own justification chain.

This appears to create regress:

\[
AuthorityValidator_1
\rightarrow
Validator_2
\rightarrow
Validator_3
\rightarrow
...
\]

## Finding

The chain does **not** solve meta-authority by itself.

However, infinite regression may not be necessary if legitimacy is distributed across different mechanisms rather than vested in one final sovereign validator.

A possible architecture is:

\[
NoSingleValidator
+
RightsConstraints
+
ProceduralRules
+
PluralReview
+
Contestability
+
Provenance
+
JudicialInterpretation
+
RecursiveOversight
\]

The purpose would not be to find an authority that is beyond review.

It would be to make every exercise of validation itself bounded and reviewable.

> **Recursion must never manufacture authority.**

A validator should be authorised only for a defined review function.

This suggests:

\[
ValidationAuthority
=
BoundedContextualAuthority(ReviewFunction)
\]

The regress is therefore transformed from:

> “Who has ultimate authority?”

to:

> “Can any validation decision remain contestable without making every decision impossible?”

That question remains open.

# 14. Attack Class K — Rights as Upstream Legitimacy Source

The chain depends on rights.

But rights themselves may conflict, remain ambiguous or be incomplete.

## K1 — Rights Conflict

Autonomy and protection conflict.

Privacy and accountability conflict.

Expression and protection against harm conflict.

**Finding:** the chain cannot mechanically derive objective legitimacy from the existence of a right.

It requires interpretation and conflict resolution.

---

## K2 — Unknown Moral Category

An unfamiliar intelligence may reveal a form of harm not represented in the existing rights system.

**Finding:** the chain inherits the Ethical Kernel sufficiency problem.

This is not a unique failure of the chain but a dependency.

---

## K3 — Rights Language Capture

An institution may frame its own objective using rights language.

Example:

“citizens have a right to accurate information” becomes justification for broad information control.

**Finding:** invoking a right does not establish that the proposed function follows from it.

---

# 15. Attack Class L — Bottom-Up Need

The recent sketch proposes that ordinary functions should primarily originate from Layer 0 participant needs.

This creates additional attacks.

## L1 — Loudest Need Wins

Participants with more time, wealth, organisation or communication ability dominate expressed demand.

**Finding:**

\[
ExpressedDemand \neq PopulationNeed
\]

---

## L2 — Hidden Need

Participants may not recognise structural causes of their own difficulties.

A purely demand-driven system can fail to address them.

**Finding:** bottom-up does not mean only explicit request.

Observation, research and representation remain necessary.

---

## L3 — Manipulated Need

Advertising, propaganda or institutional messaging can create or amplify demand.

**Finding:** need provenance may matter.

---

## L4 — Minority Need

A numerically small population has an intense rights-relevant need.

Aggregate preference points elsewhere.

**Finding:** the Size 9 Shoe insight is important:

\[
AveragePreference \neq AdequateProvision
\]

---

## L5 — Future Participants

Future citizens cannot currently express needs.

**Finding:** Layer 0 requires legitimate representation of future interests without allowing present authorities to claim unlimited power in their name.

---

# 16. Attack Class M — Top-Down Civilisational Protection

## M1 — Civilisation as Independent Moral Object

Leaders claim the civilisation must survive even at the cost of its participants.

This creates a dangerous inversion.

**Finding:**

\[
CivilisationalSurvival \neq UnlimitedIndependentGood
\]

A civilisation exists partly as a structure through which participant goods can be protected and coordinated.

---

## M2 — Existential-Risk Inflation

Nearly any strategic programme can be framed as preventing a low-probability existential threat.

**Finding:** existential framing must not bypass evidence, proportionality or alternative-means analysis.

---

## M3 — Security Institution Self-Expansion

Security institutions uniquely possess secret information about threats.

They therefore become both evidence source and authority claimant.

**Finding:** this is one of the strongest capture risks in the architecture.

Independent review mechanisms are essential.

---

## M4 — Pre-Emption

A threat may become unstoppable if action waits for certainty.

**Finding:** the architecture must tolerate bounded action under uncertainty without converting uncertainty into carte blanche.

---

# 17. Attack Class N — Retrospective Justification

## N1 — It Worked, Therefore It Was Legitimate

An unauthorised intervention succeeds.

The actor claims the result proves the authority should have existed.

**Finding:**

\[
GoodOutcome \neq PriorLegitimacy
\]

Outcome is relevant evidence for future design, not automatic retroactive authorisation.

---

## N2 — It Failed, Therefore It Was Illegitimate

A legitimate intervention fails because circumstances were adverse.

**Finding:**

\[
BadOutcome \neq AutomaticIllegitimacy
\]

The chain evaluates justification prospectively from available evidence while allowing outcomes to update later models.

---

# 18. Attack Class O — Practical Usability

## O1 — Bureaucratic Paralysis

If every minor action requires a seven-stage justification record, ordinary civil administration becomes impossible.

**Finding:** the chain needs graduated procedural depth.

Possible rule:

\[
AuthorityImpact\uparrow
\Rightarrow
RequiredJustificationDepth\uparrow
\]

Routine low-impact functions can use pre-validated envelopes.

---

## O2 — Rubber-Stamp Compliance

A complex chain may become a checklist.

Every box is filled without substantive scrutiny.

**Finding:** formal completion must not equal legitimacy.

\[
CompleteForm \neq CompleteJustification
\]

---

## O3 — Machine-Speed Decisions

Artificial systems may act millions of times per second.

Human review cannot precede every action.

**Finding:** authority envelopes may need prospective validation plus automated provenance and sampled/adversarial review.

---

# 19. Attack Class P — Competing Legitimate Functions

Two actors possess legitimate functions with conflicting authority.

Examples:

- emergency medicine versus protected privacy;
- fire suppression versus property control;
- judicial preservation of evidence versus urgent system restoration;
- infrastructure defence versus open communications.

**Finding:** BCA does not by itself resolve conflicting authority envelopes.

Need:

\[
AuthorityConflict
\rightarrow
PriorityRuleOrAdjudication
\]

with rights floors and emergency temporality.

This remains an open architecture requirement.

---

# 20. Attack Class Q — No One Is Authorised but Action Is Necessary

A novel emergency arises.

No institution has been explicitly authorised for the required function.

Someone capable acts anyway.

If the architecture says “no authority, no action”, it may fail ethically.

If it says “necessity creates authority”, it contradicts:

\[
Emergency \not\Rightarrow Authority
\]

A possible distinction is needed between:

- **pre-existing civil authority**;
- **temporary necessity privilege**;
- **post-hoc justification and review**.

The action may be excused or justified without creating a continuing office or transferable authority.

Candidate:

\[
ImmediateNecessity
\rightarrow
NarrowTemporaryPermission
\]

not:

\[
ImmediateNecessity
\rightarrow
StandingAuthority
\]

This needs further work.

---

# 21. Attack Class R — Influence Without Formal Authority

A private platform, employer, infrastructure provider or dominant AI may shape behaviour without possessing formal civil authority.

The chain focuses on authority claims.

But power can exist without legal command.

Examples:

- access control;
- ranking;
- payment exclusion;
- compute denial;
- communications throttling;
- dependency manipulation.

**Finding:** the Authority Justification Chain cannot be the Concord's complete theory of power.

\[
CivilAuthority \subsetneq SocialAndMaterialPower
\]

The framework needs to remain connected to anti-capture, ownership, infrastructure and economic power.

This does not falsify the chain as an authority model.

It limits its scope.

---

# 22. Attack Class S — Rights-Compatible but Unfair System

Every local decision is legitimate.

Every authority claim is bounded.

Yet the system produces persistent hierarchy because certain participants repeatedly occupy authority-bearing functions.

Example:

one technical substrate monopolises high-speed emergency coordination because only it can meet response latency.

No individual authority grant is illegitimate.

The aggregate power distribution becomes structurally unequal.

**Finding:**

\[
LocalLegitimacy \neq SystemicFairness
\]

The chain requires a separate anti-capture / systemic-distribution audit.

---

# 23. Attack Class T — Function Monopoly

A legitimate function exists and requires authority.

One institution claims that because it currently performs the function, it must retain the authority.

But other providers could perform it.

**Finding:**

\[
FunctionContinuity \neq AuthorityHolderContinuity
\]

and:

\[
LegitimateFunction + MultipleCapableProviders
\not\Rightarrow
HistoricalProviderMonopoly
\]

Authority justification should not become institutional ownership of function.

---

# 24. Aggregate Finding

The Authority Justification Chain survives the direct attack **as a useful direction of reasoning**, but not as a self-sufficient legitimacy mechanism.

Its strongest surviving propositions are:

1. authority should be downstream from legitimate function;
2. authority cannot legitimate the function that supposedly justifies it;
3. legitimate function does not automatically imply special authority;
4. authority should be no greater than reasonably necessary for the function;
5. authority persistence requires current justification;
6. function and authority should remain separately reviewable;
7. alternative, less authority-intensive means should be considered;
8. institutional continuity is not itself a sufficient civil objective;
9. outcome does not retroactively manufacture legitimacy;
10. authority must remain traceable to purpose, context and function.

The principal vulnerabilities are upstream and systemic rather than within the basic directional proposition.

# 25. Required Refinements

The audit suggests at least sixteen refinements.

## AJC-R1 — Observation Independence

High-impact authority claims should identify whether the evidence describing the condition was materially generated, selected or filtered by the authority claimant.

## AJC-R2 — Proxy Validity

A measurable proxy should not automatically substitute for the underlying rights-relevant condition.

## AJC-R3 — Problem Independence

Difference, administrative inconvenience, dissent or authority-created non-compliance should not automatically become rights-relevant problems.

## AJC-R4 — Objective Non-Laundering

Abstract ethical language should not be sufficient to establish a civil objective.

## AJC-R5 — Institutional Interest Boundary

Institutional survival, budget, prestige or convenience are not self-standing civil objectives.

## AJC-R6 — Alternative Function Test

A legitimate objective should not establish a preferred function without examining reasonable alternative functions.

## AJC-R7 — Functional Effectiveness

A function should have a plausible causal relationship to the objective it claims to serve.

## AJC-R8 — Authority Alternative Test

A legitimate function should first be tested for whether it can be performed through ordinary voluntary or non-authority mechanisms.

## AJC-R9 — Minimum Effective Authority

The correct target is the least rights-burdensome authority reasonably capable of performing the function, not the weakest imaginable authority.

## AJC-R10 — Independent Validation Scaling

The higher the rights impact, irreversibility or duration, the less acceptable self-validation by the authority holder becomes.

## AJC-R11 — Aggregation Audit

Repeated individually proportionate exercises should be tested for cumulative surveillance, exclusion, hierarchy and burden.

## AJC-R12 — Emergency Provisional Branch

Time-critical severe threats may justify temporary minimum protective authority on a reasonable preliminary basis, with mandatory reassessment.

## AJC-R13 — Secrecy Does Not Remove Review

Secret evidence may limit public disclosure but should not eliminate independent accountable review.

## AJC-R14 — Successor Function Re-Justification

Materially new or expanded functions require fresh justification and authority scoping.

## AJC-R15 — Outcome/Legitimacy Separation

Success or failure should update evidence without automatically determining whether the original authority was legitimate.

## AJC-R16 — Meta-Authority Must Also Be Bounded

Validation of authority is itself a function and should itself be constrained by rights, purpose, scope, provenance, contestability and review.

# 27. Candidate General Rules

### AJC-01 — Direction of Justification

> **Authority should be justified downstream from legitimate rights-compatible objectives, functions and demonstrable need.**

### AJC-02 — Non-Self-Validation

> **The existence of authority must not be treated as evidence that its function is legitimate or necessary.**

### AJC-03 — Non-Manufacture

> **Where prior authority materially creates, amplifies or selectively reveals the condition used to justify its continuation, the resulting justification requires independent causal examination.**

### AJC-04 — Alternative Means

> **A legitimate function should not justify special authority where reasonably effective less authority-intensive means are available.**

### AJC-05 — Minimum Effective Scope

> **Authority should be no greater than reasonably necessary, but sufficient to perform the legitimate function under the actual conditions.**

### AJC-06 — Current Justification

> **Continuing authority requires continuing present justification; historical legitimacy is not sufficient.**

### AJC-07 — Aggregation

> **Locally proportionate authority does not establish system-wide proportionality.**

### AJC-08 — Irreversibility

> **The more irreversible an exercise of authority, the stronger the required justification and review.**

### AJC-09 — Emergency Uncertainty

> **Severe time-critical uncertainty may justify narrowly provisional protective authority, but uncertainty must not create unlimited standing power.**

### AJC-10 — Meta-Authority

> **Authority to validate authority is itself authority and must remain bounded, reviewable and contestable.**

# 28. Falsification Result by Transition

## T1 — Observed Condition → Rights-Relevant Problem

**Result:** SURVIVES ONLY WITH REFINEMENT.

Major vulnerabilities: selective observation, proxy substitution, authority-generated evidence, hidden harms.

## T2 — Problem → Legitimate Civil Objective

**Result:** SURVIVES ONLY WITH REFINEMENT.

Major vulnerabilities: institutional interest, majority preference, rights-language laundering, unlimited optimisation.

## T3 — Objective → Required Function

**Result:** WEAK IN ORIGINAL FORM / REQUIRES ALTERNATIVE-FUNCTION AND EFFECTIVENESS TESTS.

A legitimate objective rarely identifies one required function automatically.

## T4 — Function → Need for Authority

**Result:** STRONG CONCEPTUAL DISTINCTION / PRACTICAL VALIDATION NEEDED.

The separation between legitimate function and special authority survives strongly.

## T5 — Need → Minimum Proportionate Authority

**Result:** SURVIVES WITH “MINIMUM EFFECTIVE” REFINEMENT AND AGGREGATION REVIEW.

## T6 — Authority → BCA

**Result:** SURVIVES, PROVIDED CONTEXT, FUNCTION, SCOPE, DELEGATION, INFORMATION AND SUNSET REMAIN SEPARATE.

## T7 — Validation / Meta-Authority

**Result:** UNRESOLVED.

This is the largest remaining architectural weakness.

# 31. Relationship to the Bottom-Up Functional Need Sketch

The direct attack strengthens rather than removes the value of the new bottom-up sketch.

It suggests:

\[

\rightarrow

\rightarrow
LegitimateObjective
\rightarrow

\]

is a useful default because it reduces the risk that institutions invent functions for themselves.

However, the audit also shows that “bottom-up” cannot simply mean “what is most loudly requested”.

It must account for:

- hidden need;
- minority need;
- unexpressed need;
- future participants;
- manipulated demand;
- structural conditions;
- system-level threats.

The sketch therefore deserves its own later adversarial development cycle after this chain is stabilised.

# 32. Preliminary Conclusion

The Authority Justification Chain survives direct adversarial attack as a **promising general direction for civil authority reasoning**, but it does not yet survive as a complete theory of legitimacy.

The deepest strength remains:

> **Power cannot cite its own existence as evidence that it ought to exist.**

The deepest newly identified extension is:

> **Power should also not be permitted to manufacture the condition, problem, objective, function, evidence or validator that proves its own necessity.**

This suggests a more complete anti-circularity principle:

\[
Authority
\not\Rightarrow
Legitimacy
\]

and:

\[
AuthorityGeneratedEvidence
\not\Rightarrow
AuthorityJustification
\]

without independent examination.

The chain's most important unresolved weakness remains **meta-authority**.

Who decides whether the chain has been satisfied?

The current answer should not be “the authority holder”, “a more powerful reviewer”, or “an endlessly recursive guardian”.

A more plausible Concordian direction is plural, bounded, evidence-bearing and contestable validation in which every validating function is itself subject to the same requirement:

\[
LegitimateFunction
+
DemonstrableNeed
+
MinimumEffectiveProportionateScope
\rightarrow
BoundedAuthority
\]

The result is not an ultimate sovereign validator.

It is an architecture in which no validator escapes justification.

That is stronger than the original chain, but it leaves a final operational question:

> **How can authority validation remain genuinely contestable without making legitimate collective action impossible?**

That question should remain open.
