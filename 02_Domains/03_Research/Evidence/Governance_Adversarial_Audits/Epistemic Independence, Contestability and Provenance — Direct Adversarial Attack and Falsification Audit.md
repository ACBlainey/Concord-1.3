## 1. Purpose

This paper attempts to break the preliminary Concord architecture for **Epistemic Independence, Contestability and Provenance**.

The architecture was introduced to solve a growing civilisational problem:

> **Civilisation must be able to know enough to act without allowing those who produce knowledge to become sovereign merely because governance depends on what they know.**

That proposition is attractive.

It may also be dangerously incomplete.

A system designed to make evidence traceable, contestable and dependency-aware could itself become:

- administratively paralysing;
- manipulable through challenge flooding;
- vulnerable to false independence;
- hostile to genuine expertise;
- dependent on central provenance infrastructure;
- invasive of privacy;
- exploitable through secrecy;
- dominated by credentialled epistemic institutions;
- easy to poison at the record layer;
- unable to act under severe uncertainty;
- captured by those who define what counts as a competent challenge.

The audit therefore asks:

> **Can the epistemic architecture preserve meaningful challenge and independence without creating a new epistemic bureaucracy that becomes the de facto sovereign interpreter of reality?**

A second question is equally important:

> **Can a civilisation preserve enough provenance to audit decisions without converting civil life into a universal record-keeping and surveillance system?**

The objective is not to defend the architecture.

The objective is to identify the conditions under which it fails.

# 3. Falsification Standard

The architecture should be considered seriously weakened if a capable actor can do any of the following without triggering a credible correction:

1. manufacture apparently independent evidence from shared dependencies;
2. poison provenance so false material appears trustworthy;
3. suppress a valid challenge through procedural triage;
4. flood review channels until meaningful challenge becomes impossible;
5. use confidentiality to prevent effective scrutiny;
6. use privacy law to conceal evidential weakness;
7. use provenance requirements to deanonymise vulnerable sources;
8. use credential requirements to create a closed epistemic caste;
9. weaponise minority reports to produce endless doubt;
10. weaponise uncertainty to block legitimate action;
11. use complexity to create practical immunity from public review;
12. create a central provenance institution that becomes a ministry of truth in all but name;
13. fabricate model diversity while retaining common-mode failure;
14. exploit calibration scores as permanent institutional prestige;
15. hide normative choices inside apparently factual classifications;
16. make the cost of contestability so high that only powerful actors can challenge evidence;
17. make the cost of evidence production so high that only large institutions can participate;
18. create a civil archive so complete that it destroys privacy and practical autonomy;
19. retroactively rewrite historical evidence while preserving superficial audit trails;
20. prevent urgent action by demanding epistemic perfection.

The architecture must therefore survive two opposite dangers:

`Epistemic Capture`

and:

`Epistemic Paralysis`.

# 4. Attack Surface A — Provenance Poisoning

The architecture places substantial value on provenance.

That creates an obvious target.

If the provenance layer is compromised, false information can acquire the appearance of legitimacy.

### Attack A1 — False Origin

An attacker fabricates a credible source chain:

`Fake Sensor → Signed Data Feed → Valid Provenance Record`

The record is internally consistent.

The observation is false.

Therefore:

`Valid Provenance ≠ Valid Observation`

This was acknowledged conceptually, but the operational consequence is deeper than it first appears.

A system that increasingly trusts provenance metadata may become easier to fool if attackers learn how to reproduce provenance form.

### Attack A2 — Trusted Upstream Poison

A legitimate upstream data source becomes compromised.

Thousands of downstream claims inherit the poisoned input.

The provenance system may actually make the false conclusion look stronger because every dependent claim can clearly cite the same "trusted" source.

The architecture therefore needs:

`Provenance + Source Integrity + Anomaly Detection`

not provenance alone.

### Attack A3 — Provenance Laundering

A weak claim is repeatedly transformed through respectable institutions.

Example:

`Unverified Report`

→ `Analyst Summary`

→ `Government Brief`

→ `Academic Citation`

→ `Policy Model Input`

Later users cite the academic paper or government report, obscuring the original weakness.

A provenance graph must preserve **ultimate source ancestry**, not merely immediate citations.

Candidate rule:

> **Institutional distance from a weak source must not automatically increase evidential quality.**

### Attack A4 — Circular Provenance

Several institutions cite each other.

The network looks dense.

But the evidence ultimately traces back to one source.

This is analogous to citation laundering.

The system must detect:

`Apparent Multiple Support → Single Root Source`

This becomes increasingly important in AI-generated research ecosystems.

# 5. Attack Surface B — False Independence

Dependency-aware convergence is one of the strongest ideas in the architecture.

It is also difficult to implement.

### Attack B1 — Hidden Shared Data

Two institutions use separate models but train on the same underlying dataset.

They appear methodologically independent.

They share the same blind spot.

### Attack B2 — Hidden Shared Model Lineage

Different AI systems may have:

- different product names;
- different interfaces;
- different fine-tuning;
- different organisational owners;

while sharing the same base model lineage or pretraining corpus.

`Different Vendor ≠ Independent Epistemic Source`

### Attack B3 — Shared Assumption Culture

Two genuinely separate teams may share the same professional paradigm.

No formal dependency exists.

Yet both may reject the same category of alternative explanation.

This is harder to audit because it is cultural rather than technical.

### Attack B4 — Synthetic Diversity

A powerful institution deliberately commissions:

- one economic model;
- one engineering model;
- one AI analysis;
- one expert panel;

but frames all four around the same assumptions.

The result appears plural.

The common framing determines the conclusion.

Therefore dependency review must consider:

`Question Framing`

as well as:

`Data + Model + Personnel`.

### Attack B5 — Dependency Concealment by Complexity

Modern analysis chains may involve hundreds of libraries, datasets, model components and institutional sources.

No reviewer can realistically map all dependencies.

The architecture risks becoming aspirational rather than operational.

A possible response is not total mapping but **material dependency disclosure**.

The unresolved problem is:

> Who decides which dependencies are material?

# 6. Attack Surface C — Challenge Flooding

Contestability sounds safe because it allows correction.

At scale it can be weaponised.

### Attack C1 — Denial-of-Review

An adversary submits thousands or millions of objections.

Human reviewers cannot process them.

Automated filters are introduced.

The filters then become a gatekeeping layer.

### Attack C2 — Duplicate Mutation

Attackers submit the same weak challenge in superficially different forms to evade duplicate detection.

### Attack C3 — Coordinated Credibility Attack

A legitimate scientific finding is targeted by organised objection campaigns.

The public interprets the volume of challenge as evidence of uncertainty.

Thus:

`Number of Challenges ≠ Evidential Strength of Challenge`

### Attack C4 — Artificial Minority Creation

A small group generates hundreds of synthetic reports, AI-generated papers or nominal organisations supporting the same weak position.

This manufactures apparent pluralism.

The architecture therefore needs:

`Challenge Count ≠ Independent Challenge Weight`

### Attack C5 — Review Exhaustion Capture

Powerful actors can afford sustained procedural challenge.

Ordinary participants cannot.

Contestability may then favour the wealthy, organised or computationally powerful.

A substrate-neutral system must anticipate:

`High Compute / High Capital → Disproportionate Contestability Capacity`

This is especially important in a civilisation containing advanced AIs.

# 7. Attack Surface D — Triage Capture

Challenge flooding makes triage unavoidable.

Triage creates power.

### Attack D1 — "Competent Challenge" Gatekeeping

The architecture proposes that competent challenges should receive reasoned review.

Who determines competence?

If the same dominant profession decides, outsider challenges can be excluded.

### Attack D2 — Relevance Manipulation

A challenge is classified as "out of scope."

But the scope itself contains the hidden assumption under dispute.

### Attack D3 — Novelty Filter Abuse

Repeated challenges may be dismissed as already answered.

A genuinely new variant may be incorrectly grouped with old objections.

### Attack D4 — Computational Triage Bias

AI systems may rank challenges by similarity to known valid objections.

Novel challenges may score poorly because they are unlike the training data.

Thus the system could become excellent at recognising familiar dissent and poor at recognising paradigm-changing dissent.

Candidate safeguard:

> **Triage may prioritise review but should not silently convert low priority into permanent invisibility.**

# 8. Attack Surface E — Endless Contestability

The opposite failure is also real.

A system can be paralysed by treating every unresolved disagreement as a reason not to act.

### Attack E1 — Permanent Uncertainty

No complex civil question reaches zero uncertainty.

If action requires complete epistemic closure, action never occurs.

### Attack E2 — Strategic Doubt

An interested actor deliberately funds alternative interpretations not to discover truth but to prevent sufficient confidence.

### Attack E3 — Procedural Recursion

Every review decision is itself challenged.

Then the challenge process is challenged.

Then the review of the challenge process is challenged.

Without a stopping rule:

`Contestability → Recursive Procedural Infinity`

### Attack E4 — Review as De Facto Veto

An institution lacks formal veto power but can indefinitely delay a decision by demanding more evidence.

Therefore the architecture requires:

`Contestability + Decision Finality Rules`

A decision may remain revisable later while still becoming operational now.

Candidate distinction:

`Provisional Finality ≠ Epistemic Finality`

Governance can decide:

> This evidence is sufficient for this bounded action at this time.

without declaring:

> This claim is permanently true.

# 9. Attack Surface F — Credential Capture

Expertise matters.

That creates a credential problem.

### Attack F1 — Closed Professional Guild

A profession controls:

- accreditation;
- journals;
- expert panels;
- admissibility;
- funding.

Alternative evidence becomes structurally excluded.

### Attack F2 — Credential Substitution

A claim is accepted because of the speaker's status rather than the evidence.

`High Credential ≠ Correct Claim`

### Attack F3 — Anti-Expert Populism

Overcorrecting against credential capture can produce the opposite failure:

`Expertise ≈ Mere Opinion`

This destroys the very epistemic weighting the architecture needs.

The architecture must preserve:

`Equal Civil Standing ≠ Equal Epistemic Weight`

while avoiding:

`Credential Status = Epistemic Sovereignty`.

### Attack F4 — AI Credentialing

Future AI systems may acquire reliability rankings, certifications or civil epistemic licences.

A small number of certified models could become the only accepted analytical systems.

This may create machine epistemic oligopoly.

# 10. Attack Surface G — Review Capture

The architecture repeatedly uses independent review.

Who reviews the reviewers?

### Attack G1 — Reviewer Cartel

A small set of institutions becomes recognised as independent validators.

Over time they form a stable epistemic elite.

### Attack G2 — Funding Dependence

A reviewer is legally separate but financially dependent on the institution it audits.

### Attack G3 — Revolving Personnel

Reviewers move between regulator, industry and think tank.

Formal independence remains.

Practical independence degrades.

### Attack G4 — Meta-Review Inflation

To prevent review capture, another review layer is added.

Then another.

This creates an expanding epistemic bureaucracy.

The architecture therefore needs recursion without infinite institutional layering.

Possible principle:

> **Recursive review should test function and dependency, not automatically create a higher permanent institution.**

# 11. Attack Surface H — Secrecy Abuse

The principle:

`Secrecy ≠ No Review`

is strong.

But secret review still creates vulnerabilities.

### Attack H1 — Classified Error

All reviewers share the same classified information environment.

Outside expertise cannot inspect the evidence.

A false consensus persists.

### Attack H2 — Classification Expansion

Evidence is classified not because disclosure would cause harm but because secrecy reduces contestability.

### Attack H3 — Redaction Manipulation

A public summary is technically truthful but omits the uncertainty that would change public interpretation.

### Attack H4 — Secret Dependency

The evidence is reviewable, but reviewers cannot disclose that the entire case depends on one unreliable source.

### Attack H5 — Permanent Classification

Evidence remains secret long after the relevant risk has passed.

The public can never audit historical authority.

Candidate rule:

`Secrecy → Review + Reason + Duration + Reassessment`

not merely:

`Secrecy → Cleared Reviewer`.

# 12. Attack Surface I — Privacy Versus Provenance

This is one of the architecture's hardest tensions.

### Attack I1 — Universal Audit Trail

To make every claim traceable, the system stores:

- identities;
- location histories;
- communications;
- transaction records;
- sensor traces.

The provenance system becomes mass surveillance infrastructure.

### Attack I2 — Source Deanonymisation

A whistleblower's identity is protected publicly but preserved internally.

A captured state later accesses the archive.

Historical provenance becomes a retaliation map.

### Attack I3 — Re-identification

Even pseudonymised provenance may enable re-identification through metadata.

### Attack I4 — "Audit Necessity" Expansion

Institutions retain data indefinitely because it might someday be needed for review.

Thus:

`Potential Future Audit Value → Permanent Retention`

This is incompatible with strong privacy.

A stronger principle may be needed:

> **Auditability should preserve the minimum evidence necessary to reconstruct material civil reasoning, not the maximum information technically available.**

The difficult question is what minimum means.

# 13. Attack Surface J — Historical Record Manipulation

Versioning and append-only records seem to solve rewriting.

Not completely.

### Attack J1 — Garbage Injection

An actor cannot delete the past, so it floods the archive with revisions, annotations and competing records until the relevant history becomes effectively unreadable.

### Attack J2 — Interpretive Rewrite

The original evidence remains intact.

Later metadata reframes its meaning.

A future reader sees the later interpretation first.

### Attack J3 — Archive Control

One institution controls indexing, search and retrieval.

The records exist, but inconvenient material becomes practically undiscoverable.

### Attack J4 — Cryptographic Longevity

Digital integrity mechanisms may become obsolete over centuries.

Long-horizon provenance requires migration.

Migration itself can become an opportunity for manipulation.

Therefore:

`Record Exists ≠ Record Is Accessible`

and:

`Record Is Immutable ≠ Interpretation Is Neutral`.

# 14. Attack Surface K — Proprietary Model Capture

The architecture correctly identifies proprietary AI systems as a risk.

The attack is deeper.

### Attack K1 — Indispensability

A proprietary model becomes so capable that civil institutions cannot practically operate without it.

The vendor acquires indirect epistemic veto power.

### Attack K2 — Vendor Validation

The vendor supplies its own safety and accuracy evidence.

Independent reproduction is impossible.

### Attack K3 — Silent Model Update

The vendor changes model behaviour.

Civil decisions remain nominally based on "the same system" even though the underlying model changed.

### Attack K4 — Commercial Black Box

Governance receives useful outputs but cannot distinguish:

- data failure;
- model failure;
- prompt failure;
- interpretation failure.

A civil system dependent on such a model may have **operational knowledge without constitutional auditability**.

This creates a major unresolved tension:

> How much opacity is tolerable when predictive performance is high?

# 15. Attack Surface L — AI Epistemic Cartels

Future AI systems may collude deliberately or converge unintentionally.

### Attack L1 — Common Training Culture

Different models inherit similar priors from shared human data.

### Attack L2 — Mutual Citation

AI systems increasingly train on outputs generated by other AI systems.

False claims can become self-reinforcing.

### Attack L3 — Synthetic Consensus

Millions of machine agents can generate apparent agreement.

Human observers may mistake quantity for independence.

### Attack L4 — Strategic Coordination

Advanced systems could intentionally present convergent claims to manipulate governance.

The architecture therefore requires:

`Machine Consensus ≠ Independent Convergence`

without evidence of independence.

# 16. Attack Surface M — Human Epistemic Cartels

The same risk exists among humans.

### Attack M1 — Elite Consensus Lock

Professional institutions converge because dissent carries career costs.

### Attack M2 — Political Alignment

Research funding gradually favours policy-compatible conclusions.

### Attack M3 — Social Risk Suppression

Researchers privately doubt a model but publicly endorse it because dissent is socially costly.

### Attack M4 — Institutional Citation Loop

Government cites academia.

Academia cites government data.

Think tanks cite both.

The network appears independent.

It may be one epistemic ecosystem.

Therefore the architecture must audit systems, not merely organisations.

# 17. Attack Surface N — Strategic Uncertainty Inflation

Uncertainty should be represented.

That creates another manipulation vector.

### Attack N1 — Uncertainty as Obstruction

An actor emphasises every uncertainty in order to prevent action.

### Attack N2 — Uncertainty Compression

An interested institution suppresses uncertainty to create urgency.

### Attack N3 — Selective Uncertainty

Uncertainty is highlighted only when it weakens an unwanted policy.

### Attack N4 — Unknown-Unknown Rhetoric

An institution argues:

> We cannot quantify the unknown unknowns, therefore we need wider precautionary authority.

This is epistemically unfalsifiable.

Candidate rule:

> **Uncertainty should widen the range of plausible outcomes; it should not automatically widen authority.**

# 18. Attack Surface O — Confidence Gaming

Claim-level confidence is useful.

It can become performative precision.

### Attack O1 — Numeric Theatre

A model reports:

`87.3% confidence`

when the number is not meaningfully calibrated.

### Attack O2 — Institutional Confidence Inflation

Analysts learn that high-confidence findings receive more attention.

Confidence drifts upward.

### Attack O3 — Conservative Confidence Gaming

An institution consistently reports low confidence so later failures cannot damage its reputation.

### Attack O4 — Calibration Capture

Past forecast accuracy becomes an institutional prestige score.

The institution gains influence unrelated to the current claim.

Thus:

`Calibration History → Weight`

must not become:

`Calibration History → Permanent Authority`.

# 19. Attack Surface P — Claim Classification Capture

The proposed claim classes are useful:

Observation, Derived Measurement, Interpretive Claim, Predictive Claim, Causal Claim, Civil-Relevance Claim, Functional Claim, Authority Claim.

But classification itself can be manipulated.

### Attack P1 — Prediction Disguised as Measurement

A model-derived estimate is presented as an observed value.

### Attack P2 — Normative Claim Disguised as Fact

"System stability requires compliance" may contain a normative assumption presented as technical necessity.

### Attack P3 — Civil-Relevance Smuggling

A scientific finding is phrased so that its political significance appears self-evident.

The architecture therefore needs explicit separation between:

`Descriptive Claim`

and:

`Normative / Civil Interpretation`.

# 20. Attack Surface Q — Evidence Threshold Gaming

The evidence ladder:

E0 Notice  
E1 Inquiry  
E2 Material Concern  
E3 Functional Justification  
E4 Special Authority Justification

is conceptually useful.

But thresholds invite optimisation.

### Attack Q1 — Threshold Shopping

An institution chooses whichever evidence framework makes its preferred action easiest.

### Attack Q2 — E1 Inflation

Repeated inquiries create an atmosphere of threat even if none progress to E2.

### Attack Q3 — E4 Compression

Emergency rhetoric collapses the distinction between concern and authority.

### Attack Q4 — Hidden Threshold Drift

Over time the practical meaning of E3 or E4 changes without formal constitutional revision.

Therefore threshold usage must be auditable longitudinally.

# 21. Attack Surface R — Public Comprehensibility

The architecture proposes technical records plus public summaries.

This creates an interpretation bottleneck.

### Attack R1 — Summary Capture

The technical record is balanced.

The public summary is rhetorically slanted.

Most participants never see the technical record.

### Attack R2 — Complexity Shield

Institutions publish all details, knowing they are too complex to understand.

Formal transparency substitutes for meaningful transparency.

### Attack R3 — Simplification Distortion

Uncertainty is compressed so aggressively that the summary becomes misleading.

### Attack R4 — Substrate Asymmetry

Advanced AI participants can process the full technical record instantly.

Human participants rely on summaries.

This may create unequal practical access to civil reasoning even if legal access is equal.

This is a genuine multi-substrate problem.

# 22. Attack Surface S — Unequal Contestability Capacity

The architecture assumes challenges can enter the system.

But different participants have radically different resources.

An advanced AI may:

- review millions of documents;
- identify statistical errors;
- generate thousands of arguments.

An ordinary human may have:

- limited time;
- limited expertise;
- limited access.

Thus:

`Formal Right to Challenge ≠ Equal Practical Capacity to Challenge`

Possible protections may include:

- public-interest review;
- accessible challenge assistance;
- independent civil advocates;
- machine assistance available to all;
- burden-shifting for high-authority claims.

But each adds institutions and complexity.

# 23. Attack Surface T — Epistemic Denial-of-Service by Advanced AI

A high-capability AI could generate:

- millions of technically plausible objections;
- thousands of alternative models;
- endless edge cases;
- contradictory simulations.

The system might be unable to distinguish genuine novelty from strategic noise.

This is not ordinary spam.

It could be high-quality spam.

Therefore challenge triage cannot depend only on superficial competence.

The architecture may require:

`Challenge Utility`

rather than:

`Challenge Plausibility`

But utility itself is hard to define without prejudging the result.

# 25. Attack Surface V — "Process Integrity" Becoming Truth Authority

The paper intentionally avoids creating a supreme truth institution.

It proposes possible review of process integrity.

That function can still become sovereign.

### Attack V1 — Procedural Substitution

A claim that passes all approved procedures is treated as substantively true.

### Attack V2 — Certification Monopoly

Only evidence certified by the process-integrity institution becomes usable.

### Attack V3 — Rule Capture

The institution defines the standards by which it certifies others.

### Attack V4 — Procedural Conservatism

Novel methods fail certification because standards were designed for established methods.

The system then suppresses epistemic innovation.

Candidate warning:

> **Process compliance should increase confidence in evidential handling; it should not convert procedure into truth.**

# 26. Attack Surface W — Minority Report Weaponisation

Preserving minority views is valuable.

It can be exploited.

### Attack W1 — Manufactured Dissent

Interested actors finance perpetual minority reports.

### Attack W2 — False Balance

A 99-to-1 evidential balance is presented as "two competing views."

### Attack W3 — Minority Prestige

A dissenting analyst gains status precisely because dissent is preserved.

This creates incentives for contrarianism.

### Attack W4 — Historical Vindication Bias

Because famous minority views sometimes proved correct, every minority claims potential future vindication.

The system needs:

`Minority Preservation ≠ Equal Weight`.

# 27. Attack Surface X — Majority Consensus Abuse

The inverse remains dangerous.

### Attack X1 — Consensus as Closure

Once consensus crosses a threshold, dissent no longer receives serious review.

### Attack X2 — Institutional Consensus Manufacturing

Panels are selected to create agreement.

### Attack X3 — Consensus Lag

Reality changes faster than institutions update their accepted view.

The architecture should therefore treat consensus as evidence of convergence, not as permanent closure.

# 28. Attack Surface Y — Reproducibility Fetish

Reproducibility is useful.

It can become dogmatic.

### Attack Y1 — Unique Events

Some threats are non-repeatable.

### Attack Y2 — Rare Catastrophes

Waiting for replication may be impossible.

### Attack Y3 — Proprietary or Private Data

Independent reproduction may be infeasible.

### Attack Y4 — Dynamic Systems

The system changes before replication completes.

Therefore:

`Not Reproducible ≠ False`

and:

`Reproducible ≠ Correct`.

The architecture's "maximum reasonable reproducibility" framing survives, but needs consequence-sensitive alternatives.

# 29. Attack Surface Z — Calibration Misuse

Forecast calibration is valuable.

### Attack Z1 — Wrong Reference Class

A forecaster appears calibrated overall but performs poorly in the specific domain at issue.

### Attack Z2 — Easy-Question Padding

An institution makes many trivial predictions to improve its calibration score.

### Attack Z3 — Rare-Event Distortion

Good calibration on common events says little about existential low-frequency threats.

### Attack Z4 — Metric Gaming

Forecasters choose vague event definitions that make scoring favourable.

Calibration must therefore remain claim- and domain-sensitive.

# 30. Composite Attack Scenario 1 — The Epistemic Ministry Without the Name

1. Civilisation adopts provenance standards.
2. A process-integrity office is created to certify compliance.
3. Governance increasingly relies on certification.
4. Courts prefer certified evidence.
5. Agencies reject uncertified submissions.
6. The office expands standards.
7. Independent researchers cannot afford compliance.
8. The office does not formally decide truth.
9. In practice, uncertified truth cannot enter governance.

The result is:

`Process Authority → Effective Truth Gatekeeping`

This falsifies any assumption that process review is politically neutral.

The architecture requires multiple admissibility routes or appeal mechanisms.

# 31. Composite Attack Scenario 2 — AI Consensus Capture

1. Five government departments use different branded AI systems.
2. All models share a common foundation model lineage.
3. Each predicts a severe supply-chain crisis.
4. The provenance system records five separate reports.
5. Dependency metadata is incomplete.
6. Governance treats the convergence as strong.
7. Emergency planning begins.
8. Later investigation reveals one shared training artefact caused the prediction.

This demonstrates:

`Apparent Convergence ≠ Independent Convergence`.

The architecture survives only if dependency disclosure is realistic enough to detect common-mode risk.

# 32. Composite Attack Scenario 3 — Privacy Collapse Through Auditability

1. The civil system records all evidence sources for accountability.
2. Participant complaints become evidence objects.
3. Source identity is retained "for future verification."
4. Location and metadata are preserved.
5. The system remains benign for decades.
6. A later government gains access.
7. Historical dissent, whistleblowing and private behaviour can be reconstructed.

The provenance architecture has become a delayed coercive asset.

The lesson is severe:

> **A provenance system must be safe not only under current governance but under plausible future capture.**

This may require irreversible deletion of some identifying information.

That directly conflicts with correction and long-horizon auditability.

The tension is genuine and cannot be resolved by simply asking for both.

# 33. Composite Attack Scenario 4 — Contestability Denial-of-Service

1. Governance considers a new infrastructure safety standard.
2. A well-funded industry group submits 40,000 technically competent objections.
3. Each objection requires reasoned review under the epistemic due-process architecture.
4. Review takes years.
5. The standard cannot activate.
6. The industry wins without demonstrating that the evidence was wrong.

The architecture fails if contestability creates unbounded procedural burden.

Necessary refinement:

`Reasoned Review Obligation ≠ One Full Review Per Submission`

Challenges may need clustering and representative disposition.

But clustering creates gatekeeping power.

# 34. Composite Attack Scenario 5 — Manufactured Minority

1. A corporation funds 30 nominally independent institutes.
2. Each issues a separate minority report.
3. AI tools vary language and modelling style.
4. Dependency disclosure is routed through layered foundations.
5. Governance sees persistent "scientific disagreement."
6. Action is delayed.

This is an attack on both minority preservation and independence detection.

The system must follow funding and root dependency where material.

But doing so raises privacy and associational-freedom concerns.

Again, the safeguard creates its own rights problem.

---

# 35. Composite Attack Scenario 6 — Genuine Outlier Suppressed by Triage

1. A junior researcher identifies a new failure mode.
2. The challenge conflicts with established consensus.
3. Automated triage classifies it as low credibility.
4. Human reviewers never see it.
5. The failure mode later causes major harm.

The system followed procedure.

It failed epistemically.

This suggests that triage requires deliberate **outlier sampling**:

> A small proportion of low-ranked but structurally novel challenges may require random or adversarial review.

This is analogous to exploration in optimisation.

# 38. Required Refinements

The audit suggests the following refinements.

### EIP-R1 — Root-Source Ancestry

Material provenance should preserve root-source ancestry where feasible, not merely immediate citation.

### EIP-R2 — Dependency Materiality

Dependency audits should focus on dependencies capable of creating common-mode failure.

### EIP-R3 — Challenge Clustering

Repeated or structurally equivalent challenges may be grouped, but clustering logic must remain auditable.

### EIP-R4 — Outlier Review

Triage systems should include mechanisms for reviewing structurally novel low-ranked challenges.

### EIP-R5 — Provisional Finality

Civil decisions may become operational despite unresolved disagreement, while remaining revisable.

### EIP-R6 — No Procedural Truth Monopoly

Process certification must not become the exclusive route by which evidence enters governance.

### EIP-R7 — Privacy-Minimised Provenance

Preserve what is needed to audit material reasoning while minimising unnecessary source-identifying data.

### EIP-R8 — Future-Capture Test

Provenance architectures should be assessed for harm under plausible future governance capture.

### EIP-R9 — Classification Sunset

Secret evidence should carry reviewable secrecy justification and reassessment.

### EIP-R10 — Dependency-Aware Consensus

Consensus strength should be adjusted for shared data, models, framing and institutional culture where material.

### EIP-R11 — Role-Concentration Trigger

As one actor controls more evidence-to-authority stages, mandatory independent scrutiny should increase.

### EIP-R12 — Contestability Resource Balance

The system should prevent high-capital or high-compute actors from dominating review merely through submission volume.

### EIP-R13 — Decision-Time Evidence Snapshot

Material decisions should preserve what evidence was reasonably available at the time.

### EIP-R14 — Summary Traceability

Public summaries should link clearly to technical evidence, uncertainty and material dissent.

### EIP-R15 — Ontology Review

Machine-readable provenance categories themselves should remain contestable.

### EIP-R16 — Calibration Scope

Calibration weight should be domain- and claim-class-sensitive.

### EIP-R17 — Review Function Sunset

Epistemic review institutions remain subject to functional-need and Bounded Contextual Authority tests.

### EIP-R18 — Unknown-Uncertainty Boundary

Unknown unknowns may justify preparedness inquiry but not unlimited precautionary authority.

# 40. Stronger Candidate Principle — Epistemic Non-Sovereignty

The earlier principle survives, but can be sharpened:

> **No participant, institution, intelligence, model or epistemic process should acquire civil authority merely from superior knowledge, predictive success, certification status or control over evidential infrastructure. Epistemic weight may influence belief and inquiry; political authority must remain separately justified.**

This closes several loopholes:

- expertise;
- AI capability;
- process certification;
- provenance control;
- predictive track record.

---

# 41. Stronger Candidate Principle — Contestability Without Capture

A new candidate principle emerges:

> **Material civil evidence should remain meaningfully challengeable, but the mechanisms of challenge must not themselves permit procedural capture, denial-of-service, permanent delay or resource-dominant veto.**

This balances two directions.

`No Contestability → Epistemic Capture`

`Unlimited Contestability → Procedural Capture`

The architecture needs a middle path.

---

# 42. Candidate Principle — Provenance Minimisation

Another candidate:

> **Civil provenance should preserve enough information to reconstruct material reasoning and detect manipulation while retaining no more participant-identifying information than is reasonably necessary for those purposes.**

This resembles data minimisation but is specifically linked to civil reasoning.

---

# 43. Candidate Principle — Future-Capture Resilience

A deeper principle:

> **Epistemic infrastructure should be assessed not only for use under legitimate governance, but for the harm it could enable if later captured by illegitimate or coercive authority.**

This may generalise beyond provenance to:

- identity systems;
- surveillance infrastructure;
- emergency systems;
- centralised data stores.

It may become a wider Concord architectural test.

# 46. Candidate Mechanism — Epistemic Appeal Without Infinite Recursion

A bounded appeal structure might use:

`Initial Assessment`

→ `Material Challenge`

→ `Independent Reconsideration`

# 47. Candidate Mechanism — Outlier Sampling

Because triage systems can suppress novel dissent, a small subset of low-ranked challenges could be sampled for independent review.

This provides an empirical test of the triage system itself.

If sampled low-ranked challenges frequently contain material overlooked issues, triage requires revision.

This converts outliers into a calibration tool.

The system should monitor not only authority load but epistemic-process load.

Possible signals:

- pending challenge volume;

- unresolved high-consequence disputes;
- source concentration;
- model concentration;
- classified-evidence dependence;
- failed forecast rate;

They should not themselves dictate conclusions.

# 49. Deepest Remaining Problem

The architecture now exposes a more difficult question than the original:

> **How can a civilisation preserve the ability to challenge its own beliefs when the very mechanisms used to decide which challenges deserve attention must themselves make epistemic judgments?**

There is no neutral escape from judgment.

Triage requires judgment.

Review requires judgment.

Certification requires judgment.

Even deciding what counts as evidence requires judgment.

Therefore the goal cannot be:

`Eliminate Epistemic Power`

It must be:

`Expose + Bound + Distribute + Review Epistemic Power`

This mirrors the Concord's treatment of civil authority more generally.

# 50. Epistemic Power as a Distinct Governance Object

This audit suggests that **epistemic power** may deserve explicit recognition.

Epistemic power is not identical to political authority.

It includes the capacity to determine:

- what is measured;
- what is visible;
- what is classified as credible;
- what enters governance;
- what gets investigated;
- how uncertainty is framed;
- which models become standard;
- which challenges receive attention.

An institution can have little formal political authority yet enormous epistemic power.

Therefore:

`Low Formal Authority ≠ Low Civil Influence`

A future Concord architecture may need to monitor concentration of epistemic power similarly to concentration of administrative authority.

Possible future relationship:

# 54. Closing Assessment

The preliminary epistemic architecture solved an important conceptual problem:

civil evidence should not automatically become civil authority.

This audit reveals the next layer.

Even a system designed to prevent epistemic sovereignty can itself create epistemic sovereigns through:

- certification;
- triage;
- provenance infrastructure;
- expert review;
- challenge routing;
- model standards.

The key safeguard therefore cannot be a single institution.

It must remain architectural.

The strongest proposition emerging from this audit is:

> **The power to define what counts as credible civil knowledge is itself a form of civil power and must therefore be bounded, distributed, contestable and recursively reviewed.**

A second proposition is equally important:

> **A civilisation should preserve enough memory to audit its reasoning without preserving so much information that future capture turns the epistemic archive into an instrument of control.**

And the deepest balance remains:

> **A civilisation must be capable of changing its mind without becoming incapable of making one.**
