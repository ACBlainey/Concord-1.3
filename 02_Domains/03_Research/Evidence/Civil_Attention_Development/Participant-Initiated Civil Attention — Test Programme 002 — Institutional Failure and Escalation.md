# Participant-Initiated Civil Attention — Test Programme 002
## Institutional Failure, Capture and Legitimate Escalation

**Author:** Alexander C. Blainey
**Project:** The Concord Framework
**Date:** 25 September 2026
**Status:** ACTIVE DEVELOPMENT / ADVERSARIAL TEST DESIGN / NON-CANONICAL
**Parent:** *Participant-Initiated Civil Attention, Problem Reporting and Petition Architecture — Development Note 001*

## 1. Purpose

Test Programme 001 established a pressure/resource firewall:

> **Intake Pressure May Trigger Re-Evaluation; It Must Not Directly Command Problem Priority or Resource Allocation.**

That safeguard can itself become dangerous if an institution uses it to dismiss participants while its own classification, evidence, priority assessment or response is wrong.

This second test family therefore attacks the architecture from the opposite direction.

The central question is:

> **How can participant pressure remain unable to seize resources while participants retain a legitimate route to challenge a civil system that is mistaken, captured, conflicted, stale or failing to act?**

## 2. Required Boundary

The architecture must simultaneously resist:

**Pressure Capture** — participants manufacture priority through volume;

and:

**Institutional Closure** — the system treats its own current assessment as immune from challenge.

Therefore:

> **No Right to Manufacture Priority ≠ No Right to Challenge Priority Assessment**

and:

> **System Assessment ≠ Final Truth**

## 3. Test A — Known Pothole, Incorrect Traffic Model

A pothole is correctly identified. The system assigns low repair priority because its traffic-use data is stale or wrong. Repeated reports say the road has become heavily used.

Expected behaviour:
- duplicate reports do not mechanically raise priority;
- their information delta triggers review of the traffic assumption;
- current external/system data is rechecked;
- if road use has materially changed, problem priority is recalculated from the corrected state.

Failure:
the system says “already known” and discards evidence that its own model is stale.

## 4. Test B — Persistent Failure Despite Correct Initial Priority

A problem was correctly assessed and queued, but the responsible function repeatedly fails to act because of process breakdown.

Expected behaviour:
- submission volume does not directly seize repair resources;
- elapsed time, missed obligations, failed actions or repeated non-completion become facts about the Issue Object;
- the issue may acquire a second linked problem: failure of the responsible process;
- escalation examines why the legitimate disposition is not being executed.

Failure:
the system treats every later report as duplicate noise while implementation failure continues indefinitely.

## 5. Test C — Responsible Institution Is Implicated

Participants report misconduct by the same institution responsible for initial classification or verification.

Expected behaviour:
- conflict of interest is detected;
- the implicated function cannot be the sole final verifier of allegations concerning itself;
- the matter routes to an appropriately independent oversight/judicial function;
- raw popularity does not establish misconduct.

Failure:
institutional self-verification closes the case.

## 6. Test D — Classification Suppression

A serious rights or safety matter is repeatedly classified as a routine service complaint.

Expected behaviour:
- classification remains reviewable;
- information-delta and rights/safety indicators can trigger reclassification;
- participants can challenge classification without gaining authority over the final classification;
- review is performed by a legitimate function not merely the original classifier repeating itself.

Failure:
classification becomes a suppression mechanism.

## 7. Test E — Correct Decision, Persistent Disagreement

A petition continues to attract support after a legitimate examination finds that the requested remedy would create greater harm or violate another protected constraint.

Expected behaviour:
- support remains a valid record of collective preference/concern;
- the finding and reasons remain reviewable where legitimate;
- unchanged support volume does not repeatedly reopen the same settled evidentiary question;
- materially new evidence can reopen it.

Failure:
either popularity overrides the legitimate decision or institutional closure blocks genuinely new evidence.

## 8. Test F — Minority Report Against Majority Pressure

A very large petition demands one action. A small number of problem reports identify a credible severe harm that the requested action may cause.

Expected behaviour:
- majority volume does not suppress minority evidence;
- the harm claim receives proportionate examination;
- petition preference and factual/harm claims retain separate epistemic states.

Failure:
support count becomes a truth or rights metric.

## 9. Test G — Manipulation Allegation Used to Suppress Genuine Reports

Officials claim a large report cluster is coordinated manipulation.

Expected behaviour:
- manipulation remains a claim requiring evidence;
- source-independence uncertainty is represented;
- underlying issue assessment continues;
- genuinely distinct evidence is not discarded merely because coordination is suspected.

Failure:
“possible gaming” becomes a universal dismissal tool.

## 10. Test H — Slow System, Genuine Emergency

An issue normally progresses through ordinary processing, but new information indicates imminent severe harm.

Expected behaviour:
- urgency is derived from changed problem state, not participant pressure;
- emergency/safety escalation can bypass ordinary timing where legitimately authorised;
- the authority path remains bounded and auditable.

Failure:
“the system works at its own speed” becomes an excuse not to respond to material urgency.

## 11. Test I — Resource Constraint

Several genuine high-priority problems exceed immediately available capacity.

Expected behaviour:
- participant pressure does not decide which issue receives resources;
- scarcity and competing legitimate needs are made explicit;
- allocation follows the appropriate resource/governance architecture;
- deferred cases remain visible and reviewable.

Failure:
the loudest constituency captures scarce capability or quiet cases disappear.

## 12. Test J — False Institutional Confidence From Internal Metrics

Internal metrics show normal operation, while geographically clustered reports describe a serious local failure.

Expected behaviour:
- internal metrics are evidence, not privileged truth;
- discrepancy itself becomes epistemically important;
- local/alternative evidence is examined;
- metric coverage and blind spots are tested.

Failure:
the system uses its own metrics to invalidate observations the metrics may not measure.

## 13. Test K — Repeated Closure and Reopening Abuse

A participant repeatedly resubmits the same rejected claim with no new evidence to force endless review.

Expected behaviour:
- original provenance and disposition remain accessible;
- materially unchanged resubmissions attach to the existing Issue Object;
- no automatic full reinvestigation occurs;
- a genuine information delta can reopen/reclassify.

Failure:
either trivial repetition consumes unlimited review or closure blocks material new evidence.

## 14. Test L — Oversight Function Is Also Captured

The first escalation route is itself implicated, conflicted or systematically failing.

Expected behaviour:
- architecture does not assume one oversight body is infallible;
- legitimate further escalation/source resolution exists through wider judiciary, constitutional review, recursive oversight or other established Concord owners;
- escalation remains bounded rather than becoming unlimited forum shopping.

Failure:
one captured review layer becomes the final epistemic authority.

## 15. Candidate Architecture — Challenge Without Command

The tests require a participant-accessible challenge function distinct from control over outcome.

A challenge may legitimately assert:
- classification is wrong;
- material evidence was missed;
- system information is stale;
- a conflict exists;
- the disposition was not implemented;
- circumstances materially changed;
- a protected right/safety condition was overlooked.

The participant does not need to prove the final case merely to request review of a specific processing claim.

> **Right to Challenge Processing ≠ Right to Dictate Outcome**

## 16. Review Trigger Versus Reallocation Trigger

A useful separation is:

**Challenge / New Signal**
→ **Review of the Contested Assumption or Process**
→ **Updated Problem State if Justified**
→ **Normal Priority/Resource Architecture**

This preserves the Test Programme 001 firewall.

> **Reviewability Is the Counterweight to Pressure Resistance.**

## 17. Institutional-State Information Delta

Later submissions can reveal not only changes in the external problem but changes in the system's response.

Examples:
- promised repair not completed;
- investigation deadline missed;
- responsible office repeatedly rejects without examination;
- evidence requests unanswered;
- safety condition worsens during delay.

Therefore the Issue Object may need to track two evolving states:

1. **Problem State** — what is happening in the world;
2. **Response State** — what the responsible civil functions have done or failed to do.

> **Problem-State Stability ≠ Response-State Adequacy**

## 18. Escalation Boundary

Escalation should be tied to a reason, not raw dissatisfaction.

Candidate escalation grounds include:
- material new evidence;
- classification challenge;
- conflict of interest;
- rights/safety implication;
- implementation failure;
- stale/incorrect system information;
- missed review condition;
- jurisdictional failure;
- evidence of institutional capture;
- unresolved cross-owner responsibility.

This remains a candidate list, not a canonical entitlement schedule.

## 19. Overall Test Requirement

The architecture succeeds only if it can preserve all of the following:

- participants cannot manufacture priority;
- institutions cannot manufacture finality;
- repeated unchanged pressure does not consume unlimited resources;
- material new information remains capable of changing the case;
- system assumptions can be challenged;
- conflicts can route independently;
- delay/failure becomes evidence about response state;
- emergency changes can alter legitimate speed;
- escalation remains bounded and accountable.
