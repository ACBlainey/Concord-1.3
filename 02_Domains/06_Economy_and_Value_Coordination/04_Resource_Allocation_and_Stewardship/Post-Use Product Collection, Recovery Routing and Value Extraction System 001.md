# 1. Trigger

Product Lifecycle Stewardship establishes:

> **Post-Use Product != Automatically Waste.**

If a post-use product remains a concentration of components, materials, embodied energy, manufacturing effort and technical value, then a further system function becomes necessary:

# **recover that value.**

Design for recovery is insufficient if retired products never reach a suitable recovery pathway.

The civilisation therefore requires an interface between:

**End of Use**

and

**Next Productive Use.**

---

# 2. Persistent function

The persistent function is:

> **collect post-use products, identify their remaining value, and route them toward the highest reasonably viable safe next use.**

This includes:
- collection;
- identification;
- condition assessment;
- triage;
- testing;
- valuation;
- routing;
- aggregation;
- reverse logistics;
- whole-product reuse;
- refurbishment;
- repurposing;
- component harvesting;
- material recovery;
- hazardous recovery;
- residual disposal.

This is not merely waste collection.

> **Post-Use Collection Is Resource Acquisition From the Existing Material Stock.**

---

# 3. Resource recovery hierarchy

A collected product should not automatically be sent to material recycling.

Candidate routing preference:

1. continued safe use;
2. whole-product reuse;
3. repair;
4. refurbishment;
5. upgrade;
6. repurpose;
7. component harvesting;
8. remanufacturing feedstock;
9. material recovery;
10. hazardous/residual treatment.

The appropriate route depends on actual condition, safety, demand, recovery cost and resource value.

> **Recycling Too Early Can Destroy Higher-Order Value.**

---

# 4. Collection interfaces

Possible collection routes include:
- manufacturer take-back;
- retailer return;
- dedicated recovery centres;
- civic collection;
- commercial collectors;
- scheduled household/business collection;
- enterprise closure/wind-down recovery;
- trade-in;
- deposit-return;
- repair-centre handoff;
- direct resale/reuse;
- specialist hazardous-product recovery.

No single route need own the entire system.

---

# 5. Product intake

At intake, the system may identify:
- product/product-class ID;
- Product Passport/Resource Map where available;
- apparent condition;
- safety hazards;
- ownership/transfer status;
- data/privacy risks;
- reusable assemblies;
- likely repairability;
- component value;
- material value;
- recovery cost;
- available demand/routes.

Unknown products can enter a manual/technical assessment path.

---

# 6. Triage

A candidate triage process:

**Post-Use Product Received**
→ **Safety Isolation if Needed**
→ **Identify Product/Materials**
→ **Assess Functional Condition**
→ **Assess Repair/Reuse Potential**
→ **Assess Component Value**
→ **Assess Material Value**
→ **Check Current Demand**
→ **Select Highest Reasonable Recovery Route**
→ **Record Outcome/Provenance where useful**

This should not blindly maximise monetary price.

Relevant value may include:
- scarcity;
- embodied resource;
- environmental burden avoided;
- repair utility;
- strategic material value;
- social/economic demand;
- safety cost.

---

# 7. Value extraction

“Value extraction” here means preservation/recovery of legitimate remaining utility.

Possible value layers:

## Functional value
The whole product still works.

## Repair value
A modest intervention restores useful function.

## Refurbishment value
The product can return to service after deeper restoration.

## Repurpose value
The product can perform a different useful function.

## Component value
Subassemblies remain usable.

## Material value
Materials can re-enter production.

## Knowledge value
Failure/usage information can improve future design where legitimately collected.

> **The Highest Remaining Value May Exist Above the Material Layer.**

---

# 8. Recovery routing market

Post-use products create supply.

Repairers, refurbishers, remanufacturers and material processors create demand.

A future Concord system could match:
- available retired products/components/materials;
- recovery capability;
- geographic/logistical constraints;
- current material demand;
- qualification/safety requirements.

This can create a **Recovery Routing Market** without assuming one central recycler.

---

# 9. Reverse logistics

Conventional logistics moves:

**resources/products → users.**

Recovery logistics adds:

**users → recovery nodes → next productive users/manufacturers.**

Efficient reverse logistics may use:
- existing delivery routes;
- consolidation points;
- local recovery centres;
- enterprise distribution networks;
- scheduled collection;
- manufacturer return chains.

> **The Supply Chain Does Not Necessarily End at the Customer.**

# 11. Participant incentive

Recovery should not rely only on moral exhortation.

Possible mechanisms include:
- resale value;
- trade-in;
- deposit return;
- component/material purchase;
- collection credits;
- manufacturer take-back;
- free collection;
- reduced replacement cost.

The appropriate mechanism remains an Economy/Commerce design question.

> **A Recoverable Resource Should Be Able to Signal Its Remaining Value.**

---

# 12. Ownership transfer

Collection requires clear transfer state.

Possible states:
- owner retains ownership during assessment;
- owner sells product;
- owner donates product;
- ownership transfers through take-back;
- product enters regulated hazardous recovery;
- enterprise transfers product stock during wind-down.

Resource stewardship does not itself determine ownership law.

> **Collection for Recovery != Automatic Confiscation.**

---

# 13. Data-bearing products

Computers, phones, vehicles, robots, medical devices and other products may contain participant/enterprise data.

Recovery must therefore include a data-state check.

Possible routes:
- verified data erasure;
- storage removal;
- secure destruction of unrecoverable data-bearing components;
- participant-controlled transfer;
- protected refurbishment process.

> **Material Recovery Must Not Become Data Leakage.**

---

# 14. Safety and qualification

Some products require qualified handling.

Examples:
- high-voltage systems;
- batteries;
- pressure vessels;
- medical devices;
- hazardous chemicals;
- radioactive materials;
- contaminated equipment;
- safety-critical components.

Recovery value never overrides safe handling.

---

# 15. Component provenance and grading

Recovered components may need a confidence state.

Candidate fields:
- source product;
- component ID/class;
- age/use where known;
- test result;
- condition grade;
- safety limitations;
- remaining-life estimate where meaningful;
- certification;
- provenance.

A recovered component should not be represented as equivalent to new merely because it physically fits.

---

# 16. Material quality

Recovered material may have:
- purity;
- contamination;
- degradation;
- mixed composition;
- processing requirements.

Resource routing should therefore match material state to suitable next uses rather than treating all recovered material as interchangeable.

---

# 17. Design feedback

Recovery systems observe where product design succeeds or fails.

Aggregate evidence can reveal:
- products impossible to disassemble;
- common premature failures;
- unrecoverable material combinations;
- components routinely discarded despite remaining value;
- hazardous recovery bottlenecks;
- missing repair documentation;
- economically irrational packaging/material choices.

That evidence can feed product design.

**Recovery Evidence**
→ **KCS / Product Stewardship**
→ **Design Improvement**
→ **Future Products Become Easier to Recover**

> **Product Retirement Can Teach Product Creation.**

---

# 18. Resource inventory

Recovered products/components/materials can become part of a dynamic resource picture.

A candidate system may know:
- available recovered components;
- material quantities;
- quality grades;
- locations;
- recovery status;
- expected future flows.

This can help manufacturers use existing material stock before demanding new extraction where appropriate.

> **The Civilisation's Resource Base Includes Materials Already in Circulation.**

---

# 19. Strategic reserve possibility

Some recovered scarce components/materials may have low current market price but high future/systemic importance.

A purely immediate-price routing system could discard them.

Resource Stewardship may therefore require a mechanism to recognise strategic value.

This remains a future development question.

---

# 20. Enterprise retirement interface

Enterprise wind-down may release:
- equipment;
- inventory;
- spare parts;
- tools;
- machinery;
- computing systems;
- raw materials;
- facilities components.

These should enter recovery/reallocation pathways rather than automatically becoming liquidation waste.

Thus enterprise retirement and product recovery share infrastructure.

---

# 21. Manufacturer interface

Product Passport/Resource Map information can allow recovery systems to know:
- how to disassemble;
- material composition;
- hazards;
- component compatibility;
- repair route;
- manufacturer take-back route.

Manufacturers may also become buyers of their own recovered components/materials.

This creates a potential closed material loop.

# 24. Failure modes

## Waste-default routing
Everything is treated as disposal material.

## Recycling destruction
Functional products/components are shredded prematurely.

## Recovery theatre
Collection exists but material ultimately enters ordinary waste.

## Negative-value dumping
Hazard/cost is transferred to weaker actors or places.

## Data leakage
Data-bearing products are reused without safe handling.

## False grading
Recovered components are misrepresented.

## Logistics inefficiency
Recovery consumes more scarce resources than it preserves.

## Monopoly capture
One recovery operator gains unnecessary control over resource flows.

## Ownership ambiguity
Items are collected without legitimate transfer.

## Information gap
Recoverers cannot identify materials/disassembly routes.

# 27. Conclusion

Once post-use products are recognised as resources, collection and recovery become a normal productive system function.

The system's purpose is not merely to remove unwanted objects.

It is to transform:

**retired product**

into the highest reasonably recoverable next state:

**working product**
or
**repaired/refurbished product**
or
**new-purpose product**
or
**tested component**
or
**manufacturing feedstock**
or
**recoverable material**

before accepting residual disposal.

This closes a missing loop between Product Stewardship and the wider Economy:

**Manufacture → Use → Retirement → Collection → Value Recovery → Productive Re-entry.**
