# 1. Trigger

The existing resource-lifecycle work establishes that post-use products can retain functional, component and material value.

A further question follows:

**How does a product physically re-enter a recovery pathway when its current participant no longer needs it?**

The answer should not assume one central Concord collection system.

Products leave use for many reasons:
- replacement by a newer or different model;
- changed participant need;
- failure;
- damage;
- obsolescence;
- upgrade;
- relocation;
- enterprise closure;
- planned retirement.

Different products, participants and locations may require different return paths.

> **Resource Stewardship != Centralised Resource Collection.**

---

# 2. Distributed return architecture

A mature recovery system can support multiple interoperable entry routes.

Possible routes include:

## Participant hand-in

A participant voluntarily hands in a product that is:
- no longer needed;
- no longer functional;
- replaced;
- unsuitable for current use;
- ready for recovery.

## Ordinary refuse / recycling collection

Suitable product classes may enter recovery through ordinary scheduled collection where safe and practical.

This may include dedicated separated categories rather than undifferentiated waste.

## Distributed civic collection points

Participants may take products to:
- recycling centres;
- recovery centres;
- local collection points;
- specialist hazardous-product facilities;
- temporary or mobile collection facilities.

## Retail-adjacent collection

Working or potentially reusable products may be accepted at or near retail facilities.

This can be especially useful where participants naturally travel to a retail location while replacing an item.

## Retail trade-in / old-for-new return

When purchasing a replacement, the participant can hand the previous product to the retailer.

The retailer becomes a collection interface rather than necessarily the recovery owner.

Collected products can then be periodically:
- collected by manufacturers;
- collected by recovery operators;
- consolidated through reverse logistics;
- delivered by the retailer to a suitable recovery facility.

> **Retail Distribution Can Operate in Reverse as a Recovery Interface.**

## OEM / manufacturer return

The original equipment manufacturer may accept its own products back directly or through retailers/service centres.

Possible outcomes include:
- inspection;
- repair;
- refurbishment;
- upgrade;
- remanufacture;
- component harvesting;
- material recovery;
- safe decommissioning;
- specialist disposal.

## Existing secondary markets

A product that remains useful need not enter formal recovery at all.

It may pass through:
- resale;
- donation;
- exchange;
- refurbishment markets;
- repair businesses;
- direct participant-to-participant transfer.

> **A Recovery Architecture Should Not Destroy Existing Reuse Pathways Merely to Centralise Them.**

---

# 3. Collection interface != recovery owner

A collection point need not determine the product's final route.

For example:

**Participant**
→ **Retailer**
→ **Periodic Consolidated Collection**
→ **OEM / Recovery Operator**
→ **Triage**
→ **Reuse / Upgrade / Refurbishment / Component Recovery / Material Recovery**

Likewise:

**Participant**
→ **Civic Collection**
→ **Sorting / Aggregation**
→ **Specialist Recovery Route**

This permits distributed convenience while preserving specialist processing.

> **Collection Interface != Recovery Ownership.**

---

# 4. OEM stewardship

Manufacturers are unusually well positioned to understand:
- product construction;
- materials;
- component compatibility;
- disassembly;
- firmware/software dependencies;
- failure modes;
- upgrade possibilities;
- specialist hazards;
- recoverable parts;
- product-generation compatibility.

Resource stewardship may therefore justify a proportionate expectation that an OEM participates in the decommissioning, reuse, upgrading or recovery of its products.

This does not yet establish one universal legal producer-responsibility rule.

The appropriate responsibility may vary with:
- product class;
- scale;
- hazard;
- scarcity;
- expected life;
- recoverability;
- market structure;
- manufacturer capability;
- participant burden;
- availability of independent recovery routes.

> **Product Creation Can Carry a Continuing Stewardship Relationship With Product Retirement.**

---

# 5. OEM return and renewal service

A manufacturer could offer a service analogous in participant experience to a recall, but initiated for lifecycle renewal rather than defect correction.

Possible flow:

**Participant Product**
→ **OEM Return**
→ **Inspection**
→ **Condition / Version Assessment**
→ **Repair / Upgrade / Modernisation**
→ **Testing / Re-certification**
→ **Return to Participant**

The product may receive:
- newer components;
- improved control electronics;
- updated software;
- replacement wear parts;
- safety improvements;
- additional functions;
- improved efficiency;
- compatibility upgrades.

This creates a route between ordinary repair and full product replacement.

> **New Function Need Not Always Require a New Whole Product.**

---

# 6. Upgrade-return versus replacement

Where the physical product retains substantial value, an upgrade-return service may preserve:
- chassis;
- structural components;
- motors;
- housings;
- displays;
- mechanical assemblies;
- scarce materials;
- participant familiarity;
- embedded manufacturing effort.

The manufacturer replaces only what materially requires replacement.

This can reduce demand for:
- new extraction;
- complete remanufacture;
- packaging;
- logistics;
- disposal/recovery of still-useful assemblies.

It will not be appropriate for every product.

Safety, technical feasibility, cost, contamination, certification and efficiency may justify replacement instead.

---

# 7. Product-as-platform design

If future renewal is anticipated during original design, products can be designed around replaceable generations of modules.

Candidate examples:
- control module;
- battery;
- communications module;
- processor;
- sensor package;
- power electronics;
- wear assemblies;
- user-interface module.

The durable product body can then survive several technology generations.

> **Product Lifetime and Component Generation Need Not Be Identical.**

This strengthens the existing design-for-disassembly and upgradeability work.

---

# 8. OEM feedback advantage

OEM return creates a particularly valuable evidence loop.

The manufacturer can observe:
- actual failure modes;
- unexpectedly durable components;
- weak components;
- difficult disassembly;
- contamination;
- repair frequency;
- upgrade demand;
- component recovery rates;
- material recovery losses;
- software-related retirement;
- participant reasons for replacement.

Flow:

**Product Design**
→ **Manufacture**
→ **Use**
→ **OEM Return**
→ **Observed End-State**
→ **Recovery / Upgrade**
→ **Design Evidence**
→ **Next Product Generation**

> **The Returned Product Is Also Evidence About the Product That Was Designed.**

---

# 9. Distributed responsibility

The architecture need not place all responsibility on one actor.

Possible participants include:
- product owner/user;
- retailer;
- OEM;
- independent repairer;
- refurbisher;
- secondary market;
- civic collection service;
- specialist recycler;
- hazardous-material operator;
- logistics provider;
- component market;
- material processor.

Different actors may perform different stages.

The architectural requirement is therefore better expressed as:

> **Ensure a Credible Recovery Path; Do Not Assume One Universal Recovery Operator.**

---

# 10. Concord role

The Concord should not automatically:
- collect every product;
- own returned products;
- operate every recovery facility;
- dictate every recovery route;
- replace ordinary markets;
- replace manufacturers;
- replace civic refuse systems.

Its architecture may instead support:
- interoperable product/resource information;
- recovery-route visibility;
- minimum stewardship expectations where legitimately established;
- provenance;
- routing interfaces;
- aggregate resource forecasting;
- recovery-capacity visibility;
- participant access to return routes;
- feedback from recovery to design.

> **System Responsibility Can Mean Ensuring the Path Exists, Not Performing Every Step.**

---

# 10A. Civil Contact Point as the primary discovery interface

The Concord already contains a candidate **Civil Contact Point** architecture described as a portable civil interface connecting participants with relevant services and participation.

Product-return discoverability should therefore use that existing civil interface rather than create a separate participant-facing recovery-information system.

A participant could ask their Civil Contact Point, in effect:

> **What legitimate return, repair, reuse, upgrade or recovery routes are currently available for this product?**

The Civil Contact Point could expose relevant options such as:
- OEM return or renewal;
- retailer hand-in;
- old-for-new collection;
- civic collection;
- ordinary separated collection;
- repair/refurbishment;
- resale/donation/reuse;
- specialist hazardous recovery.

The Civil Contact Point does not need to own or perform those services.

The cleaner relationship is:

**Recovery Providers / Service Owners**
→ **Current Route and Eligibility Information**
→ **Civil Contact Point**
→ **Participant Discovery**
→ **Participant Choice**
→ **Relevant Independent Provider**

> **Civil Contact Point Is the Primary Discovery Interface, Not the Recovery Operator.**

This also avoids unnecessary duplication across Concord services. Participants should not need to learn a separate discovery system for every domain where an existing general civil interface can expose the relevant service.

However, **primary != exclusive**.

Existing Civil Contact Point development explicitly recognises risks of gatekeeping, monopolisation, service dependency and exclusion. Product recovery should therefore remain discoverable through legitimate direct provider, retailer, civic and other channels as appropriate.

> **Primary Civil Interface != Exclusive Access Path.**

This preserves the convenience of one normal Concord service-information interface without making that interface structurally indispensable to resource recovery.

---

# 10B. Recovery-service information contract

Civil Contact should not need to understand or reproduce the internal operation of every recovery provider.

Instead, participating service owners should expose enough current information for Civil Contact to answer the participant-facing question:

> **What legitimate options are available to me for this product now?**

A provisional minimum information envelope is:

`RecoveryRouteNotice = <ProviderRef, ProductOrClassAccepted, RouteType, GeographicOrServiceScope, Eligibility, ParticipantActionRequired, CollectionOrDropOffMode, CostOrCreditWhereRelevant, SafetyRestrictions, DataHandlingRequirementWhereRelevant, CapacityOrAvailabilityStateWhereMaterial, EffectiveDate, ReviewOrExpiryRef, DirectProviderContactRef, Provenance>`

This is an interface envelope, not a central registry ownership claim.

The provider remains responsible for the accuracy of its service information within its legitimate scope. Civil Contact presents/routs that information and should preserve source/provenance so participants can distinguish provider-supplied conditions from Concord-wide rules.

> **Service Discovery != Service Certification.**

> **Civil Contact Routing != Civil Contact Ownership of the Underlying Service.**

Where provider information is stale, contradictory or unavailable, Civil Contact should expose that uncertainty rather than manufacture a route.

> **Unknown Current Availability != Available Service.**

Where a participant experiences an incorrect refusal, erroneous eligibility state or other consequential administrative effect, the existing Civil Contact development already points toward Governance, Judiciary, participant-data and provenance interfaces rather than creating a resource-specific appeals system.

Thus the resource architecture should reuse those correction paths rather than duplicate them.

# 11. Participant convenience

Recovery will underperform if returning an item is disproportionately difficult.

A distributed architecture can make return a normal part of existing participant activity:
- replacement purchase;
- ordinary waste/recycling collection;
- visit to a retail centre;
- service/repair visit;
- civic recycling trip;
- manufacturer return shipment;
- trade-in.

> **Recovery Accessibility Is Part of Recoverability.**

A theoretically excellent recovery process has little practical value if products rarely reach it.

---

# 12. Working-product boundary

A working product should not automatically be destroyed merely because it is handed in.

Where legitimate demand exists, routing should first consider:
- direct reuse;
- resale;
- donation;
- refurbishment;
- redeployment;
- repurpose.

Retail-adjacent return may therefore connect to secondary markets as well as OEM/recycling routes.

> **Hand-In != Waste Declaration.**

---

# 13. Data-bearing products

Returned products may contain:
- personal files;
- credentials;
- account tokens;
- communications;
- location history;
- commercial data;
- medical or other sensitive information.

Collection convenience must not create data leakage.

Data-bearing products require an independently legitimate data sanitisation / custody process before reuse, refurbishment or component recovery where necessary.

> **Physical Return != Consent to Transfer Personal Data.**

---

# 14. Ownership and consent

Voluntary hand-in normally requires a legitimate transfer basis.

The architecture must distinguish:
- voluntary transfer;
- trade-in;
- deposit return;
- lease return;
- producer take-back obligation;
- hazardous-product controls;
- abandoned property;
- ordinary waste rules.

This document does not determine those legal categories.

> **Resource Value Does Not Erase Ownership or Consent Boundaries.**

---

# 15. Reverse logistics

Retail and distribution networks already move products toward participants.

Some may support reverse flows.

Examples:

**Retail Outlet**
→ **Regional Consolidation**
→ **OEM**

or

**Retail Outlet**
→ **Regional Recovery Hub**
→ **Multiple Specialist Routes**

Periodic collection allows small participant returns to become economically/logistically useful aggregated flows.

Existing delivery vehicles or logistics routes may sometimes support reverse movement where efficient and safe.

This requires whole-route assessment rather than assuming reverse logistics is always beneficial.

---

# 16. Manufacturer independence boundary

OEM stewardship should not unnecessarily create an OEM monopoly over repair or recovery.

Independent actors may sometimes provide:
- better repair;
- local refurbishment;
- compatible parts;
- component markets;
- specialist recovery;
- alternative upgrades.

The objective is resource stewardship, not automatic exclusive control by the original manufacturer.

> **OEM Responsibility != OEM Monopoly.**

---

# 17. Failure modes

## Centralisation failure
A distributed function is unnecessarily turned into one large Concord-operated collection system.

## Convenience failure
Recovery exists on paper but participant return is too difficult.

## Premature destruction
Working products are shredded/recycled instead of reused.

## OEM abandonment
Manufacturer captures sale value while leaving avoidable recovery burden elsewhere.

## OEM lock-in
Stewardship becomes justification for blocking independent repair/recovery.

## Data leakage
Returned devices transfer participant data with the hardware.

## Retail bottleneck
Retailers collect products without viable downstream removal capacity.

## False circularity
Items are collected but ultimately stored/disposed because no real recovery route exists.

## Logistics externality
Reverse transport consumes disproportionate resources relative to recovered value.

## Ownership overreach
Resource stewardship overrides legitimate participant ownership/consent without independent authority.

# 19. Relationship to existing architecture

## Lifecycle Stewardship Review

LSR can determine that a product/function should:
- continue;
- adapt;
- transform;
- become dormant;
- succeed/transfer;
- retire.

It does not own physical recovery routing.

## Bounded Transition Architecture

BTA can preserve coherence where consequential transition spans multiple owners/states.

It does not choose the recovery hierarchy.

## Product Lifecycle Stewardship

Designs recoverability into products.

## Post-Use Product Collection and Value Recovery

Assesses/routs returned products toward remaining value.

## Circulating Resource Inventory

Uses aggregate return/recovery evidence to estimate future resource flows.

The distributed return architecture supplies **entry pathways** into that wider loop.

# 21. Development status

This architecture is presently a domain-level development.

It should not yet be treated as a portable module.

Next development should test:
- whether every major product class can enter through at least one credible return route;
- where OEM responsibility is useful versus excessive;
- retailer capacity/burden;
- reverse-logistics efficiency;
- participant incentives;
- orphaned products where the OEM no longer exists;
- imported products;
- hazardous goods;
- software-dependent products;
- products whose manufacturer refuses or cannot recover them;
- competition/independent-repair boundaries;
- products with no economically viable recovery route.

The purpose of the next test is to discover where the distributed model fails, not to prove that it works.
