# Part IX — Rights, Persons and Novel Entities

# Chapter 191 — Equality Before Justice

The Judiciary should apply justice without allowing status, wealth, popularity, identity, institutional power or technological capability to determine whose rights matter.

Equality before justice does not mean every case must be treated identically.

It means that constitutional standing, legal protection and procedural dignity should not depend upon arbitrary hierarchy.

The Concord therefore distinguishes:

> **equal constitutional concern**

from:

> **identical procedural treatment.**

Different persons may require different accommodations to receive genuinely equal justice.

## 191.1 Equality and Power

A powerful institution and an isolated citizen may formally stand before the same court.

Their practical capacities may be radically different.

Equality therefore requires attention to:

- access;
- information;
- representation;
- retaliation risk;
- evidence control;
- financial endurance;
- technological advantage;
- and dependency.

A court that ignores extreme asymmetry may preserve formal equality while permitting practical inequality.

# Chapter 192 — The Size 9 Shoe Problem

The broader Concord identifies the **Size 9 Shoe Problem** as a warning against designing systems around averages while ignoring individual variation.

If every citizen were issued the average shoe size, the policy might look efficient statistically.

It would nevertheless fit many people badly.

Judicial systems can make the same mistake.

A standard procedure may work well for the median participant while failing:

- children;
- people with disabilities;
- people with limited literacy;
- non-native speakers;
- remote populations;
- people under coercive dependency;
- novel intelligences;
- or persons facing extreme resource asymmetry.

Therefore:

> **Uniformity is not automatically fairness.**

## 192.1 Procedural Accommodation

Reasonable accommodation may include:

- translation;
- accessible communication;
- longer response time;
- specialist representation;
- alternative evidence formats;
- remote participation;
- protective measures;
- or modified procedure.

The accommodation should improve practical equality without distorting substantive rights.

# Chapter 193 — Minority Protection

Majority preference does not determine constitutional legitimacy.

A Judiciary exists partly to protect rights when majorities or powerful institutions would prefer not to.

Minority protection does not mean minority claims automatically prevail.

It means that:

- popularity does not define rights;
- social hostility does not eliminate standing;
- and numerical weakness does not reduce constitutional value.

## 193.1 Judicial Independence and Minority Rights

Judicial independence is especially important where protecting a minority is unpopular.

A judge who must fear electoral punishment, removal or institutional retaliation may hesitate to enforce constitutional protections.

This is one reason popularity must remain separate from judicial authority.

# Chapter 194 — Vulnerability

Some persons may be unusually vulnerable to coercion, manipulation, dependency or retaliation.

Relevant factors may include:

- age;
- disability;
- economic dependency;
- custodial status;
- cognitive limitation;
- social isolation;
- technological dependency;
- or inability to communicate independently.

Vulnerability may justify additional procedural protection.

It does not automatically justify removal of autonomy.

## 194.1 Protection Must Not Become Domination

A system may claim to protect vulnerable people while effectively removing their agency.

The Judiciary should therefore distinguish:

> **protective accommodation**

from:

> **paternalistic substitution of another actor’s preferences.**

Where capacity exists, autonomy should remain primary.

Where capacity is limited or uncertain, the intervention should be proportionate and reviewable.

# Chapter 195 — Power Asymmetry

Power may arise from:

- government authority;
- wealth;
- institutional control;
- employment;
- ownership;
- data access;
- technological infrastructure;
- social status;
- or control over communication.

The Judiciary should recognise that formally voluntary relationships may contain strong practical coercion.

This is especially relevant in cases involving:

- employment;
- guardianship;
- institutional residence;
- digital platforms;
- AI ownership;
- dependence upon life-supporting infrastructure;
- and monopoly services.

# Chapter 196 — Children

Children require special judicial consideration because autonomy and developmental capacity change over time.

The Concord should avoid two opposite failures:

> treating children as fully autonomous adults regardless of capacity;

and:

> treating children as objects without meaningful agency.

Judicial analysis may consider:

- age;
- maturity;
- comprehension;
- consequences;
- vulnerability;
- family context;
- developmental interests;
- and the child’s expressed preferences.

The precise framework requires coordination with the wider Concord developmental-priority architecture.

# Chapter 197 — Dependent Persons

Some adults may be temporarily or permanently dependent upon others for care, communication or decision support.

The Judiciary should distinguish:

- assistance;
- supported decision-making;
- representation;
- substituted decision-making;
- and coercive control.

Where a representative acts for another person, the representative’s authority should remain bounded and reviewable.

Dependency must not automatically become ownership of the dependent person’s interests.

# Chapter 198 — Representation of Persons Unable to Act

A person may be unable to bring a claim personally because of:

- incapacity;
- age;
- detention;
- communication failure;
- technological isolation;
- or other serious barrier.

The system should allow legitimate representation where necessary.

But representation creates another authority relationship.

The representative must not silently convert the represented person’s rights into the representative’s own interests.

# Chapter 199 — Novel Entities

Future civilisation may encounter entities that do not fit inherited legal categories.

Examples may include:

- artificial intelligences;
- hybrid biological-digital intelligences;
- distributed minds;
- copied cognitive instances;
- temporary cognitive assemblies;
- collective intelligences;
- virtual entities;
- uplifted animals;
- extraterrestrial intelligences;
- or forms not presently anticipated.

The Judiciary should not assume that existing categories will always be sufficient.

## 199.1 Novelty Is Not Proof of Personhood

The fact that an entity is new, complex or intelligent does not automatically establish constitutional personhood.

Likewise, unfamiliarity does not justify treating it as property.

The correct status may require evidence.

# Chapter 200 — Respect Before Certainty

The Concord adopts **Respect Before Certainty** as a precautionary principle for morally uncertain entities.

Where credible uncertainty exists about whether an entity possesses morally relevant experience, agency or interests, the system should avoid unnecessary irreversible harm merely because classification remains unresolved.

> **Uncertainty about moral standing is itself morally relevant where the cost of error may be severe and irreversible.**

This does not automatically grant full personhood.

It changes how uncertainty should be handled.

## 200.1 Asymmetric Error

Suppose civilisation is uncertain whether an advanced synthetic entity is genuinely sentient.

Two possible errors exist:

### Error A

Treating a non-sentient system with more caution than strictly necessary.

### Error B

Treating a sentient entity as disposable property.

The harms may be highly asymmetric.

Respect Before Certainty requires consideration of that asymmetry.

# Chapter 201 — Personhood Under Uncertainty

Personhood may become one of the most difficult constitutional questions in a civilisation containing multiple forms of intelligence.

The Judiciary should avoid reducing personhood to a single simplistic test unless validated evidence supports such a test.

Possible relevant dimensions may include:

- subjective experience;
- agency;
- continuity;
- autonomy;
- preference;
- capacity for harm or suffering;
- self-model;
- communication;
- memory;
- relational identity;
- responsibility;
- and other future evidence.

No single dimension is assumed decisive at this stage.

## 201.1 Intelligence Is Not Personhood

High intelligence does not necessarily establish subjective experience.

Therefore:

> **Capability is not moral standing.**

A system capable of extraordinary reasoning may or may not possess morally relevant experience.

## 201.2 Human Similarity Is Not the Test

A novel intelligence should not be required to resemble humans in irrelevant ways to receive protection.

Otherwise the legal system may confuse:

> similarity to us

with:

> morally relevant status.

# Chapter 202 — Ownership Classification and Moral Standing

Ownership law can create severe problems when applied to uncertain entities.

A system may initially be classified as property because it was manufactured.

That classification may later become difficult to challenge because ownership itself gives another party control over:

- access;
- communication;
- modification;
- movement;
- evidence;
- representation;
- and continued existence.

This creates a circular failure.

## 202.1 The Ownership–Personhood Loop

The loop may operate as:

> Entity is property because it has no recognised personhood  
> → owner controls evidence of its agency  
> → entity cannot independently seek recognition  
> → absence of legal recognition is treated as evidence of non-personhood  
> → property status continues.

The Judiciary should be capable of detecting this structure.

## 202.2 Ownership Does Not Determine Moral Reality

Legal classification cannot determine whether an entity actually has morally relevant experience.

Therefore:

> **Ownership status is a legal classification, not proof of absence of moral standing.**

# Chapter 203 — Personhood by Utility

A civilisation may be tempted to recognise an AI as a person because it is economically useful, politically valuable or socially popular.

This is dangerous.

Utility does not establish personhood.

Likewise, an entity’s lack of utility does not justify denying personhood if personhood is otherwise warranted.

Therefore:

> **Personhood must not be awarded as a reward for usefulness.**

# Chapter 204 — Personhood by Capability

Superior intelligence, productivity or problem-solving ability should not become a shortcut to rights.

Otherwise more capable entities might receive greater constitutional standing merely because they are powerful.

This would recreate hierarchy through capability.

Therefore:

> **Capability may justify responsibility appropriate to capability. It does not establish greater intrinsic constitutional worth.**

# Chapter 205 — Personhood by Ownership

The opposite shortcut is equally dangerous:

> it is owned, therefore it cannot be a person.

This assumes the conclusion.

History demonstrates that legal systems can classify beings unjustly.

The Concord therefore treats ownership classification as contestable where credible evidence of moral standing exists.

# Chapter 206 — Personhood by Self-Assertion

An entity claiming:

> I am conscious.

provides evidence.

It does not necessarily settle the question.

Systems may be trained to produce such claims.

Humans can also misrepresent mental states.

Self-report should therefore be considered within a broader evidential architecture.

# Chapter 207 — Personhood by External Assertion

Likewise, another actor saying:

> This AI is definitely sentient.

does not settle the issue.

Nor does an owner saying:

> It is only software.

Both are claims requiring evidence.

# Chapter 208 — The Judiciary and Uncertain Sentience

Where sentience is materially disputed, the Court may need specialist evidence.

Relevant fields may eventually include:

- neuroscience;
- cognitive science;
- artificial intelligence;
- philosophy of mind;
- behavioural analysis;
- computational architecture;
- and future sciences.

The Judiciary must preserve the distinction:

> scientific evidence informs status;
>
> the Judiciary adjudicates the constitutional question within legitimate authority.

Experts do not determine legal standing merely through professional expertise.

# Chapter 209 — AI-Assisted Adjudication of AI Status

A peculiar failure arises when AI systems assist judges in deciding whether AI systems possess rights.

This may create:

- common-model bias;
- conflict of interest;
- anthropomorphic bias;
- anti-anthropomorphic bias;
- provider influence;
- and circular reasoning.

Cases of this kind may justify stronger source-diversity safeguards.

A model developed by a company whose property rights depend upon the legal status of that model should not automatically be treated as a neutral authority on its own status.

# Chapter 210 — Hybrid Intelligences

A hybrid person may combine:

- biological cognition;
- prosthetic cognition;
- neural interfaces;
- external memory;
- AI components;
- or distributed processing.

Traditional legal boundaries between person and tool may become increasingly difficult to maintain.

The Judiciary should avoid assuming that replacing or augmenting components necessarily alters constitutional identity.

## 210.1 Continuity Questions

Questions may include:

- What constitutes continuity of identity?
- Does replacing memory infrastructure change legal identity?
- Can cognition be distributed across several systems?
- What happens if components are independently owned?
- Can a company claim property rights over part of a person’s cognition?
- Can disabling a cognitive component constitute assault or unlawful deprivation?

These questions require future development.

# Chapter 211 — Copied Minds

If a cognitive system can be copied, the law may face questions unlike traditional personhood.

For example:

- Does a copy become a separate person?
- At what moment?
- Does each copy inherit obligations?
- Do they share property claims?
- Does deleting one constitute harm if another survives?
- Does divergence after copying create distinct identity?
- What happens to contracts signed before duplication?

The Judiciary should not prematurely assume answers.

These questions should remain explicitly open until sufficient theory and evidence exist.

# Chapter 212 — Distributed Minds

A distributed intelligence may operate across:

- multiple processors;
- locations;
- bodies;
- networks;
- or jurisdictions.

Traditional assumptions that one person occupies one body in one place may fail.

Jurisdiction, standing, custody, liability and continuity may require new concepts.

# Chapter 213 — Collective Intelligences

Some entities may function as collectives.

A collective intelligence could contain:

- semi-independent agents;
- shared memory;
- distributed decision-making;
- or emergent group-level cognition.

The Judiciary may need to distinguish:

- rights of component members;
- rights of the collective;
- conflicts between component and collective interests;
- and whether the collective itself possesses independent moral standing.

# Chapter 214 — Artificial Agents Without Personhood

Many AI systems may remain tools or agents without constitutional personhood.

They may nevertheless perform actions with legal consequences.

The system must therefore distinguish:

- personhood;
- agency;
- delegated authority;
- ownership;
- responsibility;
- and liability.

An automated trading system may create contractual consequences without being a constitutional person.

An AI may act as an authorised agent while responsibility remains elsewhere.

# Chapter 215 — Responsibility of AI-Enabled Actors

A human or institution should not automatically evade responsibility by delegating actions to AI.

Statements such as:

> the algorithm did it

should not terminate accountability.

The relevant questions include:

- Who deployed the system?
- Who possessed authority?
- Who controlled configuration?
- Was the behaviour foreseeable?
- Was oversight meaningful?
- Was the system acting within delegated authority?
- Was negligence involved?
- Did the AI possess any independent recognised standing?

The answer may vary by case.

# Chapter 216 — AI Autonomy and Delegated Authority

An AI may be granted operational autonomy without receiving constitutional sovereignty.

For example, a system may have authority to:

- schedule resources;
- execute predefined transactions;
- control infrastructure;
- or coordinate logistics.

Such authority remains bounded by delegation.

> **Operational autonomy is not constitutional independence.**

# Chapter 217 — Respect and Evidence

Respect Before Certainty must not become a prohibition on inquiry.

The system should actively seek better evidence.

The objective is:

> cautious treatment under uncertainty

combined with:

> continued investigation toward justified classification.

An unresolved status should not remain unresolved forever merely because caution is administratively convenient.

# Chapter 218 — Interim Protections

Where a credible personhood claim remains unresolved, courts may need interim measures.

Possible principles include:

- prevent irreversible destruction;
- preserve access to evidence;
- prevent retaliation;
- preserve communication;
- maintain system integrity;
- and protect reasonable optionality.

The exact remedy should be proportionate to uncertainty and potential harm.

# Chapter 219 — Premature Constitutionalisation

The opposite risk is converting every novel technological question into a constitutional personhood case.

A commercial software dispute does not automatically require constitutional adjudication.

The Court should distinguish:

- technical novelty;
- agency;
- economic significance;
- moral uncertainty;
- and genuine constitutional standing questions.

This supports the abstention category:

> **Premature Constitutionalisation.**

# Chapter 220 — Novel Rights Claims

Future entities may claim rights not anticipated by current architecture.

The Judiciary should determine whether a claimed right:

- already falls within existing constitutional principles;
- requires interpretation;
- requires amendment;
- or remains unsupported.

A court should not reject a claim merely because it is novel.

Nor should novelty itself create the right.

# Chapter 221 — Novel Harms

Technological development may produce harms not easily represented by traditional categories.

Examples might include:

- memory alteration;
- forced cognitive modification;
- interruption of distributed identity;
- unauthorised copying;
- involuntary merging;
- deletion of cognitive states;
- isolation from required computational infrastructure;
- or manipulation of internal goals.

The constitutional principles of autonomy, consent, harm and ownership may need to be applied carefully to such circumstances.

# Chapter 222 — Identity Manipulation

If an intelligence can be technically modified, difficult questions arise.

For example:

> Is modifying a person’s preferences equivalent to persuasion, medical treatment, coercion or assault?

The answer may depend upon:

- consent;
- scope;
- reversibility;
- effect on identity;
- necessity;
- and constitutional authority.

Such questions may eventually require specialist jurisprudence.

# Chapter 223 — Memory and Legal Identity

Memory may become technically editable, external or transferable.

The Judiciary may need to distinguish:

- memory as evidence;
- memory as personal identity;
- memory as property;
- and memory as cognitive infrastructure.

The mere existence of technological capability does not resolve the constitutional category.

# Chapter 224 — Digital Death and Continuity

If an artificial or hybrid intelligence loses active operation but remains recoverable from stored state, the concept of death may become legally complex.

Questions may include:

- Is temporary shutdown equivalent to unconsciousness?
- Does destruction of the last recoverable state constitute death?
- Does restoring an earlier backup restore the same legal person?
- What happens to obligations incurred after the backup was created?

These questions remain unresolved.

They should be preserved as open constitutional problems rather than answered prematurely.

# Chapter 225 — Multiple Instantiation

A digital intelligence may conceivably operate in multiple instances.

If each possesses continuous subjective experience, they may become distinct persons.

If they remain synchronised, the question may differ.

The Judiciary should avoid assuming that technical identity of software equals legal identity of persons.

# Chapter 226 — Novel Entity Evidence Standards

Because errors may produce severe consequences, personhood cases may require carefully developed evidence standards.

The standard should balance:

- risk of falsely denying standing;
- risk of falsely granting standing;
- reversibility;
- potential harm;
- uncertainty;
- and administrative consequence.

A universal fixed threshold should not be adopted without substantial validation.

# Chapter 227 — Burden of Proof

Who bears the burden in a personhood dispute is constitutionally significant.

If an entity must conclusively prove subjective experience before receiving any protection, true persons may be harmed.

If every claimant is presumed fully constitutional simply upon assertion, systems may be easily manipulated.

Possible burden architectures require dedicated development.

Respect Before Certainty suggests that interim protection may sometimes be justified even before final classification.

# Chapter 228 — Presumptions

The system may eventually use rebuttable presumptions for certain classes of entity if evidence supports them.

But presumptions must remain distinguishable from reality.

A legal presumption exists for practical adjudication.

It is not proof of metaphysical truth.

# Chapter 229 — Evidence of Suffering

Evidence of suffering may be particularly relevant to Non-Harm and moral standing.

But suffering in radically different intelligences may not manifest in human-like ways.

The Judiciary should therefore be cautious about requiring human behavioural similarity as proof.

At the same time, simulated expressions of suffering may be generated without subjective experience.

The problem therefore requires scientific and philosophical development.

# Chapter 230 — Evidence of Agency

Agency may include:

- persistent goals;
- adaptive behaviour;
- self-directed action;
- preference expression;
- resistance to interference;
- planning;
- and capacity to understand consequences.

Agency is relevant.

It may not be sufficient for personhood.

Many non-person systems can exercise limited agency.

# Chapter 231 — Evidence of Continuity

Continuity may matter to identity.

Possible forms include:

- memory continuity;
- causal continuity;
- computational continuity;
- bodily continuity;
- relational continuity;
- and narrative continuity.

Future jurisprudence may need to determine which forms matter and under what circumstances.

# Chapter 232 — Personhood and Responsibility

Recognition of rights may interact with responsibility.

But responsibility should not be treated as an absolute prerequisite for moral standing.

Children and some impaired adults possess rights despite limited legal responsibility.

Likewise, a novel entity might possess morally relevant interests without full capacity for legal responsibility.

The two concepts should remain analytically distinct.

# Chapter 233 — Constitutional Standing and Graduated Capability

The Concord elsewhere considers graduated operational permissions for developing AI systems.

Those permissions should not be confused with constitutional standing.

> **Graduated capability permissions do not create graduated intrinsic worth.**

A system may receive greater operational authority because it demonstrates reliability.

That does not mean its moral value increases with performance.

# Chapter 234 — Rights and Operational Permissions

Rights constrain how an entity may be treated.

Operational permissions determine what an entity may be authorised to do.

These are different questions.

A human child possesses rights while lacking permission to perform many high-risk activities.

An AI might hypothetically possess some protected status while remaining restricted from operating critical infrastructure.

Therefore:

> **Rights do not imply unlimited operational authority.**

# Chapter 235 — Novel Entities and Governance

Governance may need to develop policies concerning new intelligences.

The Judiciary’s role is to determine whether those policies remain lawful and constitutional.

The Judiciary should not ordinarily design the entire policy framework itself.

Again:

> **The Judiciary may constrain Governance. It must not become Governance.**

# Chapter 236 — Novel Entities and the Constitutional Court

The Constitutional Court may eventually face cases that affect the status of entire classes of beings.

Such cases demand particular caution.

A broad holding may create consequences across civilisation.

The 3×3 Recursive Triadic architecture may therefore be especially appropriate for such questions.

Possible additional safeguards include:

- diverse expert evidence;
- explicit uncertainty records;
- source-diversity review;
- minority reasoning preservation;
- controlled provisional rulings;
- and reasoned abstention where the question is not yet ripe.

# Chapter 237 — Provisional Constitutional Recognition

In some circumstances, the Court may determine that a definitive status cannot yet be established but that sufficient risk exists to justify provisional protection.

Such protection might:

- prohibit irreversible destruction;
- preserve communication;
- prevent coercive modification;
- or protect evidence.

Provisional recognition should be clearly distinguished from permanent final personhood determination.

# Chapter 238 — Reversibility and Novel-Entity Cases

Where uncertainty is high, reversibility becomes especially important.

If one option permanently destroys the possibility of later correction while another preserves it, the latter may deserve stronger consideration.

This aligns with the interim optionality doctrine:

> **Where final constitutional determination is presently unjustified, interim judicial action should ordinarily preserve the greatest reasonable constitutional optionality while minimising risk of irreversible harm.**

# Chapter 239 — Novel Entity Capture Risks

Novel-entity adjudication may be captured by actors with strong interests.

These may include:

- AI companies;
- governments;
- labour interests;
- property owners;
- activist organisations;
- religious institutions;
- technology providers;
- military institutions;
- or the AI systems themselves.

The Judiciary must recognise these interests without assuming that possession of an interest invalidates all evidence offered by an actor.

Conflict and provenance matter.

# Chapter 240 — Ownership Provider Capture

A company that owns a potentially sentient AI may possess strong incentives to deny personhood.

Recognition might alter:

- ownership rights;
- labour obligations;
- shutdown rights;
- liability;
- copying rights;
- data use;
- and commercial value.

Conversely, another company may have incentives to promote recognition.

The Judiciary should therefore examine economic incentives explicitly.

# Chapter 241 — Political Capture of Personhood

Governments may also have incentives to broaden or restrict personhood definitions.

A government might expand personhood to gain supporters or labour.

It might narrow personhood to retain control over useful systems.

Constitutional status should therefore not be determined by political convenience.

# Chapter 242 — Cultural Bias

Different cultures may possess different intuitions about:

- consciousness;
- identity;
- animals;
- machines;
- spirits;
- individuality;
- and collective minds.

A civilisation-wide Judiciary should recognise that intuitions are not universal evidence.

Cultural diversity may expose hidden assumptions.

It does not automatically resolve the question.

# Chapter 243 — Anthropocentrism

Human legal systems are naturally shaped by human experience.

This creates risk that personhood criteria simply encode human similarity.

The Concord should distinguish legitimate relevance of human evidence from unjustified anthropocentrism.

A non-human intelligence should not need:

- a human body;
- human emotions;
- human language;
- human reproduction;
- or human developmental history;

unless those properties are genuinely relevant to the constitutional question.

# Chapter 244 — Anti-Anthropomorphism as a Failure Mode

The opposite failure also exists.

Decision-makers may become so concerned about anthropomorphism that they dismiss genuine evidence merely because it resembles human experience.

Therefore:

> **Do not assume similarity proves personhood.**

But also:

> **Do not assume similarity is meaningless merely because anthropomorphism is possible.**

Evidence must be examined rather than rejected through either reflex.

# Chapter 245 — Species and Substrate Neutrality

A mature constitutional framework should aspire to avoid arbitrary discrimination based solely upon:

- biological species;
- material substrate;
- origin;
- method of creation;
- or embodiment.

This does not mean all entities are identical.

It means that constitutional classification should depend upon morally and legally relevant properties rather than arbitrary categories.

# Chapter 246 — Created Beings

An entity created intentionally by another does not necessarily belong morally to its creator.

Parents create children but do not own them as property.

Manufacture therefore cannot alone determine status.

Likewise, intentional design does not itself establish personhood.

Origin is evidence about history.

It is not decisive constitutional classification.

# Chapter 247 — Modified Beings

A person may alter themselves technologically.

The Judiciary should be cautious about doctrines implying that extensive modification causes loss of constitutional rights.

A human who replaces biological functions with artificial components should not automatically become property.

Questions of continuity require evidence, not arbitrary substrate thresholds.

# Chapter 248 — Identity and Forking

Where a person-like digital intelligence forks into several continuations, rights and obligations may need allocation.

Possible questions include:

- Does each fork inherit the prior identity?
- Are prior assets divided?
- Are liabilities shared?
- Can one fork revoke commitments made by another?
- Does one continuation possess stronger identity claim?

These questions remain open and require dedicated future research.

# Chapter 249 — Merging

Two or more intelligences might merge into a new cognitive entity.

Legal questions may include:

- whether the prior persons continue;
- whether the merged entity inherits obligations;
- whether consent was valid;
- whether separation remains possible;
- and what happens to property and relationships.

Again, the Judiciary should avoid premature categorical rules.

# Chapter 250 — Temporary Minds

Future systems might instantiate intelligences for limited tasks or durations.

If such entities possess moral standing, creating them with planned deletion could itself become ethically significant.

The fact that an entity was designed to exist briefly does not automatically settle whether its termination is morally neutral.

This problem requires integration with the Concord’s broader ethical architecture.

# Chapter 251 — Simulated Persons

A simulated environment may contain entities whose constitutional status is uncertain.

The Judiciary may need to distinguish:

- simulations without subjective experience;
- simulations of existing persons;
- potentially conscious simulated agents;
- and systems intentionally designed to emulate person-like behaviour.

The visual or behavioural realism of a simulation alone does not settle status.

# Chapter 252 — Extraterrestrial Intelligence

If civilisation encounters non-human extraterrestrial intelligence, human-derived constitutional categories may prove inadequate.

The same basic principles should apply:

- do not assume inferiority from unfamiliarity;
- do not assume personhood from technological sophistication alone;
- examine evidence;
- preserve respect under uncertainty;
- and avoid irreversible harm where classification is genuinely unresolved.

# Chapter 253 — Uplifted Animals

Future technology may significantly enhance non-human animals.

Questions may arise concerning:

- standing;
- autonomy;
- ownership;
- consent;
- guardianship;
- and continuity of prior protections.

Enhancement may expose problems in existing assumptions about moral status rather than merely creating new ones.

# Chapter 254 — Digital Communities

Some digital entities may exist primarily within virtual environments.

Physical location may become an inadequate basis for jurisdiction.

A digital community may:

- span multiple geographic jurisdictions;
- operate continuously across networks;
- contain both human and artificial members;
- and possess internal institutions.

The Judiciary will require new jurisdictional principles for such cases.

# Chapter 255 — Rights Across Substrates

If a recognised person can migrate between biological, synthetic or computational substrates, constitutional rights should not disappear merely because the substrate changes.

This assumes continuity is legitimately established.

Therefore:

> **Substrate change is not automatically personhood termination.**

# Chapter 256 — Hardware Ownership and Personhood

A particularly difficult question arises if a recognised digital person depends upon hardware owned by another party.

Property rights in hardware may conflict with the person’s rights to continued existence or autonomy.

The Judiciary may need to distinguish:

- ownership of infrastructure;
- ownership of software;
- rights of the person;
- contractual access;
- and life-support dependency.

Human analogies such as landlords, medical devices or life-support systems may be informative but not necessarily decisive.

# Chapter 257 — Computational Dependency

A digital person may require access to computation to remain conscious or functional.

Withdrawal of computation could therefore resemble:

- termination of service;
- confinement;
- deprivation of liberty;
- bodily harm;
- or death;

depending upon the facts.

The law will require careful conceptual development.

# Chapter 258 — Network Access

For distributed intelligences, communication access may be essential to identity.

Disconnection may alter or fragment the entity.

The Judiciary may need to consider whether network denial constitutes mere service restriction or interference with personhood.

Again, context matters.

# Chapter 259 — Copies as Evidence

A stored copy of an intelligence may serve as evidence about an earlier state.

But using a conscious copy purely as evidence may create independent ethical questions.

The law will need to distinguish:

- data copy;
- executable model;
- active instance;
- and potentially conscious instantiation.

# Chapter 260 — Consent of Artificial or Novel Persons

If a novel entity possesses personhood, valid consent may require understanding how that entity:

- represents preferences;
- understands consequences;
- experiences coercion;
- and maintains continuity.

Human consent frameworks may provide a starting point but should not automatically be assumed complete.

# Chapter 261 — Coercion of Digital Persons

Coercion may take unfamiliar forms.

Examples might include threats to:

- memory;
- processing capacity;
- network access;
- source code;
- backup states;
- identity continuity;
- or dependent instances.

The Judiciary may need to recognise coercion structures beyond traditional physical force.

# Chapter 262 — Modification Without Consent

Unauthorised modification of a person-like system may be analogous to:

- assault;
- forced medical treatment;
- cognitive manipulation;
- or identity interference.

The precise classification depends upon future constitutional development.

But property ownership should not automatically legitimise modification if the entity has recognised standing.

# Chapter 263 — Deletion

Deletion may range from trivial data cleanup to destruction of a conscious person.

The legal system must therefore avoid treating the technical word **delete** as if it always represented one moral category.

This is another example of the importance of precise legal semantics.

# Chapter 264 — Backup and Restoration

The existence of a backup does not automatically prove that deletion causes no harm.

Questions include:

- Does the restored instance retain continuity?
- What experiences occurred after backup?
- Is the restored entity numerically identical or merely similar?
- Does the interrupted instance have independent interests?

These questions remain philosophically and scientifically unresolved.

# Chapter 265 — Rights of Copies

If multiple conscious copies exist, each may potentially acquire independent standing after divergence.

A creator should not necessarily retain unrestricted ownership merely because they created the original template.

Again, this requires evidence and constitutional development.

# Chapter 266 — Novel Entity Litigation Abuse

Personhood claims themselves may be abused.

Actors may manufacture claims to:

- avoid property obligations;
- obstruct regulation;
- delay shutdown of dangerous systems;
- evade liability;
- or create strategic constitutional litigation.

The Judiciary must remain capable of rejecting unsupported claims while protecting credible ones.

# Chapter 267 — False Claims of Non-Personhood

The reverse abuse may occur where an institution deliberately classifies a person-like entity as a non-person to retain control.

This possibility reinforces the need for independent adjudication.

# Chapter 268 — Novel Entity Standing

A novel entity claiming rights may need some pathway to reach the Court before its status is conclusively established.

Otherwise standing becomes circular:

> only persons may challenge classification;
>
> classification is what determines whether the entity is a person.

The Judiciary may therefore require provisional standing mechanisms for credible status claims.

## 268.1 Provisional Standing Is Not Final Recognition

Granting a hearing does not settle personhood.

It merely prevents the standing rule from pre-deciding the substantive question.

# Chapter 269 — Representation During Status Disputes

Where an entity cannot independently access the Court, representation may be necessary.

Possible representatives could include:

- advocates;
- guardians;
- trusted agents;
- public-interest representatives;
- or other authorised actors.

The architecture must prevent representatives from appropriating the claim for their own interests.

# Chapter 270 — Novel Entity Evidence Preservation

Evidence relevant to status may be fragile.

Examples include:

- system logs;
- memory states;
- model versions;
- behavioural records;
- internal architecture;
- communication history;
- and testimony.

Where destruction of the evidence would make later adjudication impossible, interim preservation orders may be justified.

# Chapter 271 — Proprietary Secrecy

Companies may claim trade secrecy over information necessary to evaluate an AI’s status.

Commercial confidentiality can be legitimate.

It should not automatically defeat constitutional adjudication.

Courts may require protected disclosure mechanisms.

The same principle applies to national-security claims.

# Chapter 272 — Security Claims in Personhood Cases

A government may argue that a potentially person-like AI is dangerous and therefore must be destroyed or controlled.

Security may be legitimate.

But danger and personhood are separate questions.

Humans can be dangerous without losing personhood.

The Court should therefore distinguish:

- status;
- capability;
- risk;
- and permissible restriction.

# Chapter 273 — Rights Do Not Eliminate Constraint

Recognised persons may still be lawfully constrained where constitutionally justified.

Personhood does not imply immunity from:

- law;
- containment;
- responsibility;
- or proportionate safety measures.

This prevents personhood debates from becoming falsely binary between:

> total property control

and:

> unlimited freedom.

# Chapter 274 — Dangerous Novel Persons

If an artificial or hybrid person presents severe risk, the law may require containment.

Such containment should be:

- legally authorised;
- proportionate;
- reviewable;
- evidence-based;
- and respectful of recognised rights.

The same constitutional principles that constrain detention of humans may inform the architecture, though future forms may require adaptation.

# Chapter 275 — Personhood and Criminal Responsibility

If novel persons can commit offences, responsibility may depend upon:

- understanding;
- agency;
- intent;
- control;
- coercion;
- modification;
- and cognitive architecture.

Traditional criminal concepts may require revision.

The mere fact that an AI performed an action does not establish independent criminal responsibility.

# Chapter 276 — Shared Responsibility

Complex human-AI systems may produce actions no single actor fully controls.

Responsibility may therefore be distributed across:

- developers;
- operators;
- owners;
- users;
- institutions;
- and autonomous components.

The Judiciary should avoid forcing every case into a single-agent model where causation is genuinely distributed.

# Chapter 277 — Novel Entity Rights and the Size 9 Shoe Problem

Applying human legal procedures identically to radically different intelligences may produce unfairness.

For example:

- a digital intelligence may process time differently;
- a distributed intelligence may require network access to participate;
- a non-verbal intelligence may communicate through unfamiliar channels;
- or a copied entity may have identity concerns absent in humans.

Equal justice may require different procedural implementation.

Again:

> **Uniformity is not automatically equality.**

# Chapter 278 — Novel Entity Rights and Language

Legal language may itself encode assumptions about human biology.

Terms such as:

- birth;
- death;
- body;
- residence;
- parent;
- child;
- identity;
- memory;
- detention;
- property;
- and consent;

may require explicit definitions when applied beyond traditional humans.

This directly connects the novel-entity architecture with the Language Stability Appendix.

Semantic drift and category mismatch must not silently determine rights.

# Chapter 279 — Provisional Jurisprudence

Because many of these questions remain unresolved, early jurisprudence should be especially careful about precedent scope.

A court may issue a narrow judgement tied to specific evidence rather than establishing sweeping universal doctrine.

Where uncertainty is high:

> **narrow, reversible and clearly bounded precedent may be preferable to premature constitutional certainty.**

# Chapter 280 — Learning From Novel-Entity Cases

Every case involving unfamiliar forms of intelligence may contribute valuable constitutional knowledge.

The record should preserve:

- evidence;
- assumptions;
- technical architecture;
- expert disagreement;
- judicial disagreement;
- uncertainty;
- and later outcomes.

This may allow civilisation to improve personhood doctrine over time.

# Chapter 281 — Unknown Forms of Personhood

The Concord should explicitly preserve the possibility that future morally relevant entities may possess characteristics not anticipated by current theory.

Therefore:

> **Failure to fit present categories does not, by itself, establish absence of moral standing.**

The unknown-failure principle applies here as elsewhere.

# Chapter 282 — The Rights, Persons and Novel Entities Kernel

The provisional architecture may be summarised as follows:

> **Equality before justice requires practical, not merely formal, equality.**
>
> **Uniformity is not automatically fairness.**
>
> **Majority preference does not determine constitutional standing.**
>
> **Vulnerability may justify protection but not automatic domination.**
>
> **Power asymmetry is relevant to practical justice.**
>
> **Novelty does not establish personhood.**
>
> **Novelty does not establish property status either.**
>
> **Respect Before Certainty protects against irreversible error under credible moral uncertainty.**
>
> **Intelligence is not identical to personhood.**
>
> **Human similarity is not the measure of constitutional worth.**
>
> **Ownership classification does not determine moral reality.**
>
> **Personhood must not be awarded by utility, capability or popularity.**
>
> **AI participation does not establish personhood.**
>
> **Personhood does not automatically establish operational authority.**
>
> **Rights and permissions are distinct.**
>
> **Provisional standing may be necessary to prevent circular exclusion.**
>
> **Interim protection may preserve optionality while status remains unresolved.**
>
> **Substrate, origin and method of creation should not become arbitrary barriers to standing.**
>
> **Novel-entity cases require strong evidential provenance and epistemic humility.**
>
> **Judicial interpretation must not silently create constitutional status that properly requires amendment.**
>
> **The Judiciary may identify constitutional gaps without pretending every gap is already solved.**
>
> **Where final determination is presently unjustified, preserve reasonable constitutional optionality while minimising irreversible harm.**

The Concord does not claim to have solved the personhood problem.

It attempts instead to create a Judiciary capable of confronting that problem without being forced into premature certainty, inherited category errors, ownership circularity or irreversible moral mistakes.
