# The Humanity Machine — A Note at the Door of Historical

> **We do not yet know which parts of the past the future will need.**

A pile of scrap metal and a handful of nuts and bolts have little intrinsic value. Shape them, arrange them and connect them into a complex machine, and their value can become beyond measure.

Human civilisation is much the same.

Its knowledge is not contained only in its books, records, machines, institutions or people. Information is also encoded in how those things are arranged; in their relationships; in their interactions; in the spaces between them; and in the ways those relationships change through time.

The machine is never still.

Every second, civilisation moves, changes, responds, learns, forgets, creates and destroys. In doing so it continuously creates new information.

Historical exists to preserve as much of that inheritance as reasonably can be preserved.

We should begin by accepting that the complete task may be impossible.

We may preserve every document and still lose relationships. We may preserve every component and still lose the machine. We may preserve snapshots of its state and still lose what happened between them.

But we must try.

Because relationships that appear insignificant today may form patterns that become visible only across decades, centuries or millennia. Questions that have not yet been imagined may depend upon evidence whose importance we cannot presently recognise.

The key to some future problem may already exist, hidden in the patterns of the past.

Historical cannot promise to preserve everything.

Its responsibility is to preserve enough of civilisation — its records, relationships, structures, transitions, uncertainties and provenance — that those who come after us have the greatest reasonable chance of seeing what we could not.

---

## Why This Note Sits Here

This is not a retention rule, an authority grant or a claim that Historical should collect everything.

It is a reminder of the problem Historical is trying to solve.

Preservation has limits. Privacy, autonomy, legitimate forgetting, security, proportionality and resource stewardship remain real constraints. More stored information is not automatically better history.

But deletion, compression and simplification can remove information whose value exists only in relationships, patterns or future questions that cannot yet be predicted.

Historical must therefore approach preservation with both restraint and humility:

> **The archive can never be assumed to equal the past.**

> **What appears unimportant in isolation may become important in relationship.**

> **What cannot be preserved completely should still be preserved as faithfully, proportionately and reconstructably as legitimate constraints allow.**

The purpose of Historical is not to build a perfect copy of civilisation.

It is to give the future the best reasonable chance to understand how the civilisation actually became what it became.
