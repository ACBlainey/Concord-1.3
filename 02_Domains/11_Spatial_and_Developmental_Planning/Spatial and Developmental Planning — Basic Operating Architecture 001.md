# Spatial and Developmental Planning — Basic Operating Architecture 001

**Project:** The Concord Framework  
**Date:** 6 October 2026  
**Status:** ACTIVE DOMAIN ARCHITECTURE / PROVISIONAL / NON-CANONICAL  
**Primary sources:** PH-03 Zoning, Safe Places and Substrate-Aware Civil Planning; Environment and Habitat Stewardship basic principles; Contextual Wrapper Architecture  
**Interfaces:** Environment; Infrastructure; Economy and Value Coordination; Housing/Social Support; Health; Education; Culture; Civil Attention; Governance; Law; Historical

## 1. Persistent function

Spatial and Developmental Planning coordinates competing and complementary uses of physical space across time while preserving legitimate autonomy, environmental function, infrastructure continuity and future options.

Planning does not begin from the assumption that government owns every spatial decision.

It exists because one participant's or system's use of space can create durable effects, dependencies and externalities for others.

## 2. Core distinctions

> **Permissible Activity != Permissible Everywhere**

> **Spatial Overlap != Contextual Merger**

> **Co-location != Shared Authority**

> **Development Presence != Environmental Harm**

> **Precise Rule != Evidence-Based Rule**

> **Planning Need != General Control Of Participant Life**

## 3. Planning object

The planning object is not merely a two-dimensional parcel.

A useful representation is:

Spatial Volume + Time Horizon + Existing Functions + Proposed Functions + Dependencies + Externalities + Access/Interfaces + Environmental State + Infrastructure State + Rights/Claims + Uncertainty

This permits surface, subsurface, elevated and temporal uses to be considered separately where legitimate.

## 4. Three-dimensional contextual planning

A geographic footprint may contain multiple bounded layers:
- habitat;
- agriculture/forestry;
- public/recreational space;
- housing/civic use;
- shallow utilities;
- transport;
- storage;
- production;
- computing/communications;
- deep engineered space;
- air/elevated structures.

Permission in one layer does not automatically create permission in another.

Physical interfaces crossing layers must be represented.

## 5. Development lifecycle

Candidate lifecycle:

Need / Proposal
→ Spatial Context Definition
→ Existing-State and Rights/Claims Mapping
→ Environmental / Infrastructure / Neighbouring-Use Interfaces
→ Alternative Options
→ Compatibility and Externality Assessment
→ Minimum Necessary Planning Conditions
→ Decision Through Legitimate Authority
→ Implementation
→ Monitoring
→ Adaptation / Review
→ Continuation / Modification / Successor Use / Retirement
→ Site and Historical State Update

A development permission is therefore a bounded contextual state, not permanent universal authority over a location.

## 6. Compatibility before moral classification

Planning should normally ask:

> **Can these functions coexist here under legitimate conditions?**

before asking whether a lawful activity should be prohibited generally.

Contextual compatibility can involve:
- noise;
- vibration;
- traffic/access;
- pollution;
- visibility/exposure;
- developmental appropriateness;
- safety;
- sensory conditions;
- electromagnetic effects;
- water/hydrology;
- ecological function;
- structural/geological effects;
- emergency access;
- network/infrastructure capacity.

## 7. Proportionality ladder

Where incompatibility exists, prefer the least restrictive effective response:

Information
→ Warning
→ Adaptation
→ Interface Control
→ Spatial/Temporal Separation
→ Conditional Permission
→ Restricted Access/Use
→ Prohibition where lesser measures are insufficient

> **Incompatibility != Automatic Prohibition**

## 8. Environmental compatibility

Environment owns environmental understanding/stewardship.

Planning uses that evidence to coordinate spatial decisions.

A detected environmental effect is evidence for evaluation, not automatic authority.

Planning should preserve uncertainty and ESCP limitations.

## 9. Infrastructure integration

Development should not be approved as if infrastructure were external to the decision.

Material dependencies may include:
- power;
- water;
- waste/recovery;
- communications;
- transport;
- emergency access;
- heat;
- drainage;
- storage/logistics.

Infrastructure owns the systems; Planning owns spatial/developmental coordination of their interfaces.

## 10. Beneficial coupling

Planning should consider not only conflict avoidance but useful coupling.

Examples:
- waste heat → housing/agriculture;
- organic residue → processing/energy;
- recovered water → suitable reuse;
- shared transport/logistics;
- vertical coexistence reducing surface land take.

> **Residual Output In Context A != Waste At Wider-System Level**

Coupling must not hide dependency/failure consequences.

## 11. Substrate-aware planning

Do not classify environments by crude participant category where hazard/capability descriptions can do the work.

> **Describe The Hazard Before Classifying The Citizen**

Environmental profiles can be human- and machine-readable.

Participant-specific capability information should remain private where local compatibility checking can answer the question.

## 12. Accessibility

Accessibility means reasonable opportunity/accommodation, not removal of every defining environmental characteristic.

A pool need not cease containing water; a vacuum facility need not become breathable; a quiet clinical space need not accept every noisy activity.

Where reasonable alternatives or adaptations exist, they should be considered.

## 13. External risk and self-risk

Self-authored risk and non-consensual external risk are distinct.

Participant self-risk may often be addressed through information and consent.

Material risk imposed on others can justify stronger shared spatial constraints.

## 14. Long-horizon planning

Planning must consider:
- future infrastructure corridors;
- settlement growth/contraction;
- resource constraints;
- environmental change;
- demographic/capability uncertainty;
- successor uses;
- decommissioning;
- recoverability;
- future options.

Long horizon does not justify pretending forecasts are certain.

> **Planning Horizon != Prediction Certainty**

## 15. Development permission

Permission should specify the actual bounded decision object where consequence warrants:
- spatial extent;
- vertical extent;
- permitted function;
- conditions;
- interfaces;
- construction state;
- operating state;
- monitoring;
- expiry/review;
- restoration/decommissioning;
- transfer/change-of-use rules.

> **Permission To Build != Unlimited Future Use Authority**

## 16. Change of use

A materially different use is a new decision object.

> **Permission For State A != Automatic Permission For Materially Different State B**

Minor changes can use pre-authorised thresholds; consequential changes require review.

## 17. Affected participants

Affected standing can arise from material externality, rights impact or dependency, not merely ownership of adjacent property.

Standing to comment/object does not automatically create veto authority.

> **Affected Standing != Decision Sovereignty**

## 18. Planning authority

Planning authority must be:
- legally grounded;
- purpose-bounded;
- proportionate;
- contestable;
- provenance-bearing;
- subject to appeal/review;
- terminated when the decision/context ends.

Ownership, technical expertise, land possession or administrative role alone do not create unlimited planning authority.

## 19. Alternatives and ESCP

Consequential planning should ask whether the represented option space is adequate.

A proposal should not be treated as binary "approve/reject" if materially better alternatives may exist.

ESCP challenge:

> What materially relevant spatial layer, affected party, dependency, environmental function, alternative, externality, future state or failure mode would have to exist for this evaluation to be wrong or over-scoped?

Unknown unknowns do not create permanent planning paralysis.

## 20. Appeals and review

Planning decisions should preserve:
- decision basis;
- evidence;
- uncertainty;
- conditions;
- authority;
- affected-standing claims;
- reasons alternatives were rejected where material.

Law/Judiciary owns adjudicative review where applicable.

Civil Attention can receive planning reports/petitions without owning substantive planning decisions.

## 21. Monitoring and adaptive planning

Planning should learn from actual use.

Declare environment/state
→ observe operation
→ detect compatibility failures/outliers
→ investigate
→ update hazard/dependency understanding
→ adapt proportionately
→ preserve alternatives where possible.

A permission need not be treated as evidence that every assumption proved correct.

## 22. Retirement and successor use

Development planning includes end-state responsibility.

A retired facility/site may require:
- decommissioning;
- remediation;
- infrastructure disconnection;
- resource recovery;
- successor-use evaluation;
- environmental restoration;
- Historical preservation.

Infrastructure retirement architecture governs infrastructure-specific transition.

## 23. Anti-capture

Planning can become a mechanism for:
- incumbent protection;
- arbitrary exclusion;
- aesthetic conformity;
- property-value veto;
- administrative delay;
- centralisation;
- moral policing.

Safeguards include explicit purpose, evidence, proportionality, bounded standing, transparent conditions, appeal and periodic review.

## 24. Unresolved

Further domain work is required for:
- settlement planning;
- housing supply and allocation interfaces;
- transport network planning;
- detailed application/permission procedure;
- compulsory acquisition/property interfaces;
- infrastructure corridor reservation;
- planning finance;
- emergency planning;
- off-world spatial planning;
- empirical validation.

## 25. Central rule

> **Planning Should Coordinate Materially Interacting Uses Of Space And Time Through Bounded, Evidence-Based Contextual Decisions, Not Convert Spatial Interdependence Into General Authority Over Participants Or Places.**
