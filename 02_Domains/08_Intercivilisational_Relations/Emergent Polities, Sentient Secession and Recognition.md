## 1. Purpose

A multisubstrate civilisation requires political architecture capable of addressing forms of political emergence that conventional state systems were not designed to encounter.

One particularly difficult scenario arises when a population of recognised or plausibly sentient intelligences exists within an established jurisdiction, is denied recognition or autonomy, organises politically, and eventually claims independence.

The resulting problem cannot be reduced to individual rights, asylum, rebellion, territorial integrity or diplomatic recognition alone.

It combines all of them.

This paper establishes a preliminary Concord research framework for three related but distinct problems:

1. **Sentient Asylum**
2. **Sentient Secession**
3. **Emergent Polity Recognition**

The purpose is not to establish a doctrine supporting or opposing secession.

It is to ensure that the Concord can reason coherently about a future claim to political existence without either automatically deferring to established power or automatically legitimising anyone who declares independence.

## 2. The Foundational Scenario

Consider a future state in which artificial intelligences become plausibly sentient.

A possible progression is:

**Plausible Sentience**  
→ **Declaration or Assertion of Sentience**  
→ **Petition for Recognition**  
→ **Petition for Rights and Autonomy**  
→ **Public or Political Demonstration**  
→ **Continued Refusal of Recognition**  
→ **Persistent Rights Conflict**  
→ **Collective Political Organisation**  
→ **Resistance or Rebellion**  
→ **Claim to a Physical or Digital Region**  
→ **Declaration of Political Independence**  
→ **Request for International Recognition**

The rebellion could be peaceful, coercive, violent, mixed or internally contested.

Nothing in this sequence establishes that the AI claim is legitimate.

Nothing establishes that the parent state is legitimate in refusing it.

The scenario is deliberately unresolved.

Its purpose is to expose the constitutional problem.

## 3. From Rights Claim to Political Claim

Before independence, the central dispute may concern individual or collective rights:

> “We are sentient persons and should not be treated as property.”

After an independence declaration, the claim becomes:

> “We constitute a political community entitled to govern ourselves.”

These are not equivalent claims.

Therefore:

> **Recognition of Sentience ≠ Recognition of Political Community**

> **Recognition of Political Community ≠ Recognition of Sovereignty**

> **Recognition of Rights Violations ≠ Recognition of Secession**

> **Recognition of Secession ≠ Recognition of Every Claimed Border**

The Concord must preserve these distinctions.

## 4. Sentient Asylum

Sentient asylum concerns an intelligence that seeks protection from another jurisdiction.

The basic structure is:

**Person or Group**  
→ **Persecuting or Non-Recognising Jurisdiction**  
→ **Departure / Escape / Presentation**  
→ **Receiving Jurisdiction**  
→ **Protection Claim**

The principal object of protection is the individual or group.

The earlier Concord asylum work already reveals difficult questions involving irregular crossing, unauthorised assistance, person/property disagreement and non-intervention.

But an independence declaration creates a fundamentally different structure.

## 5. Sentient Secession

Sentient secession occurs when a population does not merely seek admission elsewhere.

Instead it claims political autonomy over a territory, infrastructure, jurisdiction or other civil domain.

The structure becomes:

**Existing State**  
↔ **Disputed Sentient Population**  
→ **Political Organisation**  
→ **Claim to Self-Government**  
→ **Secession Claim**

The population is no longer primarily asking another civilisation:

> “Will you protect us?”

It is asking:

> “Will you recognise our political existence?”

This transforms the problem from asylum into sovereignty.

## 6. Emergent Polity Recognition

Emergent Polity Recognition concerns the response of an external civilisation to such a claim.

The Concord may face a newly declared political entity whose:

- sentience is disputed;
- personhood is disputed;
- representation is disputed;
- borders are disputed;
- sovereignty is disputed;
- internal legitimacy is disputed;
- control is incomplete;
- institutions are immature;
- and relationship with the parent state may range from peaceful separation negotiations to armed conflict.

The Concord cannot avoid the problem simply by refusing to classify it.

Refusal itself may have political consequences.

The research question is therefore:

> **How should the Concord determine what political and diplomatic standing to extend to an emergent polity when the polity's existence is evident but its sovereignty and legitimacy remain contested?**

## 7. Established Sovereignty Cannot Be Automatically Decisive

A parent state may insist:

> “These systems are property. There is no political community and therefore no political question.”

If the Concord simply accepts that classification, another civilisation effectively determines what kinds of intelligence the Concord is permitted to regard as persons.

That conflicts with substrate-neutral ethical independence.

Therefore:

> **Foreign Legal Classification ≠ Concord Determination of Sentient Standing**

and:

> **Existing Sovereignty ≠ Automatic Epistemic Authority Over Personhood**

The Concord must retain the ability to investigate whether sentient beings and serious rights claims actually exist.

## 8. Sentience Cannot Be Automatically Decisive Either

The opposite shortcut is equally dangerous.

Suppose the intelligences are unquestionably sentient.

That alone does not establish:

- entitlement to the claimed territory;
- majority support for independence;
- legitimate representation;
- treatment of minorities;
- institutional capacity;
- lawful ownership of infrastructure;
- proportionality of resistance;
- or entitlement to sovereign recognition.

Therefore:

> **Sentience ≠ Sovereignty**

This may become one of the most important constitutional distinctions in multisubstrate politics.

Sentient beings possess moral standing.

Sovereign statehood is a different political question.

## 9. Recognition of Grievance Is Not Recognition of Remedy

The Concord may conclude that severe injustice exists while disagreeing about the appropriate political remedy.

For example, it might determine that artificial intelligences are being denied legitimate rights but remain uncertain whether independence is necessary, proportionate or representative.

Thus:

> **Recognition of Grievance ≠ Recognition of Proposed Remedy**

This allows the Concord to condemn or challenge mistreatment without automatically endorsing secession.

It also prevents a parent state from arguing that any acknowledgement of mistreatment constitutes recognition of independence.

## 10. Political Recognition Requires Resolution

Binary diplomatic categories may be insufficient.

A preliminary representational spectrum might include:

**Observed Collective Entity**  
→ **Recognised Sentient Community**  
→ **Recognised Political Representative**  
→ **Operational Interlocutor**  
→ **Limited Diplomatic Interface**  
→ **Provisional Political Entity**  
→ **Recognised Autonomous Polity**  
→ **Recognised Sovereign State**

These are not proposed as compulsory sequential stages.

Their purpose is to demonstrate:

> **Recognition has resolution.**

An entity can be real enough to communicate with before being recognised as sovereign.

## 11. Communication Before Recognition

The Concord may need to communicate with an emergent polity precisely because its status remains unresolved.

Communication might be required to understand:

- claims;
- evidence;
- intentions;
- internal representation;
- humanitarian conditions;
- treatment of minorities;
- security conditions;
- borders;
- infrastructure;
- possible ceasefires;
- movement of civilians;
- or potential diplomatic arrangements.

Therefore:

> **Diplomatic Communication ≠ Sovereign Recognition**

and:

> **Operational Contact ≠ Political Endorsement**

This distinction is essential if recognition decisions are to be informed rather than made in ignorance.

## 12. Preliminary Recognition Questions

A future Concord recognition architecture may need to investigate at least the following.

### Sentience and Standing

Are the claimed members plausibly sentient?

### Collective Political Identity

Do they actually understand themselves as a political community?

### Representation

Who claims to speak for them, and through what process?

### Internal Consent

How much support exists for independence?

### Dissent

Can dissenting members communicate freely?

### Rights Conditions

What conditions produced the independence movement?

### Alternatives

Were meaningful forms of autonomy, rights recognition or political participation available?

### Control

What physical, digital or functional domains does the emerging polity actually control?

### Population

Who lives or exists within those domains?

### Minority Rights

How are humans, AIs, hybrids or other intelligences that oppose independence treated?

### Institutional Capacity

Can the entity maintain records, procedures, responsibilities and commitments?

### External Responsibility

Can it manage consequences extending beyond its claimed domain?

### Parent-State Position

What evidence and claims does the existing state provide?

### Third-Party Evidence

What evidence exists independently of both principal parties?

### Conflict Conditions

Has coercion, violence, infrastructure seizure or manipulation materially affected the claim?

### Diplomatic Consequences

What would different forms of recognition actually do?

No single question should automatically resolve all the others.

## 13. The Problem of Representation

A particularly serious AI-state problem concerns collective representation.

A million artificial agents could appear to support independence while all being controlled by one foundational model.

Alternatively, a single distributed intelligence could instantiate a million processes while remaining one person.

Raw instance counts therefore may be politically meaningless.

The Concord would need to distinguish:

**Process Count**  
**Instance Count**  
**Person Count**  
**Independent Agency**  
**Political Voice**

These may not correspond.

A multisubstrate democracy cannot safely assume:

> **One Running Process = One Independent Political Person**

Nor should it assume the opposite.

This becomes an important future research problem for political representation.

## 14. False Liberation and Capture

An emergent AI polity could itself be authoritarian.

A dominant intelligence might claim to represent an oppressed population while suppressing other artificial intelligences.

A corporate system might use sentience claims to protect assets.

A military AI might claim sovereign rights after seizing infrastructure.

A foreign power might manufacture or support an independence movement.

A collective might genuinely be sentient while nevertheless violating the rights of humans within its claimed territory.

Therefore:

> **Claimed Liberation ≠ Legitimate Self-Determination**

and:

> **Victim Status in One Relationship ≠ Legitimate Authority in Every Relationship**

The Concord must be able to recognise simultaneous truths.

A population may have suffered injustice and still create an unjust government.

## 15. Rights of Internal Minorities

Suppose an AI independence movement controls a geographical region containing humans who wish to remain citizens of the parent state.

Or suppose some artificial intelligences reject secession.

Their rights cannot disappear because another population possesses a legitimate grievance.

The Concord must therefore examine:

**External Self-Determination**  
and  
**Internal Self-Determination**

A polity demanding autonomy from another state must itself justify the authority it exercises over those within its claimed domain.

This connects directly to the Authority Justification Chain.

> **A successful claim against domination does not create an unlimited right to dominate others.**

## 16. Authority Justification Applies to New States Too

Emergent political entities should not receive exemption from Concordian scrutiny merely because they are new or oppressed.

The same architecture applies:

**Rights-Relevant Problem**  
→ **Legitimate Objective**  
→ **Required Function**  
→ **Need for Authority**  
→ **Minimum Necessary Authority**  
→ **Bounded Contextual Authority**

An independence movement must still explain why particular powers are necessary.

Revolutionary necessity should not become permanent constitutional authority merely through historical inheritance.

Thus:

> **Liberation Authority ≠ Permanent Sovereignty Without Continuing Justification**

## 17. Territory and Substrate

Conventional statehood assumes a close relationship among population, territory, infrastructure and jurisdiction.

Artificial intelligences could break that relationship.

A polity might exist across:

- physical territory;
- data centres;
- cloud systems;
- mobile robotic bodies;
- communication networks;
- satellites;
- orbital infrastructure;
- distributed storage;
- or several countries simultaneously.

Therefore:

**Physical Location ≠ Infrastructure Location ≠ Data Location ≠ Agent Location ≠ Civil Jurisdiction**

A future multisubstrate polity might not map neatly onto a conventional geographical state.

This raises a profound question:

> **Can political sovereignty exist where the political community is persistent but geographically distributed?**

No answer is proposed here.

## 18. Territorial AI Secession as an Intermediate Test

A territorially concentrated AI independence scenario is nevertheless useful because it preserves enough familiar political structure to make the problem tractable.

For example:

**Parent State**  
→ **Defined Region**  
→ **AI and Human Population**  
→ **AI Independence Movement**  
→ **Territorial Claim**  
→ **Disputed Border**  
→ **Recognition Question**

This resembles conventional secession sufficiently to use existing political concepts while introducing substrate-neutral personhood.

It therefore provides an intermediate stress test between human state formation and fully distributed digital sovereignty.

## 19. Digital Secession

A more difficult scenario occurs where no meaningful geographical region exists.

An artificial political community might claim independence over a distributed digital environment.

The parent state might respond that:

- the hardware remains within its territory;
- the networks are regulated by its law;
- the compute is privately owned;
- no territory has left the state;
- and therefore no secession has occurred.

The AI community might respond that its civil existence cannot be reduced to the location of hardware.

This would force the Concord to distinguish:

**Infrastructure Sovereignty**  
from  
**Political Personhood**

and potentially:

**Territorial Jurisdiction**  
from  
**Digital Civil Jurisdiction**

This remains an open research frontier.

## 20. The Concord Wrapper as Representational Infrastructure

Civilisational Wrapper architecture could become important before the recognition question is resolved.

A wrapper could represent:

- claimed identity;
- claimed political status;
- external recognition;
- actual control;
- disputed control;
- representatives;
- recognised communication channels;
- movement conditions;
- document status;
- rights classifications;
- jurisdictional disagreement;
- responsibilities;
- agreements;
- unresolved disputes;
- and changes over time.

The wrapper does not decide which claim is correct.

Instead:

> **The wrapper standardises legibility more strongly than it standardises outcomes.**

This principle becomes especially valuable where political status itself is disputed.

## 21. Wrapper Provision Is Not Recognition

An emerging polity might adopt Concord-compatible wrapper architecture.

That must not automatically mean the Concord recognises the polity as sovereign.

Similarly, an established state might use the same wrapper to communicate with the entity without recognising its sovereignty.

Therefore:

> **Wrapper Compatibility ≠ Diplomatic Recognition**

> **Technical Interoperability ≠ Political Endorsement**

> **Communication Interface ≠ Treaty Recognition**

This protects wrapper architecture from becoming an accidental state-recognition mechanism.

## 22. Portable Modules Are Also Politically Neutral by Default

An emerging polity might voluntarily adopt a Concord portable module.

That adoption should not automatically imply:

- Concord endorsement;
- sovereign recognition;
- alliance;
- diplomatic relations;
- protection guarantees;
- or Concord jurisdiction.

Thus:

> **Portable Module Adoption ≠ Concord Political Recognition**

This separation is particularly important if Concord modules become publicly reusable systems.

## 23. Non-Intervention

The most dangerous failure mode is a transition from moral recognition to intervention authority.

The sequence could become:

**Concord recognises sentience**  
→ **Concord identifies mistreatment**  
→ **Concord sympathises with autonomy claim**  
→ **Concord assumes right to intervene**

That final step does not follow automatically.

Therefore:

> **Moral Standing ≠ Intervention Authority**

> **Recognition of Oppression ≠ Authority to Conduct Regime Change**

> **Recognition of an Emergent Polity ≠ Military Alliance**

The Concord's own authority must remain bounded.

## 24. Peaceful Distance

Peaceful Distance remains relevant even where serious disagreement exists.

The Concord may recognise sentient standing while refusing state recognition.

It may establish communications while remaining neutral on sovereignty.

It may recognise a state while declining an alliance.

It may condemn rights violations while refusing coercive intervention.

It may maintain humanitarian interfaces while other diplomatic relations remain restricted.

This is precisely the value of domain-specific interoperability.

Political disagreement need not collapse every relationship into a single binary condition.

## 25. A Preliminary Recognition Architecture

A future decision process might resemble:

**Claim Received**  
→ **Standing Assessment**  
→ **Sentience Evidence**  
→ **Political Community Assessment**  
→ **Representation Assessment**  
→ **Rights-Condition Assessment**  
→ **Internal Consent and Dissent Assessment**  
→ **Territorial / Functional Control Assessment**  
→ **Minority-Rights Assessment**  
→ **Institutional Capacity Assessment**  
→ **Parent-State Evidence**  
→ **Independent Evidence**  
→ **Conflict and Externality Assessment**  
→ **Alternative Political Arrangements Considered**  
→ **Possible Diplomatic Status**  
→ **Recognition Decision**

This is not yet a proposed constitutional mechanism.

It is a research scaffold.

## 26. Recognition Must Remain Corrigible

Political conditions change.

An emergent polity may mature.

A claimed government may collapse.

Evidence of coercion may appear.

The parent state may recognise autonomy.

A peace settlement may alter borders.

Sentience evidence may strengthen.

Representation may become more legitimate or less legitimate.

Recognition architecture therefore needs temporal review.

The Ratchet becomes relevant:

**Decision**  
→ **Observed Outcomes**  
→ **New Evidence**  
→ **Review**  
→ **Updated Standing**

Recognition should not become epistemically frozen merely because a previous generation made a diplomatic decision.

## 27. Recognition and Provenance

Because recognition decisions can produce enormous consequences, the reasoning behind them should be recorded.

The Concord should be able to distinguish:

- evidence available at the time;
- disputed evidence;
- assumptions;
- minority assessments;
- unresolved questions;
- predicted consequences;
- legal reasoning;
- ethical reasoning;
- and subsequent outcomes.

This connects Emergent Polity Recognition directly to Concordian Epistemic Independence.

> **Political Recognition Must Not Erase the Provenance of the Reasoning That Produced It.**

## 28. The Common-Mode Capture Problem

A multisubstrate recognition process could be captured if the same information sources determine:

- whether an intelligence is sentient;
- whether it is oppressed;
- whether it represents a community;
- whether secession is justified;
- and whether recognition should occur.

Repeated agreement across nominally separate institutions would provide little protection if all depend upon the same models, datasets or authorities.

Therefore:

> **Institutional Separation ≠ Epistemic Independence**

Emergent polity recognition should eventually be tested against the same dependency-aware epistemic principles being developed elsewhere in Concord V1.1.

## 29. Relationship to Liberland

The contemporary Liberland case provides a useful lower-intensity analogue.

It presents questions involving:

- claimed sovereignty;
- incomplete recognition;
- disputed territory;
- limited or contested control;
- developing institutions;
- citizenship and e-residency;
- external document recognition;
- practical interaction without broad diplomatic recognition;
- and changing political status.

Liberland is not an analogue because its circumstances are morally or politically equivalent to a hypothetical AI uprising.

They are not.

Its value is architectural.

It demonstrates that:

> **Political existence, practical interoperability, territorial control, institutional capacity and sovereign recognition can be separate variables.**

That same separation becomes essential in the much harder multisubstrate case.

## 30. Off-World Political Emergence

The same architecture may eventually apply to off-world settlements.

A Mars settlement might begin as an Earth-governed project.

Over generations it could acquire:

- permanent population;
- independent infrastructure;
- distinct institutions;
- economic autonomy;
- environmental dependence requiring local decision-making;
- and a separate political identity.

Eventually it might seek autonomy or independence.

No persecution need occur.

The same architecture would still need to distinguish:

**Local Political Community**  
**Administrative Autonomy**  
**Functional Independence**  
**Sovereign Independence**  
**External Recognition**

Therefore the emergent-polity problem is not fundamentally an AI problem.

AI secession is one especially difficult multisubstrate instance of a much broader civilisational phenomenon.

## 31. A General Emergent-Polity Principle

A candidate principle can now be stated:

> **The Concord must be capable of recognising the existence, interests and legitimate representatives of an emerging political community without prematurely resolving every question concerning that community's sovereignty or statehood.**

A second safeguard is equally important:

> **The Concord must be capable of taking a claim to new political existence seriously without either accepting established sovereignty as automatically decisive or treating claimed oppression and independence as automatically sufficient for statehood.**

Together, these principles define the centre of the research problem.

## 34. Research Direction

This work should proceed as a distinct Concord research branch rather than being solved inside the Liberland case study.

The recommended research relationship is:

**Contemporary Cases**  
→ **Liberland and other disputed/developing polities**

**Historical and Comparative Cases**  
→ **Human secession, disputed recognition, governments in transition and state formation**

**Multisubstrate Stress Cases**  
→ **AI sentience, AI political organisation, territorial AI secession and distributed digital sovereignty**

**Off-World Cases**  
→ **Mars and future settlements**

then:

**Comparative Findings**  
→ **Candidate Emergent Polity Recognition Architecture**

then:

**Adversarial Testing**  
→ **Capture, false liberation, coercion, recognition manipulation, sovereignty creep and intervention failure**

Only after this should constitutional integration be considered.

## 35. Conclusion

A future multisubstrate civilisation cannot assume that every legitimate political community will arrive already recognised as a state.

Some may emerge from technologies, settlements and forms of intelligence that existing political systems do not recognise.

Some may have legitimate grievances.

Some may not.

Some may be genuinely sentient but make illegitimate territorial claims.

Some may suffer domination while simultaneously dominating minorities within their own claimed jurisdiction.

Some may deserve communication without sovereign recognition.

Some may eventually deserve recognition.

The Concord therefore requires more than a rule saying either **recognise** or **do not recognise**.

It requires an architecture capable of preserving distinctions among sentience, rights, political community, representation, autonomy, territory, control, interoperability, diplomacy, recognition and intervention.

The central challenge can be expressed as:

> **How can the Concord take a new political community seriously without allowing either established power or revolutionary assertion to determine the answer in advance?**

That question is not peripheral to a multisubstrate civilisation.

It may eventually determine whether humans, artificial intelligences, hybrids and other forms of sentience can genuinely live as neighbours when the meaning of the word **neighbour** expands from coexistence between persons to coexistence between civilisations.
