## Abstract

Conventional organisational structures often represent authority as a single hierarchy: higher-ranked participants direct lower-ranked participants, and authority is presumed to flow downward through the organisational chain.

This representation is insufficient for the Concord.

A participant may occupy a lower position within a reporting or coordination topology while simultaneously possessing greater legitimate authority concerning a particular decision.

A machine operator may be required to follow ordinary production instructions from a manager while retaining authority to refuse an instruction that would create an unacceptable safety risk. During a fire, ordinary work instructions may cease to govern entirely while every affected participant acquires or exercises protective capabilities necessary to reach safety and, where reasonably possible, assist others.

Safety is only one example. Fundamental rights, constitutional protections, professional duties, safeguarding responsibilities and other high-order ethical constraints may similarly take priority over ordinary organisational direction.

The Concord therefore requires a multidimensional authority architecture in which reporting relationships, competence, responsibility, decision jurisdiction, protective authority, functional priority, review and escalation are represented separately rather than compressed into a single concept of rank.

This paper develops the provisional concepts of **Functional Priority** and **Protective Authority** as extensions of Lateral Functional Authority.

Its central principle is:

> **Authority follows the function relevant to the decision, not merely the participant highest on the organisational chart.**

And where legitimate functions conflict:

> **Priority attaches to the legitimate function or constraint appropriate to the context, not to the personal rank of its holder.**

---

# 1. The Single-Hierarchy Problem

A conventional organisation may be represented:

**Senior Manager**

↓

**Manager**

↓

**Supervisor**

↓

**Operator**

This is useful for showing some coordination and reporting relationships.

It becomes dangerous when interpreted as:

> **Higher Organisational Position = Greater Authority Concerning Every Decision**

That inference does not necessarily follow.

The manager may possess greater responsibility for production.

The operator may possess greater knowledge of the immediate machine state.

A safety specialist may possess greater decision competence concerning a particular hazard.

A constitutional or rights-protection function may constrain all three.

Therefore:

> **Reporting Topology ≠ Complete Authority Topology**

---

# 2. Multiple Authority Topologies

A functioning Concord organisation may contain several overlapping structures.

## 2.1 Coordination Topology

Who coordinates ordinary activity?

## 2.2 Responsibility Topology

Who carries responsibility for particular outcomes or systems?

## 2.3 Competence Topology

Who possesses relevant demonstrated competence concerning a particular question?

## 2.4 Decision-Jurisdiction Topology

Who is legitimately authorised to determine a particular class of decision?

## 2.5 Protective Authority Topology

Who may prevent, refuse, interrupt or modify action to protect safety, rights or other sufficiently important ethical constraints?

## 2.6 Functional Priority Topology

Where legitimate functions conflict, which function takes priority under the relevant circumstances?

## 2.7 Review Topology

Who reviews the legitimacy, evidence and consequences of decisions?

## 2.8 Escalation Topology

Where does a disagreement go when legitimate functions cannot resolve it locally?

These structures may overlap.

They need not coincide.

---

# 3. Rank Is Therefore an Incomplete Abstraction

A single organisational rank attempts to compress a multidimensional authority system into one vertical measurement.

But a participant may simultaneously be:

- below another participant in coordination;
- above them in technical decision competence concerning one system;
- equal in fundamental standing;
- independently protected by constitutional rights;
- responsible for identifying a particular hazard;
- authorised to stop a particular process;
- subject to review by a third participant.

There may therefore be no meaningful answer to:

> **Which participant is higher?**

The better question is:

> **Which function has legitimate jurisdiction and priority concerning this decision in this context?**

---

# 4. The Dangerous Machine Example

Consider a manager and machine operator.

Under normal conditions:

**Manager**

→ legitimate production coordination function

→ instructs operator to start machine

**Operator**

→ legitimate operational function

→ starts machine.

There is no conflict.

Now suppose the operator detects a serious unsafe condition.

The manager says:

> Start the machine.

The operator says:

> No. It is unsafe.

A conventional rank model may interpret this as:

**Managerial Authority**

versus

**Subordinate Refusal**

The Concord should analyse it differently.

---

# 5. Two Legitimate Functions Are Present

The manager possesses:

> **Production Coordination Authority**

The operator possesses:

> **Safe Operation Responsibility and Protective Authority**

The question is therefore not:

> **Who outranks whom?**

It is:

> **Which function has priority concerning the decision to operate the machine under the present conditions?**

Where the danger satisfies the relevant threshold, the answer may be:

> **Safety Stop Authority > Production Direction Authority**

for that particular decision.

This does not mean:

> **Operator > Manager**

It means:

> **The operator's activated safety function takes priority over the manager's ordinary production function concerning operation of the unsafe machine.**

---

# 6. Priority of Function Is Not Superiority of Person

This distinction is foundational.

> **Priority of Function ≠ Superiority of Participant**

The operator does not become the manager.

The manager does not lose their wider coordination responsibilities.

The operator does not gain authority over unrelated production decisions.

Instead, one function temporarily constrains another because the circumstances make that constraint legitimately controlling.

Thus:

> **Lower Position in One Functional Topology ≠ Lower Authority in Every Functional Topology.**

---

# 7. Coordination Authority Is Not Absolute Control

A participant responsible for coordinating others requires sufficient authority to perform that function.

But:

> **Coordination Authority ≠ Absolute Control**

A manager's legitimate ability to direct ordinary work does not necessarily include authority to:

- require knowingly unsafe action;
- violate fundamental rights;
- suppress legitimate emergency action;
- override every professional safety determination;
- prevent lawful protective intervention;
- convert an ordinary instruction into unlimited personal command.

General organisational authority remains bounded.

---

# 8. Protective Authority

The Concord therefore provisionally defines **Protective Authority** as:

> **A bounded capability possessed or activated by a participant to refuse, interrupt, modify, escape, warn against, or where legitimately necessary intervene in an action where doing so is reasonably necessary to protect fundamental rights, immediate safety, or another sufficiently high-order ethical constraint.**

Protective Authority may arise from different legitimate bases.

These may include:

- fundamental rights;
- duty of care;
- professional responsibility;
- immediate self-protection;
- protection of others;
- constitutional obligation;
- recognised safety responsibility;
- emergency necessity;
- role-specific safeguarding responsibility.

---

# 9. Protective Authority May Not Originate From Management

This distinction is important.

An employee's right to refuse a genuinely dangerous instruction should not necessarily be understood as:

> **Management gives the employee permission to refuse management.**

That makes the protective capability conceptually dependent upon the very authority it may need to constrain.

Instead, some protective authority may arise independently from:

- the participant's fundamental standing;
- applicable rights;
- legitimate responsibility;
- the ethical architecture;
- the relevant safety context.

Therefore:

> **Protective Authority Need Not Be Delegated by the Authority It Constrains.**

This is a powerful anti-capture property.

---

# 10. The Fire Example

Consider an ordinary workplace.

Under normal conditions:

**Managerial Instructions**

→ **Normal Work Functions**

→ **Employee Compliance**

Now a fire occurs.

The context has materially changed.

Ordinary standing instructions such as:

- remain at workstation;
- complete assigned task;
- do not leave the area;
- await ordinary managerial permission;

may cease to govern where compliance would create unacceptable danger.

The participant's immediate context becomes:

**Fire / Serious Hazard**

→ **Immediate Safety Need**

→ **Protective Authority Activates**

→ **Ordinary Conflicting Instructions Become Inapplicable or Subordinate**

→ **Participant Exits to Safety**

→ **Participant May Take Reasonable Action to Assist Others**

→ **Emergency Context Ends**

→ **Ordinary Authority Structure Resumes Where Appropriate**

---

# 11. Emergency Protective Autonomy

A candidate principle is:

> **Where an immediate and sufficiently serious threat to life, bodily safety or another fundamental protected interest makes compliance with ordinary instructions unreasonably dangerous, a participant may possess authority to disregard conflicting ordinary instructions to the minimum extent reasonably necessary to reach safety, protect affected rights, and where reasonably practicable assist others.**

This should not be interpreted as unlimited emergency authority.

It is bounded by necessity.

---

# 12. Safety Does Not Require Permission From the Hazard

A system becomes dangerously circular if:

1. authority creates a dangerous condition;
2. the affected participant recognises the danger;
3. the participant requires permission from that same authority to escape or stop the danger.

Therefore:

> **The Exercise of Protective Authority Must Not Necessarily Depend Upon Prior Approval From the Authority Being Constrained.**

Otherwise the protective mechanism fails precisely when it is most needed.

---

# 13. Authority to Say No

One of the most important protective capabilities is therefore:

> **Bounded Authority to Refuse**

Where a participant has reasonable grounds to believe an instruction would create an unacceptable danger to themselves or others, they may possess legitimate authority to refuse execution pending resolution.

This is not necessarily insubordination.

It may be the correct exercise of their function.

Thus:

> **Refusal of an Instruction ≠ Refusal of Legitimate Authority**

A participant may accept the manager's legitimate coordination role while rejecting a specific instruction that exceeds the legitimate scope of that role under the circumstances.

---

# 14. The Right to Refuse Is Not Unlimited

Protective Authority must itself remain bounded.

Otherwise:

> “I think this is unsafe”

could become a universal mechanism for avoiding legitimate obligations.

The architecture therefore requires appropriate thresholds.

Depending on consequence and urgency, relevant considerations may include:

- severity of potential harm;
- probability of harm;
- immediacy;
- available evidence;
- participant competence;
- availability of safer alternatives;
- reversibility;
- uncertainty;
- affected participants;
- applicable rights;
- feasibility of rapid review.

The exact thresholds require domain-specific development.

---

# 15. Uncertainty Should Not Automatically Defeat Protection

In many safety situations certainty is impossible before action.

An operator may not know that a machine will fail.

They may possess evidence suggesting a credible serious risk.

Therefore:

> **Absence of Certainty ≠ Absence of Legitimate Protective Authority**

The required evidential threshold should scale with:

- potential consequence;
- urgency;
- reversibility;
- cost of temporary interruption;
- cost of mistaken continuation.

---

# 16. Protective Action and Responsibility

Protective Authority does not eliminate accountability.

A participant exercising consequential protective authority should, where circumstances permit:

- articulate the reason;
- identify the hazard or protected interest;
- take proportionate action;
- notify appropriate functions;
- preserve relevant evidence;
- cooperate with review.

But:

> **Accountability for Protective Action Must Not Become a Punitive Mechanism That Makes Legitimate Protective Action Practically Impossible.**

Otherwise the formal right to stop unsafe action becomes meaningless.

---

# 17. Escalation Rather Than Rank Override

Return to the machine example.

The operator refuses to start the machine.

The manager should not necessarily respond:

> I outrank you. Start it.

Instead:

**Operator Activates Safety Stop**

↓

**Production Function Pauses**

↓

**Manager Activates Escalation / Coordination Function**

↓

**Maintenance / Engineering / Safety Function Evaluates Machine**

↓

**Required Remediation**

↓

**Relevant Competent Function Determines Safety Condition**

↓

**Machine Cleared or Remains Restricted**

↓

**Production Function Resumes When Legitimate**

The manager still performs an important function.

But that function changes from **commanding operation** to **coordinating resolution of the obstacle preventing legitimate operation**.

---

# 18. This Is Not a Failure of Management

An operator saying “no” for legitimate safety reasons should not necessarily be understood as management losing control.

It may demonstrate that the organisation is functioning correctly.

The system has detected a condition outside the ordinary operational envelope and changed authority state accordingly.

Thus:

> **Legitimate Functional Refusal Can Be Evidence of Organisational Integrity Rather Than Organisational Breakdown.**

---

# 19. Decision Competence

Safety also exposes a separate problem: competence.

The participant highest in the reporting topology may not be the participant most qualified to determine the relevant technical question.

Therefore:

> **Reporting Authority ≠ Decision Competence**

and:

> **Management Competence ≠ Universal Domain Competence**

A manager may be highly competent at:

- coordination;
- scheduling;
- resource allocation;
- personnel management;
- conflict resolution.

An operator may nevertheless possess superior knowledge of the immediate machine state.

A maintenance engineer may possess superior diagnostic competence.

A specialist safety function may possess another relevant form of expertise.

None of these differences establishes general personal superiority.

---

# 20. Functional Qualification

A legitimate function should therefore be associated with the competence reasonably required to exercise it.

> **Appointment to a Function ≠ Demonstrated Fitness for the Function**

> **Rank ≠ Qualification**

> **Prior Authority ≠ Present Competence**

> **Generic Leadership Competence ≠ Domain Competence**

> **Competence in Domain A ≠ Competence in Domain B**

> **Transferable Skill ≠ Transferable Authority**

A participant may bring valuable transferable capabilities from another domain.

Those capabilities are evidence.

They are not automatic jurisdiction.

---

# 21. Authority Must Not Outrun Competence

A candidate principle is:

> **Consequential authority should not substantially exceed the participant's demonstrated competence, relevant understanding, or reliable access to competent supporting functions necessary to exercise that authority responsibly.**

This does not mean every coordinator must be the greatest technical expert.

It means the authority architecture must account for the knowledge necessary to exercise consequential authority safely.

---

# 22. Professional Managers

This architecture provides a response to the problem of professional authority detached from domain understanding.

A professional manager may possess excellent managerial competence.

The Concord should recognise it.

But managerial competence does not automatically confer:

- engineering competence;
- medical competence;
- scientific competence;
- operational competence;
- safety competence;
- legal competence.

The appropriate response is not necessarily to prohibit professional management.

It is to prevent managerial authority from absorbing unrelated functional authority merely because management occupies a conventionally higher organisational rank.

---

# 23. Advancement Across Domains

The Concord can recognise advancement without treating rank as portable authority.

> **Achievement on One Ladder Does Not Automatically Place a Participant at the Same Height on Another Ladder.**

A participant's previous achievements may demonstrate:

- leadership;
- reliability;
- judgement;
- transferable skills.

These may justify consideration for another role.

But:

> **Demonstrated Capability Is Potentially Transferable Evidence, Not Transferable Authority.**

---

# 24. Functional Priority

Where two legitimate functions conflict, the Concord requires a priority architecture.

For example:

**Production Direction**

versus

**Immediate Safety Stop**

Under defined dangerous conditions:

> **Immediate Safety Stop > Production Direction**

But this means:

> **Safety Function Has Priority Concerning This Decision**

not:

> **Safety Participant Is Superior to Production Participant**

Thus:

> **Functional Priority ≠ Personal Hierarchy**

---

# 25. High-Order Ethical Constraints

Safety is only one category.

Similar priority relationships may exist concerning:

- fundamental sentient rights;
- bodily autonomy;
- consent;
- safeguarding;
- constitutional protections;
- emergency escape;
- prevention of serious harm;
- protected privacy;
- due process;
- other high-order ethical constraints.

A Concord authority architecture therefore requires some constraints that ordinary organisational authority cannot casually override.

---

# 26. Rights as Constraints on Authority

A participant should not need to outrank another participant for their rights to constrain that participant's authority.

This is essential.

> **Rights Protection ≠ Higher Personal Rank**

A junior employee's fundamental rights can constrain a chief executive.

A citizen's constitutional rights can constrain a head of government.

A patient's rights can constrain a senior clinician.

An AI participant's recognised fundamental rights could constrain a human administrator.

The right has priority where applicable.

The participant does not thereby become personally superior.

---

# 27. Ethical Priority Without Ethical Caste

The architecture therefore permits:

> **Higher-Order Ethical Constraint**

without creating:

> **Higher-Order Person**

This is particularly important for multisubstrate civilisation.

Rights and ethical protections must attach to legitimate standing and context rather than to organisational rank.

---

# 28. Decision Decomposition

Not every conflict can be resolved by declaring one participant the decision-maker.

Complex decisions may contain several components.

For example:

**Engineering Function**

→ determines structural properties and technical risk.

**Safety Function**

→ evaluates specified safety thresholds.

**Economic Function**

→ evaluates resource consequences.

**Rights Function**

→ identifies protected constraints.

**Political Function**

→ determines legitimate collective priorities where choice remains.

Therefore:

> **Different Components of One Decision May Belong to Different Functional Jurisdictions.**

This is **Decision Decomposition**.

---

# 29. Expertise Does Not Become Sovereignty

The existence of Functional Priority must not create technocracy.

An expert may possess strong authority concerning a technical determination without possessing authority to determine every social choice affected by that determination.

Therefore:

> **Decision Competence ≠ Universal Decision Sovereignty**

> **Technical Expertise ≠ Political Sovereignty**

> **Epistemic Weight ≠ General Authority**

Experts should not become rulers merely because legitimate decisions require expertise.

---

# 30. Managers Do Not Become Universal Experts

The inverse is equally important:

> **Coordination Responsibility ≠ Universal Decision Competence**

Managers need not pretend to know everything.

A well-designed lateral system should allow them to rely upon legitimate specialist functions without losing their own legitimate coordinating function.

This turns recognition of limited knowledge from a weakness into part of competent governance.

---

# 31. Functional Collision

Where two legitimate functions produce incompatible requirements:

**Function A**

↓

**Conflict**

↑

**Function B**

the system requires a defined response.

Possible responses include:

- explicit priority rule;
- temporary protective stop;
- specialist review;
- lateral negotiation;
- escalation;
- independent adjudication;
- decomposition of the decision;
- emergency assumption of bounded authority.

Thus:

> **Functional Conflict Should Trigger Resolution Architecture, Not Automatic Rank Supremacy.**

---

# 32. Functional Jurisdiction

Before determining priority, the system must determine whether the claimed function genuinely applies.

Therefore:

> **Claimed Functional Relevance ≠ Established Functional Jurisdiction**

Otherwise a participant could manufacture authority by defining their function broadly enough to touch everything.

---

# 33. Function Must Not Become the New Rank

The Concord must guard against replacing:

**Rank Creep**

with:

**Function Creep**

A broadly defined function such as:

- public safety;
- national security;
- organisational integrity;
- efficiency;
- welfare;
- system protection;

could potentially be invoked to justify authority over almost anything.

Therefore:

> **A Function Cannot Legitimately Be Defined So Broadly That Functional Bounding Becomes Nominal Rather Than Real.**

Functions themselves require justification and boundaries.

# 37. No Responsibility Without Capability

The architecture also requires the inverse constraint.

If a participant is assigned responsibility but denied the capabilities necessary to influence the relevant outcome, responsibility becomes incoherent.

Therefore:

> **No Authority Without Legitimate Function; No Responsibility Without Sufficient Capability.**

Responsibility and capability should remain appropriately coupled.

---

# 38. No Capability Without Accountability

The opposite must also remain true.

Protective, technical, managerial and emergency authority can all be abused.

Therefore:

> **Protective Purpose ≠ Unlimited Protective Power**

and:

> **Capability Should Carry Proportionate Accountability.**

The challenge is to provide accountability without chilling legitimate protective action.

---

# 39. Cultural Requirement

Participants must understand that saying “no” can sometimes be the correct exercise of responsibility.

Likewise, managers must understand that receiving a legitimate refusal is not automatically a challenge to their personal status.

A useful Concord cultural norm is:

> **Challenge the function where necessary without degrading the person.**

And:

> **Accept legitimate functional limits without interpreting them as loss of status.**

This is important because conventional rank culture can convert technical disagreement into personal conflict.

---

# 40. Public Explanation

A simple explanation might be:

> **Your manager may normally have authority to tell you what work needs doing. That does not mean they can legitimately order you to perform an action that creates an unacceptable danger. If you are responsible for operating a machine and identify a serious safety problem, you may have the authority to stop and say no. Your manager remains your manager, but their production authority does not automatically override your safety responsibility. The problem should then move to the function capable of resolving it, such as maintenance or safety review.**

For emergencies:

> **If there is a fire or another immediate serious danger, ordinary work instructions do not require you to remain in danger. You may take the action reasonably necessary to reach safety and, where practicable, help others reach safety too.**

# 42. Relationship to Lateral Functional Authority

Lateral Functional Authority established:

> **People are equal. Functions differ. Respect may be earned. Authority must be justified.**

Functional Priority adds:

> **Different legitimate functions may possess different authority concerning the same participant or event.**

Protective Authority adds:

> **Some participants may possess or acquire legitimate authority to constrain ordinary organisational authority where safety, rights or other sufficiently important ethical conditions require it.**

Together they produce a multidimensional authority system rather than a hierarchy of persons.

# 44. Adversarial Questions

Before adoption, the architecture should test:

- malicious or habitual false safety refusals;
- disagreement over whether a hazard threshold has been reached;
- managers suppressing legitimate refusals;
- employees using rights language to avoid legitimate obligations;
- safety specialists accumulating excessive veto power;
- professional guild capture;
- emergency panic;
- conflicting safety functions;
- disagreement between local operational knowledge and remote specialist expertise;
- AI safety systems overriding humans;
- humans overriding AI participants;
- military and emergency command;
- clinical emergencies;
- child and dependent safeguarding;
- constitutional rights during emergencies;
- deliberate sabotage disguised as protective action;
- whistleblower retaliation;
- situations where stopping activity itself creates greater danger;
- cascading infrastructure where local safety and system safety conflict.

---

# 45. Falsification

The architecture should be revised if:

- distributed protective authority makes reliable coordination impossible;
- refusal powers routinely become unaccountable vetoes;
- functional priority cannot be specified without reconstructing permanent hierarchy;
- competence systems become guild or credential capture;
- urgent action becomes paralysed by jurisdictional ambiguity;
- managers retain responsibility while lacking sufficient capability to discharge it;
- specialist functions become effectively sovereign;
- formal lateralism conceals persistent de facto personal hierarchy.

Layer 0 must test the actual authority relationships rather than merely the declared structure.

---

# 46. Conclusion

The Concord should not treat organisational authority as a single vertical hierarchy.

A participant may be lower in a reporting topology while possessing controlling authority concerning a particular safety, rights, technical or protective decision.

This does not reverse the hierarchy.

It demonstrates that the hierarchy was never a complete representation of authority.

A manager may legitimately direct production.

An operator may legitimately stop unsafe operation.

A maintenance specialist may legitimately determine technical remediation.

A safety function may legitimately determine whether specified safety conditions are satisfied.

A rights framework may constrain all of them.

None need become a superior class of participant.

The controlling relationship changes as the relevant function and context change.

Therefore:

> **Neither participant becomes superior to the other. The controlling function changes because the context changes.**

The Concord can consequently preserve decisive action, leadership, specialist competence, safety and accountability without assuming that the participant highest on an organisational chart must possess the final authority concerning every decision.

The deeper principle is:

> **Authority follows the legitimate function relevant to the decision.**

And where legitimate functions conflict:

> **Priority belongs to the function or ethical constraint legitimately controlling the decision in that context—not to the personal rank of the participant exercising it.**
