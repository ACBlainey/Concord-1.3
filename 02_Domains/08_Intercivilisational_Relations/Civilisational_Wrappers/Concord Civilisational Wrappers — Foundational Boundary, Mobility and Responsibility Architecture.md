## 1. Purpose

The Concord is intended primarily as a complete multisubstrate civilisation design.

It must, however, also be capable of interacting peacefully and intelligibly with civilisations that do not internally use Concord architecture.

This creates the need for a **Concord Civilisational Wrapper**.

A wrapper is not intended to replace the internal government, constitution, culture, economy or legal system of another civilisation. It is an interoperability architecture through which substantially different civilisations may interact while preserving their legitimate internal differences.

The basic model is:

**Civilisation A → A-Specific Wrapper → Common Intercivilisational Interface ← B-Specific Wrapper ← Civilisation B**

The wrapper therefore operates primarily at the **boundary between civilisations**.

Its central problem is not:

> How should another civilisation govern itself?

It is:

> How can different civilisations recognise, communicate with and interact with one another sufficiently safely and intelligibly without requiring them to become the same civilisation?

This paper establishes preliminary foundations for answering that question.

---

## 2. Starting Point: The Ethical Kernel

The primary starting point for Concord wrapper architecture is the **Ethical Kernel**.

The Concord's ethical foundations were deliberately developed from recurring ethical principles found across multiple major human religious, philosophical and cultural traditions and subsequently expressed in substrate-neutral form.

This provides a reasonable starting hypothesis that the Ethical Kernel may be compatible with a very wide range of existing societies.

This is not yet an empirical conclusion.

There may be societies, institutions, practices or future civilisations whose principles conflict materially with some element of the Kernel.

Wrapper research must therefore test compatibility rather than assume universal compatibility.

Nevertheless, the working starting assumption is:

> **The Ethical Kernel provides the candidate minimum ethical common ground from which Concord interoperability can begin.**

The wrapper should seek the minimum common architecture necessary for interaction rather than demanding unnecessary internal convergence.

---

## 3. Interoperability Without Forced Internal Uniformity

The wrapper follows a central Concord design principle:

> **Interoperability without forced internal uniformity.**

Two civilisations may disagree substantially about government, economics, culture, religion, AI, rights, law or social organisation while still maintaining peaceful relations.

The wrapper should therefore distinguish between:

**internal compatibility**

and

**boundary compatibility**.

A civilisation need not become internally Concordian merely to communicate, trade or cooperate with a Concord civilisation.

The wrapper should seek sufficient compatibility at the boundary for the particular interaction being undertaken.

---

## 4. Interaction-Specific Compatibility

Civilisational compatibility should not normally be reduced to a binary classification:

**Compatible / Incompatible**

Different interactions require different levels and kinds of compatibility.

Two civilisations might have extensive commercial relations while maintaining restrictions on migration.

They might collaborate scientifically while refusing military cooperation.

They might recognise each other's citizens while providing significantly different rights after entry.

They might permit communications from artificial intelligences while prohibiting autonomous artificial persons from entering their jurisdiction.

Compatibility should therefore be assessed by **interaction domain**.

A preliminary progression might include:

**Recognition → Communication → Transaction → Movement → Shared Systems → Deep Cooperation**

Progress through this sequence is neither mandatory nor necessarily permanent.

High interoperability in one domain does not imply compatibility in every other domain.

---

Wrapper architecture will eventually need to support a broad range of interactions, including:

This list is preliminary.

The immediate research undertaken in this paper concerns the more fundamental architecture beneath many of these interactions:

**boundaries, jurisdiction, mobility, warnings, autonomy, shared responsibility and civil presence.**

# PART I — PHYSICAL AND DIGITAL CIVIL GEOGRAPHY

## 6. The Problem of Digital Civil Geography

Existing human civilisations generally possess reasonably intelligible geographical boundaries.

Their digital environments do not.

Modern digital infrastructure developed through globally interconnected telecommunications systems, private corporations, multinational cloud infrastructure, undersea cables, satellites, distributed services and cross-border data systems.

Consequently:

**Physical jurisdiction is geographically structured.**

**Digital infrastructure is geographically diffuse.**

This distinction becomes much more important once artificial or other digital intelligences can themselves possess civil standing.

For humans, ambiguity in digital jurisdiction can create regulatory and commercial problems.

For a digital citizen, ambiguity over digital jurisdiction may determine:

- which civilisation they are currently within;
- which laws apply;
- which rights are recognised;
- which authority may act upon them;
- whether they have legally travelled;
- whether they are resident or merely communicating remotely;
- and what protections apply if they are harmed.

A multisubstrate civilisation therefore requires a more explicit concept of **digital civil geography**.

---

## 7. Physical–Digital Jurisdictional Equivalence

The Concord should seek equivalent civil concepts for physical and digital civilisation wherever doing so preserves clarity, rights and jurisdiction.

A provisional principle is:

> **Physical and digital civil geography should be jurisdictionally equivalent wherever practicable, even where their underlying physical infrastructure is not geographically identical.**

This does not mean that network infrastructure must perfectly reproduce political geography.

An undersea cable may begin in one country's routing infrastructure, cross territorial waters, continue across the international seabed, enter another country's territorial waters and terminate in another routing system.

Its physical path does not constitute a neat digital equivalent of the political map.

The civil architecture can nevertheless preserve intelligible jurisdictional transitions.

---

## 8. Three Geographies

Wrapper architecture should distinguish at least three related forms of geography:

**Physical geography**  
Actual physical territory and territorial boundaries.

**Infrastructure geography**  
Cables, routers, satellites, data centres, compute nodes and other physical systems through which digital activity occurs.

**Civil digital geography**  
The jurisdictional spaces within which digital persons and legally meaningful digital activities exist.

These geographies interact but should not be collapsed into one another.

Therefore:

**Physical Location ≠ Infrastructure Location ≠ Data Location ≠ Agent Location ≠ Civil Jurisdiction**

The relationships should be intelligible and traceable where civilly significant.

They need not always be identical.

---

## 9. Borders Are Not Walls

A border should not be understood primarily as an obstruction.

It is fundamentally a **change of jurisdiction**.

This applies equally to physical and digital borders.

Therefore:

> **Digital Border ≠ Digital Firewall**

A firewall may be one mechanism used at a digital border.

The border itself identifies a transition between civil jurisdictions.

Digital borders may consequently be open, controlled, conditional, restricted or associated with departure into spaces not governed by either civilisation.

---

## 10. Digital Ports

The physical port provides a useful conceptual analogue for digital civilisation.

A simplified digital crossing might be represented as:

**Digital Jurisdiction A → Digital Port A → Transit Environment → Digital Port B → Digital Jurisdiction B**

The underlying data may physically travel through many pieces of infrastructure.

The civil transition is nevertheless made intelligible through recognised jurisdictional gateways.

Different digital ports may implement different border policies.

An open border between closely cooperating civilisations might permit recognised digital citizens to cross with minimal friction.

A controlled border might require identity verification and confirmation of entry rights.

A restricted border might permit communications while prohibiting autonomous digital-person entry.

A gateway into non-controlled space might simply establish that the citizen is leaving the jurisdiction and that civil protections or enforcement capabilities may change.

---

## 11. Civil Movement Is Not Data Movement

A digital person's information crossing a network boundary does not necessarily mean that the person has travelled.

The wrapper must eventually distinguish at least:

**communication across a border;**

**data transfer across a border;**

**remote action across a border;**

**service provision across a border;**

**temporary digital presence;**

**digital residency;**

**digital migration;**

**distributed civil presence;**

**replication or forking across a border.**

These cannot safely be treated as equivalent merely because all involve data transmission.

A human making an international telephone call has not physically entered the foreign state.

Similarly, a digital citizen communicating with foreign infrastructure need not necessarily have entered the foreign digital jurisdiction.

This requires the eventual development of a formal concept of **digital civil presence**.

# PART II — MULTISUBSTRATE MOVEMENT

## 12. The Movement Asymmetry Problem

A multisubstrate civilisation must avoid creating accidental civil inequalities merely because different substrates move differently.

Consider:

**Human Citizen → crosses physical border → jurisdictional transition recognised**

while:

**Digital Citizen → crosses digital environment → jurisdictional transition invisible**

The second individual could enter another jurisdiction without either the individual or the civil systems having a clear record that a jurisdictional transition occurred.

This would create a fundamental asymmetry.

At minimum, a recognised digital individual should not be able to pass unknowingly between civil jurisdictions merely because contemporary network architecture was not designed for digital citizenship.

---

## 13. Substrate-Neutral Mobility

A provisional Concord principle is:

> **Equivalent classes of recognised persons should possess equivalent civil standing regarding movement and presence regardless of substrate, while the mechanisms governing safe exercise of those rights may legitimately differ according to the characteristics and capabilities of the substrate.**

This means:

**Equal Civil Standing ≠ Identical Movement Mechanism**

A human may undergo physical security screening.

A digital citizen may require integrity, capability or security verification appropriate to digital existence.

Neither mechanism should automatically imply lesser personhood.

---

## 14. Jurisdictional Zones

Civil geography is not necessarily divided only by national or civilisational borders.

It may contain:

- special economic zones;
- research zones;
- free-movement zones;
- restricted infrastructure zones;
- protected environments;
- experimental legal zones;
- shared jurisdictions;
- temporary jurisdictions;
- diplomatic zones;
- and other areas with variant rules.

Digital civil geography should be capable of representing these distinctions.

A digital citizen may therefore have permission to enter a research environment without acquiring general residency rights, or to operate commercially within a special economic zone while remaining subject to different rules elsewhere.

The physical and digital rules need not be identical.

Differences should, however, be intelligible and justified.

---

## 15. GTP and Jurisdictional Geography

Geodesic Triangular Partitioning may eventually provide a useful spatial reference system for such arrangements.

A possible relationship is:

**GTP Spatial Cell**  
+  
**Jurisdictional Classification**  
+  
**Physical Access Conditions**  
+  
**Digital Access Conditions**  
+  
**Permitted Activities**  
+  
**Applicable Rights and Responsibilities**

This is a research direction rather than an established wrapper mechanism.

GTP should provide spatial legibility without being confused with political ownership.

As elsewhere in the Concord:

> **Spatial Address ≠ Political Ownership**

# PART III — DIFFERENT CIVILISATIONS, DIFFERENT RIGHTS

## 16. Recognition Does Not Imply Entry

Another civilisation may legitimately maintain substantially different border policies.

For example, a society might conclude that autonomous artificial intelligence represents an unacceptable existential or social risk and prohibit autonomous AI within its jurisdiction.

A Concord civilisation may disagree strongly with that judgement.

It may recognise artificial persons as citizens possessing full moral and civil standing.

Nevertheless:

> **Recognition of a foreign civilisation does not imply that every Concord citizen acquires a right to enter it.**

Existing human civilisations already maintain different entry conditions for different classes of travellers.

A wrapper should not silently transform Concord's internal rights architecture into authority over another civilisation's border law.

---

## 17. Asymmetric Borders

This creates the possibility of asymmetric movement.

For example:

**Concord Human → Civilisation B: permitted**

**Concord Digital Citizen → Civilisation B: prohibited**

while Civilisation B's human citizens may be permitted to enter the Concord.

Such asymmetry should be made visible.

It should not automatically trigger retaliation.

Therefore:

> **Asymmetry ≠ Automatic Retaliation**

Whether an asymmetry should affect treaties, diplomatic relationships, free-movement arrangements or other civil policy is a separate governance question.

The wrapper should expose the condition.

It should not itself acquire political authority merely because it detected an asymmetry.

---

## 18. Internal Difference and Wrapper Restraint

The Concord wrapper should not generally attempt to compel another civilisation to alter internal law simply because that law differs from Concord law.

This is essential if Peaceful Distance is to be meaningful.

Different civilisations may maintain different:

- rights frameworks;
- legal protections;
- restrictions;
- cultural expectations;
- criminal laws;
- entry requirements;
- economic rules;
- AI policies;
- religious norms;
- social structures.

The fact that the Concord considers another arrangement inferior, unsafe or unjust does not by itself create Concord authority over that civilisation.

The wrapper instead makes the consequences of difference intelligible to those who interact across the boundary.

---

## 19. Limits of Simple Tolerance

This does not mean every difference is ethically irrelevant.

There is an important distinction between:

**Different Internal Law**

→ **Restricted Entry**

→ **Asymmetric Treatment**

→ **Material Rights Incompatibility**

→ **Direct Threat to Visiting Persons**

For example, a civilisation refusing AI entry presents a different problem from one permitting AI citizens to enter but then legally treating them as ownerless property that may be seized, copied, modified or destroyed.

The Concord may still lack authority to change that civilisation's internal law.

It may nevertheless have strong obligations toward its own citizens to identify and communicate the danger.

This leads directly to the warning architecture.

---

# PART IV — WARNING AND AUTONOMY

## 20. Jurisdictional Change Is Normal

Crossing into another jurisdiction inevitably changes some combination of:

- law;
- rights;
- obligations;
- recognised freedoms;
- enforcement practices;
- available protections;
- civil assistance;
- and exposure to risk.

This is true even between friendly contemporary human states.

The objective of the wrapper should therefore not be to guarantee identical conditions everywhere.

That would contradict both reality and the principle of civilisational plurality.

Instead, the wrapper should make **material changes in conditions intelligible before crossing**.

---

## 21. The Border Warning and Autonomy Principle

A foundational wrapper principle is proposed:

> **Where crossing a civilisational or materially different jurisdictional boundary changes a participant's rights, protections, freedoms, obligations or exposure to harm, the wrapper should provide proportionate and intelligible warning before entry. Except where some independently justified legal restriction applies, the warning does not itself create authority to prevent the participant from crossing.**

Therefore:

**Warning ≠ Prohibition**

**Risk Assessment ≠ Travel Authority**

**Advice ≠ Command**

**High Risk ≠ Loss of Autonomy**

---

## 22. The War-Zone Test

A war zone provides a useful limiting example.

A Concord assessment may conclude:

> Entry into this region presents a substantial risk of serious harm or death. Concord assistance may be severely limited. Travel is strongly advised against.

Nevertheless, entry may remain legally permitted.

An informed citizen may decide that the objective justifies the risk.

A journalist, humanitarian worker, researcher, resident returning home or other participant may possess reasons that the civil system cannot fully evaluate on their behalf.

The Concord therefore fulfils its protective responsibility primarily by ensuring that the individual has access to materially relevant information.

It should not convert danger alone into authority to remove autonomous choice.

---

## 23. Informed Border Autonomy

The broader principle can be expressed as:

> **Civilisational boundaries inevitably involve changes in law, rights, protections, freedoms, obligations and risk. The Concord wrapper should make material differences intelligible to affected participants, provide proportionate warnings and identify reasonable alternatives where available. Such warnings are informational and advisory rather than coercive. Unless a separate legitimate legal authority restricts movement, the participant retains autonomy to cross despite the warning.**

The operational sequence is:

**Inform → Warn → Offer Alternatives → Respect the Decision**

---

## 24. Warnings and Safe Spaces

This architecture closely resembles the existing Concord treatment of different classes of Safe Spaces.

A Safe Space does not necessarily guarantee that a particular environment is appropriate for every participant.

Instead, the civil system may establish a standard for what must be disclosed so that participants can judge suitability for themselves.

The same pattern can operate at civilisational borders.

The common architecture is:

**Environment → Defined or Observed Conditions → Standardised Disclosure → Individual Assessment → Autonomous Choice**

The Concord defines the **minimum information standard**.

The participant determines **personal suitability**.

---

## 25. Risk Determines Disclosure, Not Automatically Permission

A particularly important consequence is:

> **Risk should primarily determine the required quality and prominence of information, not automatically determine the individual's permitted choice.**

Higher credible risk may require:

- greater warning prominence;
- greater specificity;
- clearer explanation of consequences;
- stronger accessibility requirements;
- acknowledgement of uncertainty;
- explicit notice of reduced rights or assistance;
- confirmation that the participant had reasonable opportunity to receive the information.

It does not automatically justify detention or denial of exit.

---

## 26. A Warning Must Not Become Hidden Border Control

This creates an important anti-capture safeguard:

> **A warning system must never become an undeclared border-control system.**

If the Concord wishes to prevent movement for reasons such as quarantine, criminal process, immediate danger to others or restricted infrastructure, that restriction should require its own legitimate legal basis.

It should pass through the normal Concord authority architecture.

For example:

**Rights-Relevant Problem → Legitimate Objective → Required Function → Need for Authority → Minimum Necessary Authority → Bounded Contextual Authority**

A travel warning must not be used to bypass this chain.

---

## 27. Personalised Warnings

Warnings may need to differ between participants.

A destination might be relatively safe for one individual but materially dangerous for another because of differences in:

- recognised personhood;
- legal status;
- substrate;
- profession;
- intended activity;
- rights recognition;
- required infrastructure;
- health or environmental needs;
- or other relevant conditions.

The wrapper should therefore eventually support **participant-relevant compatibility assessment**.

However, this creates privacy risks.

The wrapper should not require citizens to disclose unlimited personal information merely to receive useful travel information.

Future research should investigate privacy-preserving mechanisms through which participants can determine destination compatibility without unnecessary disclosure of personal characteristics.

---

## 28. Legal Status and Advice Must Remain Separate

The wrapper should clearly distinguish:

**ENTRY LEGALITY**

from

**CONCORD RISK ADVICE**

For example:

**Entry Status:** Permitted  
**Concord Advice:** Do Not Travel

There is no contradiction.

Similarly, for a digital citizen:

**Entry Status:** Permitted  
**Personhood Recognition:** Not recognised  
**Relevant Protection:** Unavailable  
**Risk Assessment:** Extreme  
**Concord Advice:** Do Not Enter  
**Legal Concord Restriction on Departure:** None

The decision remains with the citizen.

---

## 29. Alternatives

Where practicable, the wrapper should provide alternatives rather than merely warnings.

These might include:

- alternative physical routes;
- alternative digital routes;
- transit through another jurisdiction;
- neutral or shared zones;
- remote communication;
- diplomatic interfaces;
- temporary restricted modes of operation;
- recognised research environments;
- or other lawful forms of interaction.

The wrapper's purpose is to increase the participant's meaningful options rather than simply announce danger.

# PART V — SHARED JURISDICTIONS AND RESPONSIBILITY

## 30. The Shared Infrastructure Problem

Intercivilisational systems will frequently cross or occupy more than one jurisdiction.

Examples include:

- undersea communications cables;
- energy networks;
- shared transport infrastructure;
- orbital facilities;
- research stations;
- economic zones;
- digital compute environments;
- communications systems;
- environmental resources;
- and jointly governed territories.

For multisubstrate civilisation, these arrangements create additional questions.

If a digital citizen is harmed by failure of an undersea cable connecting two civilisations, who is responsible?

The answer may depend upon:

- infrastructure ownership;
- maintenance responsibility;
- location;
- operational control;
- regulatory responsibility;
- contractual arrangements;
- causal contribution;
- and the negotiated relationship between the civilisations.

The wrapper need not determine a universal answer.

It must recognise that an answer is required.

---

## 31. Jurisdiction, Control, Responsibility and Liability

Wrapper architecture should distinguish:

**Jurisdiction** — which civil law applies?

**Control** — who possesses operational authority?

**Responsibility** — who had a relevant duty?

**Causation** — what caused the harm?

**Liability** — who must provide remedy or compensation?

**Investigation** — who determines what occurred?

These concepts are related.

They should not automatically be treated as identical.

Therefore:

> **Jurisdiction, control, responsibility and liability should be explicitly related but should not be assumed to coincide.**

---

## 32. Shared Control and Shared Responsibility

A useful starting principle is:

> **Where civil infrastructure, territory or activity is subject to shared control, the participating authorities should establish corresponding shared responsibilities and liability arrangements before relying upon that shared system.**

This does not mean:

**Shared Control = Equal Liability**

The participating civilisations may negotiate different distributions of control, responsibility and liability.

The Concord wrapper need not dictate the result.

It should require the arrangement to be sufficiently clear.

---

## 33. The No Responsibility Gap Principle

A participant should not enter a shared jurisdiction only to discover after harm that every participating authority considers somebody else responsible.

A provisional principle is therefore:

> **A shared, transitional or overlapping jurisdiction should not be structured so that participants can suffer civilly significant harm for which every participating authority can disclaim responsibility.**

This is the **No Responsibility Gap Principle**.

It applies equally to humans, digital persons and other recognised participants.

---

## 34. The No Authority Pile-Up Principle

The reverse problem must also be prevented.

Shared jurisdiction should not allow multiple authorities to accumulate overlapping power merely because several parties participate in the arrangement.

Therefore:

> **Shared responsibility must not be used to manufacture cumulative authority beyond that required for the shared function.**

This is the **No Authority Pile-Up Principle**.

Together:

**No Responsibility Gap**

and

**No Authority Pile-Up**

attempt to preserve responsibility without allowing responsibility to become a justification for uncontrolled authority.

---

## 35. Shared Responsibility Agreements

A useful provisional mechanism may be a **Shared Responsibility Agreement**.

This need not prescribe the political or legal form of the agreement.

It represents the information that should exist for a shared civil arrangement.

It might specify:

**Who controls what?**

**Who maintains what?**

**Which rules apply where?**

**Who protects participants?**

**Who investigates failure?**

**How is liability allocated?**

**How are disputes resolved?**

**What happens during emergencies?**

**How does an affected participant obtain remedy?**

**How can the arrangement be modified or terminated?**

The participating civilisations negotiate these answers.

The wrapper makes the resulting arrangement intelligible and interoperable.

---

## 36. Responsibility Continuity

The broader principle is:

> **Crossing, shared or overlapping jurisdictions should not create gaps in responsibility or unbounded accumulation of authority.**

This may be described as **Responsibility Continuity**.

A person should be capable of moving through:

**Civilisation A → Shared Space → Transit Environment → Civilisation B**

without entering a civil void in which no actor has relevant duties toward them.

This is particularly important for digital citizens whose existence may depend directly upon infrastructure during transit.

---

# PART VI — THE WRAPPER AS A CIVIL BOUNDARY SYSTEM

## 37. Jurisdictional Legibility

The preceding analysis suggests a foundational wrapper requirement:

> **A participant should be able to determine which civil jurisdiction they are entering, leaving or operating within.**

This is **Jurisdictional Legibility**.

A civilisation participating in wrapper interoperability should therefore provide a sufficiently intelligible account of:

- where its jurisdiction begins;
- where it ends;
- what internal jurisdictional variations exist;
- which recognised entry and exit points exist;
- what rules govern crossing;
- and what material consequences follow from entry.

---

## 38. Boundary Crossing as a Consent Event

The relationship to Safe Spaces suggests a deeper principle.

Where crossing a boundary materially changes the conditions under which a participant will exist or act, the transition is not merely geographical.

It is also a **consent-relevant event**.

Therefore:

> **Where reasonably practicable, a participant should have an opportunity to understand material changes in conditions before voluntarily entering a substantially different jurisdictional environment.**

The border must therefore know not only:

**Who crossed?**

but, where materially relevant:

**Did the participant have a reasonable opportunity to know what they were crossing into?**

---

## 39. Minimum Border Information Standard

The Concord may therefore legitimately define a minimum standard for warnings provided through its own wrapper architecture.

The purpose of such a standard is not to decide whether the participant should travel.

It is to ensure that the information necessary for meaningful autonomous choice is not withheld, obscured or trivialised.

A future Minimum Border Information Standard might require disclosure of materially relevant changes concerning:

- entry legality;
- civil status;
- recognised rights;
- important obligations;
- major prohibited activities;
- personhood recognition;
- available legal protection;
- known severe physical or digital risks;
- availability of Concord assistance;
- special jurisdictional zones;
- and important uncertainty.

The exact standard remains to be developed.

---

## 40. Information Before Intervention

A general wrapper principle emerging from this work is:

> **Difference should produce information before it produces intervention.**

When another civilisation adopts a different law, border policy or rights framework, the first Concordian response should generally be to:

**identify the difference → understand it → communicate it → assess its consequences → preserve participant choice**

rather than immediately convert disagreement into coercion.

This does not prohibit later governance action where a genuine rights-relevant problem or threat exists.

It preserves the distinction between knowing that a difference exists and acquiring authority to act upon it.

---

## 41. Peaceful Distance in Practice

This gives practical meaning to the Concord principle of **Peaceful Distance**.

Two civilisations may possess fundamentally different views of artificial intelligence, religion, economics, political organisation or social life.

They may not be capable of reconciling those differences internally.

The wrapper can instead seek:

**Recognise Difference → Define Boundaries → Make Restrictions Explicit → Protect Against Accidental Crossing → Provide Alternative Interaction Routes → Preserve Compatible Domains → Maintain Peaceful Distance Where Necessary**

This allows disagreement without requiring absorption, conversion or isolation.

The underlying principle remains:

> **Preserve difference, establish distance, and make the interface trustworthy.**

# PART VII — PRELIMINARY WRAPPER PRINCIPLES

## 42. Candidate Foundational Principles

The present research suggests the following preliminary principles.

### WP-01 — Ethical Common Ground

The Ethical Kernel should serve as the candidate minimum ethical foundation for Concord wrapper interoperability, subject to testing against substantially different civilisations.

### WP-02 — Interoperability Without Uniformity

Intercivilisational interaction should require only the degree of compatibility necessary for the interaction concerned rather than unnecessary internal convergence.

### WP-03 — Domain-Specific Compatibility

Compatibility should be evaluated by interaction domain rather than reduced to a single civilisational score.

### WP-04 — Jurisdictional Legibility

Participants should be able to determine which civil jurisdiction they are entering, leaving or operating within.

### WP-05 — Physical–Digital Civil Equivalence

Where physical and digital civilisation perform equivalent civil functions, equivalent civil concepts should be preferred where this preserves rights, clarity and jurisdiction.

### WP-06 — Border Is Not Barrier

A civil border represents a jurisdictional transition. It does not inherently require restriction or technological isolation.

### WP-07 — Civil Movement Is Not Data Movement

Communication, data transfer, remote action, civil presence, migration and replication should not be assumed to constitute the same form of digital border crossing.

### WP-08 — Substrate-Neutral Mobility

Equivalent recognised persons should possess equivalent civil standing regarding movement, while legitimate movement mechanisms may differ according to substrate-specific characteristics and risks.

### WP-09 — Recognition Does Not Imply Entry

Recognition of another civilisation or its citizens does not automatically create a right of entry into every jurisdiction.

### WP-10 — Asymmetry Requires Legibility

Material asymmetries in entry rights, legal protections or civil standing should be exposed clearly to affected participants.

### WP-11 — Warning Is Not Prohibition

A Concord warning should provide information and advice. It should not itself create authority to restrict movement.

### WP-12 — Risk Primarily Raises the Disclosure Requirement

Increasing credible risk should primarily increase the quality, clarity and prominence of required information rather than automatically reduce individual autonomy.

### WP-13 — Informed Border Autonomy

Participants should receive materially relevant information, proportionate warnings and reasonable alternatives where available, while retaining autonomous choice unless a separate legitimate legal restriction applies.

### WP-14 — No Hidden Border Control

Warning architecture must not become an undeclared mechanism for restricting movement.

### WP-15 — Responsibility Continuity

Shared, overlapping and transitional jurisdictions should not create gaps in responsibility toward participants.

### WP-16 — No Responsibility Gap

Participating authorities should not be able collectively to create a shared environment in which all can disclaim responsibility for foreseeable civilly significant harm.

### WP-17 — No Authority Pile-Up

Shared responsibility should not justify cumulative authority beyond that required for the shared function.

### WP-18 — Negotiated Allocation

The Concord wrapper should generally require responsibility and liability arrangements to be intelligible rather than prescribing how sovereign participants must divide them.

### WP-19 — Boundary Crossing as Consent-Relevant

Where a voluntary boundary crossing materially changes the participant's conditions, the participant should have reasonable opportunity to understand those changes beforehand.

### WP-20 — Information Before Intervention

Intercivilisational difference should ordinarily generate investigation, disclosure and participant information before it generates coercive intervention.

---

## 44. The Wrapper Does Not Govern the Foreign Civilisation

Nothing in this architecture should be interpreted as granting the Concord wrapper general authority over another civilisation.

The wrapper may:

- identify;
- translate;
- authenticate;
- compare;
- disclose;
- warn;
- route;
- record;
- facilitate;
- and support negotiated interaction.

Those functions do not automatically imply sovereignty.

The wrapper should remain an **interface**, not quietly evolve into a supranational government.

---

## 45. Relationship to the Authority Justification Chain

Whenever wrapper functions begin to exercise coercive authority, they should be subject to the same Concordian reasoning as other civil authority.

The existence of a border does not itself justify unlimited border authority.

The existence of risk does not justify unlimited protective authority.

The existence of incompatibility does not justify authority to change another civilisation.

The existence of shared responsibility does not justify unlimited shared governance.

Therefore:

**Civil Problem → Legitimate Objective → Required Wrapper Function → Need for Authority → Minimum Necessary Authority → Bounded Intercivilisational Authority**

The wrapper must not cite its own interoperability function as evidence that it deserves broader sovereignty.

# PART IX — RESEARCH FRONTIER

## 46. Major Questions Now Exposed

This preliminary architecture leaves substantial work unresolved.

Future wrapper research must determine, among other issues:

- what constitutes digital civil presence;
- how digital migration differs from communication or remote operation;
- how distributed digital persons are jurisdictionally represented;
- how copies and forks affect citizenship and border crossing;
- how privacy-preserving personalised warnings can work;
- how warning classifications are produced and challenged;
- how stale or politically manipulated warnings are prevented;
- how civilisations publish reliable jurisdictional maps;
- how special legal zones are represented;
- how shared jurisdictions allocate responsibility;
- how private infrastructure operators fit into civil responsibility;
- how liability follows infrastructure failure affecting digital persons;
- how external or ungoverned digital spaces are represented;
- how rights incompatibility affects deeper interoperability agreements;
- how wrapper restrictions avoid becoming disguised ideological coercion;
- and how wrappers themselves remain corrigible and resistant to capture.

These are research questions, not settled architecture.

---

## 47. Existing-Civilisation Wrapper Challenge

A greenfield Concord civilisation can design physical and digital jurisdiction together from the beginning.

Existing civilisations cannot.

Their digital infrastructure is already globally interconnected, commercially operated and jurisdictionally diffuse.

Therefore:

> **Greenfield Concord can co-design physical and digital civil geography. Legacy wrappers must construct jurisdictional legibility over inherited digital environments that were never designed for digital citizenship.**

This distinction should be central when the generic architecture is later tested against different existing civilisations.

The US, UK, French and Chinese wrapper studies should therefore investigate how substantially different legal and political systems expose different boundary, jurisdictional, rights and digital-interoperability problems.

They should not begin by assuming that one implementation will fit all four.

## 48. Provisional Assessment

The wrapper is beginning to appear less like a conventional diplomatic institution and more like a **civilisational boundary and interoperability architecture**.

Its purpose is not to eliminate difference.

It is to make interaction across difference sufficiently intelligible, voluntary, accountable and safe.

Its emerging grammar is:

**Recognise → Locate → Translate → Inform → Warn → Offer Alternatives → Permit Autonomous Choice → Establish Responsibility → Interact → Provide Remedy → Preserve Peaceful Distance**

The Ethical Kernel provides the candidate common ethical foundation.

Jurisdictional legibility tells participants where they are.

Physical–digital equivalence prevents digital civilisation from developing an unrelated civil logic.

Informed Border Autonomy preserves individual choice.

Responsibility Continuity prevents shared jurisdictions from becoming responsibility voids.

No Authority Pile-Up prevents shared responsibility from becoming an excuse for uncontrolled power.

Peaceful Distance allows profound disagreement without requiring either forced convergence or complete isolation.

---

## 49. Final Design Orientation

The Concord wrapper should not attempt to make every civilisation Concordian.

It should attempt to make the **boundary between different civilisations intelligible enough that neighbours can interact without pretending their differences do not exist**.

That requires the Concord to recognise a difficult but important reality:

Different civilisations will have different laws.

Different citizens will face different conditions when crossing borders.

Different substrates will require different mechanisms of movement.

Some jurisdictions will be safer than others.

Some will recognise rights that others reject.

Some interactions will be possible while others remain incompatible.

The solution should not automatically be prohibition, homogenisation or intervention.

The emerging Concordian alternative is:

> **Make the boundary clear. Make the conditions clear. Make responsibility clear. Warn the participant where necessary. Offer alternatives where possible. Preserve autonomy where legitimate. Maintain peaceful distance where differences cannot or need not be reconciled.**

This provides a tentative foundation from which the detailed Concord Civilisational Wrapper can now begin to be designed.
